# 4NF and 5NF — Multi-Valued and Join Dependencies

> Beyond BCNF, two more normal forms exist, driven by dependencies that functional dependencies cannot express: multi-valued dependencies (MVDs) for 4NF, and join dependencies (JDs) for 5NF. These rarely bite in practice, but when they do, the symptom is mysterious duplication that no amount of FD-based normalization fixes.

## What you already know

From [[03-Functional-Dependencies]]: an FD $X \to Y$ says "same X implies same Y." FDs are the engine of 1NF-BCNF.

From [[07-BCNF]]: BCNF is the strongest normal form based on FDs. Some anomalies remain — they require *other kinds* of dependencies to express.

From [[01-Relations-Tuples-Attributes]]: 1NF requires atomic attribute values. The temptation to violate 1NF by packing multiple values into one column (e.g., a comma-separated list of phone numbers) is exactly the temptation that, if resisted by adding multiple rows, can create MVDs.

## Why this layer exists

Some redundancy cannot be described by FDs. The classic case: an entity with two independent multi-valued attributes. If you put both in one table, you get a combinatorial explosion of rows, none of which is strictly necessary — but you cannot point to a specific FD as the cause.

4NF and 5NF formalize the dependencies that cause this kind of redundancy, and the decomposition that resolves it. They are rare in business schemas (most designers decompose naturally along aggregate boundaries), but they are real, and knowing them keeps you from being stuck when one appears.

## What is genuinely new

- **Multi-valued dependencies (MVDs)**: $X \to\to Y$ — "for each X, Y is a set, independent of the remaining attributes."
- **Fourth Normal Form (4NF)**: no non-trivial MVDs unless the determinant is a superkey.
- **Join dependencies (JDs)**: a relation $R$ satisfies a JD $\bowtie(R_1, \ldots, R_n)$ if $R = R_1 \bowtie \ldots \bowtie R_n$.
- **Fifth Normal Form (5NF / PJ/NF)**: every non-trivial JD is implied by candidate keys.

## Concepts

### Multi-valued dependencies (MVDs)

A **multi-valued dependency** $X \to\to Y$ holds on $R$ (with $Z = R - X - Y$) iff: whenever two tuples agree on $X$, the set of $Y$-values associated with that $X$ is independent of the $Z$-values.

Formally: if $t_1, t_2 \in R$ with $t_1[X] = t_2[X]$, then there exist $t_3, t_4 \in R$ with:

- $t_3[X] = t_4[X] = t_1[X]$
- $t_3[Y] = t_1[Y]$ and $t_3[Z] = t_2[Z]$
- $t_4[Y] = t_2[Y]$ and $t_4[Z] = t_1[Z]$

Read it as: "Y and Z are *independent* multi-valued facts about X." Knowing the Y-values for an X tells you nothing about the Z-values for that X.

Every FD is an MVD (an FD $X \to Y$ implies the MVD $X \to\to Y$), but not vice versa. MVDs generalize FDs.

### The classic MVD example

A customer has multiple phone numbers and multiple addresses. The phones and addresses are independent — knowing one phone tells you nothing about which addresses exist.

`customer_contacts(customer_id, phone, address)`:

| customer_id | phone | address |
|---|---|---|
| 701 | +44... | London |
| 701 | +44... | Manchester |
| 701 | +33... | London |
| 701 | +33... | Manchester |

Customer 701 has 2 phones and 2 addresses; the table has 4 rows (the Cartesian product of phones × addresses for that customer). This is the symptom of an MVD: `{customer_id} \to\to {phone}` and `{customer_id} \to\to {address}`.

The redundancy: adding a new phone requires adding *multiple* rows (one per existing address). Adding a new address requires multiple rows (one per existing phone). Delete one combination and you have not deleted either fact — you have deleted a *link* that did not exist.

### Fourth Normal Form (4NF)

A relation $R$ is in **4NF** iff for every non-trivial MVD $X \to\to Y$ in $R$, $X$ is a superkey of $R$.

A *trivial* MVD is one where $Y \subseteq X$ or $X \cup Y = R$ (it always holds). 4NF cares only about non-trivial MVDs.

Decomposition: if $X \to\to Y$ is a non-trivial MVD and $X$ is not a superkey, decompose $R$ into:

- $R_1 = XY$ (the determinant and the dependent)
- $R_2 = XZ$ (the determinant and the rest)

Both are lossless (the original can be reconstructed by joining on $X$).

For the customer_contacts example:

- $R_1$ = `customer_phones(customer_id, phone)`
- $R_2$ = `customer_addresses(customer_id, address)`

Now adding a phone adds one row; adding an address adds one row; and the only way to ask "what phones and addresses does customer 701 have?" is to query both tables independently — which is what we wanted.

### Join dependencies (JDs)

A **join dependency** $\bowtie(R_1, R_2, \ldots, R_n)$ holds on $R$ iff $R = R_1 \bowtie R_2 \bowtie \ldots \bowtie R_n$ (the relation is exactly the natural join of its projections).

A JD is *trivial* if one of the $R_i$ equals $R$. A JD with $n = 2$ is equivalent to an MVD (binary join = MVD). JDs generalize MVDs, just as MVDs generalize FDs.

JDs typically arise when an entity can be decomposed into three or more independent fact sets, each of which depends on the key, but where no two-way decomposition is lossless.

### Fifth Normal Form (5NF / PJ/NF)

A relation $R$ is in **5NF** (also called Project-Join Normal Form, PJ/NF) iff every non-trivial JD is implied by the candidate keys — i.e., every JD is a consequence of the keys, not an additional constraint.

Equivalently: $R$ cannot be losslessly decomposed into three or more projections in a way that is not already implied by its keys.

5NF is the final stop on the normal-form hierarchy for join dependencies. Beyond it lies DKNF (see [[09-DKNF]]), which generalizes further to all constraints, not just JDs.

### When these matter

- **4NF**: when an entity has multiple independent multi-valued attributes packed in one table. Common in customer-contact data, employee-skills-with-projects, etc. Rare in well-designed schemas because designers naturally separate them.
- **5NF**: when a ternary (or higher) relationship can be decomposed into binary ones without loss. Even rarer; usually arises in supplier-part-project-style schemas.

In practice, if you follow aggregate-boundary design (see [[02-Aggregates]]) and decompose entities into separate tables for each independent multi-valued attribute, you reach 4NF/5NF without thinking about it. The formal theory exists to *name* what you did and prove it correct.

## Banking application

### The customer-contacts MVD

A banking customer ([[00-Banking-Case-Study]]) has:

- Multiple phone numbers (mobile, home, work).
- Multiple addresses (residential, postal, business).
- Multiple email addresses.

These are independent. Putting them in one table:

```sql
CREATE TABLE customer_contacts_denormalized (
    customer_id BIGINT NOT NULL,
    phone       VARCHAR(32),
    address     VARCHAR(255),
    email       VARCHAR(255)
);
```

produces the MVD explosion: 2 phones × 2 addresses × 2 emails = 8 rows for one customer. Update anomalies everywhere.

### The 4NF decomposition

```sql
CREATE TABLE customer_phones (
    customer_id BIGINT NOT NULL,
    phone       VARCHAR(32) NOT NULL,
    phone_type  VARCHAR(16) NOT NULL,
    CONSTRAINT customer_phones_pk PRIMARY KEY (customer_id, phone)
);

CREATE TABLE customer_addresses (
    customer_id BIGINT NOT NULL,
    address     VARCHAR(255) NOT NULL,
    address_type VARCHAR(16) NOT NULL,
    CONSTRAINT customer_addresses_pk PRIMARY KEY (customer_id, address)
);

CREATE TABLE customer_emails (
    customer_id BIGINT NOT NULL,
    email       VARCHAR(255) NOT NULL,
    CONSTRAINT customer_emails_pk PRIMARY KEY (customer_id, email)
);
```

Each table holds one independent multi-valued fact. Adding a phone is one row; adding an address is one row; no combinatorial explosion.

### A 5NF example — suppliers, parts, projects

Suppose the bank procures services from vendors, for projects, using specific products. The relationship is genuinely ternary: "vendor V supplies product P to project J." But it can be decomposed into three binary projections if certain constraints hold:

- `vendor_products(vendor, product)` — "V can supply P"
- `vendor_projects(vendor, project)` — "V works on J"
- `product_projects(product, project)` — "P is used on J"

If the original ternary table `supplies(vendor, product, project)` equals the join of these three binary projections, then 5NF says: store the binaries, not the ternary. The ternary is reconstructable when needed via a three-way join.

This case is rare. In banking, ternary relationships usually cannot be decomposed this way (the relationship itself carries information — e.g., "this specific vendor supplies this specific product to this specific project at this specific price"). If the ternary carries information beyond the three binaries, 5NF does *not* require decomposition.

## Code

```sql
-- The 4NF-compliant customer-contacts schema
CREATE TABLE customers (
    id BIGSERIAL PRIMARY KEY,
    legal_name VARCHAR(255) NOT NULL,
    tax_id     VARCHAR(32)  NOT NULL UNIQUE
);

CREATE TABLE customer_phones (
    customer_id BIGINT      NOT NULL REFERENCES customers(id),
    phone       VARCHAR(32) NOT NULL,
    phone_type  VARCHAR(16) NOT NULL CHECK (phone_type IN ('MOBILE','HOME','WORK')),
    CONSTRAINT customer_phones_pk PRIMARY KEY (customer_id, phone)
);

CREATE TABLE customer_addresses (
    customer_id  BIGINT       NOT NULL REFERENCES customers(id),
    address      VARCHAR(255) NOT NULL,
    address_type VARCHAR(16)  NOT NULL CHECK (address_type IN ('RESIDENTIAL','POSTAL','BUSINESS')),
    CONSTRAINT customer_addresses_pk PRIMARY KEY (customer_id, address)
);

CREATE TABLE customer_emails (
    customer_id BIGINT       NOT NULL REFERENCES customers(id),
    email       VARCHAR(255) NOT NULL,
    CONSTRAINT customer_emails_pk PRIMARY KEY (customer_id, email)
);
```

### Querying the decomposed contacts

```sql
-- All contact info for customer 701 (reconstructing the denormalized view)
SELECT c.legal_name,
       p.phone, p.phone_type,
       a.address, a.address_type,
       e.email
FROM customers c
LEFT JOIN customer_phones    p ON p.customer_id = c.id
LEFT JOIN customer_addresses a ON a.customer_id = c.id
LEFT JOIN customer_emails   e ON e.customer_id = c.id
WHERE c.id = 701;
```

Note: this query, if customer 701 has 2 phones, 2 addresses, and 2 emails, returns 8 rows — the Cartesian product. That is the cost of the denormalized *view*. The source-of-truth tables are 4NF; the *view* you produce for display may be denormalized for convenience. See [[10-Normalization-Trade-offs]] and [[07-Views-Materialized-Views]].

PostgreSQL-specific: when querying multi-valued attributes separately (without joining them), use `GROUP BY` and `array_agg` to avoid the Cartesian explosion:

```sql
SELECT c.legal_name,
       array_agg(p.phone)         AS phones,
       array_agg(a.address)       AS addresses,
       array_agg(e.email)         AS emails
FROM customers c
LEFT JOIN customer_phones    p ON p.customer_id = c.id
LEFT JOIN customer_addresses a ON a.customer_id = c.id
LEFT JOIN customer_emails   e ON e.customer_id = c.id
WHERE c.id = 701
GROUP BY c.id, c.legal_name;
```

This avoids the Cartesian-product rows by aggregating each multi-valued set independently — a PostgreSQL idiom for staying close to the 4NF shape even in display queries.

## What can go wrong

- **Not recognizing an MVD.** The symptom is "every time I add a phone, I have to add three rows" or "this junction table is exploding in size." If you see combinatorial growth in a single table that cannot be explained by an FD, suspect an MVD.
- **Premature decomposition.** If the multi-valued attributes are *not* independent (e.g., "phone X is only used at address Y"), then there is no MVD and decomposition loses information. Verify independence before decomposing.
- **Mistaking a 1NF violation for a 4NF problem.** If the multi-valued attribute is packed into one column (comma-separated list), the problem is 1NF, not 4NF. Fix the 1NF violation first (split into rows); *then* check whether the resulting table has MVDs.
- **Assuming 5NF is necessary.** Most ternary relationships carry information beyond the three binary projections and should *not* be decomposed. Decomposing a non-decomposable ternary loses information — verify with data.
- **Forgetting that 4NF implies BCNF.** A 4NF relation is automatically in BCNF (and hence in 3NF). The hierarchy is monotonic.

## Trade-offs

- **Decomposition vs. query complexity.** Splitting multi-valued attributes into separate tables (4NF) means queries that need all contact info require multiple joins. The trade-off is write simplicity vs. read complexity.
- **Independent vs. dependent multi-valued attributes.** If two multi-valued attributes are linked (e.g., "this phone is for this address"), they are not independent — no MVD. Keeping them together may be correct. Verify with the domain, not by intuition.
- **4NF vs. denormalization.** Sometimes the denormalized single-table form is convenient for a specific read pattern (e.g., a customer-360 view). Denormalize via a view or materialized view; keep the source of truth in 4NF. See [[10-Normalization-Trade-offs]].
- **Theory vs. practice.** 4NF and 5NF violations are rare in well-designed schemas. The formal concepts matter for *recognizing* the case when it appears, not for everyday design. Most engineers will encounter one or two in a career.
