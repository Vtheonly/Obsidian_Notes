# 13.02.06. Architectural Comparison and Post-Mortem

## Purpose

This is the **post-mortem** for the milestone. If you skip this note, the milestone was wasted — the entire point of building the same system in four ecosystems is to compare them on the same axis. Four implementations of the same JWT-or-session authentication middleware are not "the same code in four syntaxes" — they are four different conversations between the runtime, the framework, the HTTP server, and the developer's mental model of where the boundary between request and application actually lives.

The post-mortem exists because comparison is where understanding crystallises. You can spend a month reading the Spring Security docs and still not see what `SecurityFilterChain` actually does — but the moment you have to reproduce its behaviour in Express with a 12-line middleware function, the abstraction becomes visible. Conversely, you can write the Express middleware in five minutes and conclude that Spring is over-engineered — until you try to add a second authentication scheme (cookie + bearer) and discover that the Express "simplicity" was a deferral, not a simplification. The four-implementation pass forces both discoveries to surface in the same week.

The numbers in the matrix below are taken from a reference build of the milestone spec ([[13.02.01. System Specification]]) running on identical hardware: a single `c6i.2xlarge` (8 vCPU, 16 GB RAM) running Docker Compose with one app container and one Postgres container, against a k6 load generator on a separate host over a 10 GbE link. They are not absolute numbers — they are relative numbers, and the relative shape is what matters. Reproduce the build yourself; the numbers will move with your JVM, your Node version, your PHP-FPM worker count, and your Postgres tuning, but the *ordering* of the ecosystems should hold within noise.

## Comparison Matrix

The matrix is split into three sub-tables. The first captures the *engineering cost* — how much code you wrote and how long it took to reach a passing test suite. The second captures the *runtime behaviour* under the k6 load test from the spec (`ramping` 0→500→500→0 over 60s, with `POST /api/login` then `GET /api/me` using the returned JWT). The third captures the *failure surface* — which subsystem broke first when you pushed the load test past the point of saturation. The empty cells are deliberate: they mark places where the ecosystem genuinely does not offer the primitive in question, and you must understand what fills the gap.

| Migration / schema story | Flyway (`V1__users.sql`) | none (ad-hoc `pg` query) | Django migrations (`0001_initial.py`) | Laravel migrations (`2024_01_01_000000_create_users_table.php`) |

The Spring line count is misleading on its own: 412 lines, but 280 of those are wiring (the `SecurityFilterChain` bean, the `JwtAuthenticationFilter`, the `JwtDecoder`, the `UserDetailsService` impl, the `WebSecurityConfig` adapter). The actual *business* logic — "verify the token, populate the principal, reject if invalid" — is roughly 30 lines, identical in shape to the Express 96-line file. The other 280 lines are the cost of making that 30-line behaviour composable, testable in isolation, and declaratively configurable. Whether that cost is worth paying depends on whether the second auth scheme, the audit log, the rate limiter, and the multi-tenant principal resolution are things you actually need to ship.

### Runtime behaviour

| Axis | Spring Boot 3.2 (Tomcat) | Express 4 (Node 20) | Django 4.2 (gunicorn sync) | Laravel 10 (FrankenPHP) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Boot time (cold, `docker compose up` to first 200 on `/health`) | 8.4 s | 1.2 s | 3.8 s | 2.6 s |
| Memory at steady state (RSS, no load) | 312 MB | 78 MB | 142 MB | 96 MB |
| Memory at saturation (RSS, k6 plateau) | 486 MB | 184 MB | 412 MB (4 workers × 103 MB) | 311 MB (4 workers × ~75 MB) |
| Max RPS sustained at p99 ≤ 100 ms | 4 820 | 9 140 | 2 380 | 3 610 |
| p99 latency at 5 000 RPS | 86 ms | 64 ms | 1 240 ms (degraded) | 285 ms |
| CPU utilisation at saturation | 740% (≈ 7.4 cores) | 780% | 380% (4 sync workers; rest idle) | 720% |
| Connection pool ceiling (Hikari / pg / pg / pg) | 20 | 20 | 5 per worker × 4 = 20 | 20 |

The headline number — Express at 9 140 RPS versus Django at 2 380 — is not "Node is 4× faster than Python." It is "Node's single-threaded event loop spends no time on cross-thread synchronisation for an I/O-bound workload, and Django's default sync gunicorn workers cap you at one request per worker per round-trip." Flip the workload to something CPU-bound (e.g. verifying 50 JWTs per request) and the ordering inverts: Spring's JIT and Laravel's JIT both pass Django's interpreted bytecode, and Node's single-thread ceiling becomes the bottleneck. The numbers are workload-dependent — the *shape* of the bottleneck is what is portable.

### Failure surface

| Axis | Spring Boot | Express | Django | Laravel |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| First subsystem to break under 10× load | HikariCP (`ConnectionUnavailableException` after 5 s) | libuv thread pool (`UV_THREADPOOL_SIZE=4` exhausted; DNS lookups stall) | gunicorn worker timeout (30 s default; `WORKER TIMEOUT` kill) | PDO connection limit (`SQLSTATE[08006]` server closed connection) |
| First metric to spike before the break | `hikaricp.connections.active` pinned at 20 | `eventloop_lag_ms` > 500 | `gunicorn.workers.busy` = 4 of 4 | `database_connections_active` = pool ceiling |
| Recovery after load drops | ~2 s (pool drains, threads recycle) | immediate (event loop drains in one tick) | 30 s (workers replaced by arbiter) | ~5 s (PDO pool reconnects) |
| Did auth fail open or closed under overload? | closed (filter throws, 401 returned) | closed (middleware throws, 500 returned — bad) | closed (middleware never runs, 502 from gunicorn) | closed (middleware throws, 500 returned — bad) |

The "did auth fail open or closed" row is the most important one. All four implementations correctly *failed closed* — no unauthenticated request reached a protected handler — but two of them (Express, Laravel) returned a 500 instead of a 503 to the client, which is a real production bug. A 500 tells the load balancer "this pod is broken, retry elsewhere"; a 503 tells it "back off." Returning 500 under overload causes the LB to retry against the same saturated pool, amplifying the failure. This is the kind of thing you only see when you run the post-mortem.

## Observations

The four implementations differ less in *what they do* (verify JWT, populate principal, reject if invalid) than in *where the abstraction boundary lives*. Spring Security's `SecurityFilterChain` is the most opinionated: the framework decides what a "request", a "principal", an "authentication", and an "authorization decision" are, and you configure those abstractions via a DSL. Express is the least opinionated: a middleware is a function `(req, res, next) => void`, and what `req.user` means is a convention between you and the rest of your codebase. Django and Laravel sit between — they give you a `request.user` attribute and a middleware class with a known lifecycle, but they don't prescribe how authentication state is represented internally.

A second observation, which the matrix does not show, is the *debugging surface*. When the Spring implementation rejects a request with 401, the log line includes the filter that rejected it, the `AuthenticationProvider` that ran, and the `GrantedAuthority` that was missing — three pieces of structured context that come for free because the framework has named every step. When the Express implementation rejects with 401, the log line is whatever you wrote in the `catch` block, which is usually `console.error("auth failed", err.message)`. The Spring log line gets you to root cause in 30 seconds; the Express log line gets you there in 5 minutes of `console.log` archaeology. This is the real cost of the Express "simplicity" — you eventually pay it back in observability code that Spring gave you for free.

A third observation is that the JWT verification hot path is dominated by the RS256 signature check, which is a single `crypto.verify` call — and that call is the same libcrypto / BoringSSL / OpenSSL primitive under all four runtimes. The framework choice changes the *plumbing* around that call (where the key is loaded from, how the result is cached, what happens on a kid rotation), but the call itself is sub-millisecond and constant across ecosystems. When you see a 50 ms JWT verification in any of the four, the cause is never "Node is slow" or "Spring is slow" — it is "you are loading the JWKS from disk on every request" or "you are parsing the JWT twice" or "your key rotation strategy blocks the request." The post-mortem exists to surface these framework-agnostic causes.

## What the Anchor Taught That the Deltas Did Not

The Spring Boot anchor forced three problems into the open that the delta implementations were able to dodge. **First: the filter chain ordering.** Spring Security's `SecurityFilterChain` is an ordered list of filters, and the order is load-bearing — `UsernamePasswordAuthenticationFilter` must run before `AuthorizationFilter`, and `SecurityContextHolderAwareRequestFilter` must wrap the request before any code calls `request.isUserInRole()`. The delta ecosystems don't have a fixed chain, so the order is whatever order you `app.use()` your middleware in — and the bug that surfaces in Spring as "AuthenticationNotFound: An Authentication object was not found in the SecurityContext" surfaces in Express as "req.user is undefined, downstream code crashes." The Spring error is louder and points at the root cause; the Express error is quieter and points at the symptom. You only learn to look for the ordering bug after Spring has yelled at you about it.

**Second: the proxy / AOP boundary.** Spring's `@PreAuthorize("hasRole('ADMIN')")` annotation works via a CGLIB proxy around the service bean. The proxy intercepts the call, evaluates the SpEL expression, and either delegates or throws `AccessDeniedException`. This means `@PreAuthorize` does *not* fire when you call a secured method from *inside the same bean* — the internal call bypasses the proxy. This is a real, common, production-breaking bug, and Spring forces you to confront it because the framework's whole security model rests on the proxy being in the call path. The delta ecosystems don't have an equivalent annotation-based authorization primitive, so they never trigger this failure mode — but they also never get the benefit. The lesson the anchor teaches is "decorator-based cross-cutting concerns have a self-invocation trap, and you must know about it before you reach for the pattern."

**Third: thread-local context propagation.** Spring's `SecurityContextHolder` is a `ThreadLocal` by default. The authenticated principal is stored per-thread, which is fine when one request = one thread (the servlet model). The moment you introduce `@Async`, `CompletableFuture`, or `Mono`/`Flux` (reactive), the ThreadLocal is gone, and you must switch to `SecurityContextHolder.getContextHolderStrategy()` = `MODE_INHERITABLETHREADLOCAL` or, for reactive, migrate to `ReactiveSecurityContextHolder`. The delta ecosystems have different but analogous problems: Express's `AsyncLocalStorage` propagates across `await` but not across `process.nextTick` in older Node versions; Django's `ThreadLocal`-based middleware is invisible to async views unless you use `contextvars`; Laravel's `Auth::user()` is request-scoped via service container, but if you queue a job, the authenticated user is gone. The anchor teaches you that *context propagation across async boundaries is a real engineering problem with a real cost* — the deltas let you ignore it until your first background job runs as the wrong user.

## What the Deltas Taught That the Anchor Hid

The delta ecosystems exposed three conveniences that Spring had trained the implementer to tolerate as cost. **First: configuration as code.** The Spring implementation required a 60-line `SecurityFilterChain` bean written in a DSL that mixes lambda-DSL (`http.csrf(csrf -> csrf.disable())`) with chained-call DSL (`http.sessionManagement().sessionCreationPolicy(STATELESS)`). The Express implementation required 12 lines: `app.use(helmet()); app.use(json()); app.use(jwtMiddleware); app.use("/api", routes);`. The Laravel implementation required editing three lines in `app/Http/Kernel.php`'s `$middleware` and `$routeMiddleware` arrays. The Spring DSL is more expressive (you can compose authentication managers, post-filter processors, session fixation strategies), but you pay for that expressiveness on every change. The delta lesson: most projects do not need the expressiveness, and the cost of having it is real.

**Second: the type-system tax for I/O boundaries.** Spring's `@RequestBody @Valid LoginRequest` annotation gives you a parsed, validated POJO — at the cost of writing the `LoginRequest` class with `@NotBlank String username` and `@NotBlank String password` and an `@PasswordMatches` class-level constraint, plus the `@ControllerAdvice` that translates `MethodArgumentNotValidException` into a 400 JSON response. Express with `zod` gives you the same outcome in 6 lines: `const LoginRequest = z.object({ username: z.string().min(1), password: z.string().min(1) }); const body = LoginRequest.parse(req.body);`. The Spring approach is more discoverable (the IDE can navigate from the controller signature to the DTO), but the Express approach is faster to write and easier to refactor. The delta lesson: the boundary between HTTP and application code is a place where Spring's type discipline costs more than it returns, and a schema-validation library closes most of the gap.

**Third: the deploy story.** Spring Boot requires a JAR (or a layered Docker image) plus a JVM, and the JVM's `TieredStopAtLevel=1` vs `TieredStopAtLevel=4` choice determines whether your first 5 000 requests are 30% slower while C2 compiles. Express requires a `node` binary plus the `node_modules/` directory; the deploy is a `tar` and a `node server.js`. Laravel requires PHP-FPM (or, since Laravel 10, FrankenPHP as a single static binary). Django requires `gunicorn` (or `uvicorn` for async). The Spring deploy story is more complex because the JVM is more complex — and the cost of that complexity shows up in cold-start time (8.4 s vs 1.2 s for Express) and in image size (340 MB vs 78 MB). For a serverless or scale-to-zero deployment, this matters; for a long-running always-hot service, it doesn't. The delta lesson: "deploy complexity" is a real axis that the anchor hides because the JVM's tuning knobs are infinitely deep, and you can spend a career on them without noticing that the simpler runtime never had the knobs in the first place.

## Failure Modes Observed

Across the four implementations, six distinct failure modes were reproduced during the load test. Each is listed below with the metric that revealed it, the ecosystem(s) in which it surfaced first, and the fix that was applied.

**1. HikariCP connection starvation (Spring).** Under 10× load, the connection pool pinned at 20 active connections, and new requests blocked for 5 s before throwing `SQLTransientConnectionException: HikariPool-1 - Connection is not available, request timed out after 5000ms`. Root cause: the `UserDetailsService` was issuing one `SELECT` per request inside the filter chain, holding the connection for the full duration of `AuthorizationFilter` — including the slow downstream handler. Fix: move the `UserDetailsService` lookup into the handler (lazy load), or raise `maximumPoolSize` to 50 and add `connectionTimeout=1000` to fail fast. The first fix is correct; the second is a deferral.

**2. libuv thread pool exhaustion (Express).** Under load, `eventloop_lag_ms` climbed from 2 ms to 1 800 ms, and the request rate stalled even though CPU was only at 28%. Root cause: the JWKS fetch on key rotation was using `https.get` (which uses libuv's thread pool for DNS), and 4 concurrent key rotations saturated the default `UV_THREADPOOL_SIZE=4`. Fix: pre-fetch the JWKS at boot, cache it, and refresh on a 5-minute timer; never block the request path on a DNS lookup. Set `UV_THREADPOOL_SIZE=16` as a defence in depth.

**3. gunicorn worker timeout (Django).** At sustained 5 000 RPS, the 4 sync gunicorn workers each hit the 30 s timeout on a slow DB query, and the arbiter sent `SIGKILL` (`[CRITICAL] WORKER TIMEOUT`). Root cause: `CONN_MAX_AGE=0` (default) was opening a new connection per request, and under load the Postgres `max_connections=100` was hit, causing connection acquisition to take 10+ s. Fix: set `CONN_MAX_AGE=60` (persistent connections), or move to `gunicorn --worker-class uvicorn.workers.UvicornWorker` for async. The first fix moves the bottleneck to Postgres connection slots; the second moves it to the GIL on CPU-bound work.

**4. PDO `SQLSTATE[08006]` server closed connection (Laravel).** Under load, the PDO connection pool hit `max_connections` on Postgres, and the next `SELECT` failed with `SQLSTATE[08006] [SQLSTATE[08006] Server closed the connection unexpectedly`. Root cause: Laravel's default `'fetch_mode' => PDO::FETCH_OBJ` combined with `config('database.connections.pgsql.pool.max')` = 50, but Postgres was tuned to `max_connections=100` shared across all four app containers (200 desired). Fix: lower per-container pool to 20, or raise Postgres `max_connections` to 400 with `shared_buffers=4GB`.

**5. JWT replay under overload (Express, Laravel).** When the implementations returned 500 instead of 503 under overload, the client retried the *same* JWT against the same pod, and the rate of `401 Invalid token` climbed because the JWKS cache had been evicted under memory pressure. The auth layer was correct, but the *observability* layer was wrong — the 401 spike was the symptom, the JWKS eviction was the cause, and the link between them was invisible until `prometheus` showed `jwks_cache_evictions_total` spiking at the same instant. Fix: pin the JWKS in memory (don't evict), return 503 under overload (so the LB backs off), and alert on `jwks_cache_evictions_total > 0`.

**6. Clock skew rejection (Spring, Laravel).** A small fraction (~0.01%) of JWTs were rejected with `JwtValidationException: jwt expired` even though they were issued 30 s ago. Root cause: the JWT had `exp = iat + 900`, but the issuer's clock was 920 ms ahead of the verifier's clock, and Spring's `JwtDecoder` by default rejects tokens within 60 s of `exp` (`clockSkew` default = 60 s). The Laravel implementation used `firebase/php-jwt` with `leeway=0` and was even tighter. Fix: set an explicit `leeway=60` (Laravel) / `clockSkew(Duration.ofSeconds(60))` (Spring), and run `chrony` on both hosts with a max drift of 50 ms. The anchor teaches you that clocks are not free; the deltas teach you the same lesson faster (the failures showed up within 5 minutes of load test, not 30).

## Lessons Learned

The milestone was completed in 7 working days across the four ecosystems. The five lessons below are the ones that survived the post-mortem — the rest were ecosystem-specific noise.

**Lesson 1: the auth abstraction is a contract, not an implementation.** The four implementations differ wildly in syntax and in failure surface, but the *contract* — `authenticate(request) -> Principal | Error` — is identical. When you read the Spring `SecurityFilterChain` and the Express middleware side by side, you can map each Spring filter to an Express middleware one-to-one: `SecurityContextHolderAwareRequestFilter` ↔ `req.user = payload`; `AuthorizationFilter` ↔ `if (!req.user.hasRole) return res.status(403).end()`. Once you see the mapping, the framework choice stops being about "which is better at auth" and starts being about "which is better at the other things I also need to ship." Auth is solved; the question is what else the framework gets right.

**Lesson 2: the cost of an abstraction is paid at the boundary.** Spring's `SecurityFilterChain` is expensive to learn (4h 35m to first green test in this milestone) but cheap to extend (1h 20m to add a second auth scheme). Express's middleware is cheap to learn (38m) but expensive to extend (1h 50m to add the same second scheme, because you have to invent the composition pattern yourself). The total cost over the lifetime of the codebase is roughly equal — but the *distribution* of the cost is different, and that matters for staffing. A team with high turnover prefers Express's low onboarding cost; a team with stable membership and a long-lived codebase prefers Spring's low extension cost.

**Lesson 3: observability is a first-class requirement, not an add-on.** Two of the six failure modes above (JWKS eviction under memory pressure, clock skew rejection) were invisible in the application logs and only visible in the metrics. The implementations that shipped with `prom-client` (Express) or Micrometer (Spring) exposed the failure in 30 seconds; the implementations that didn't (initial Django, initial Laravel) required 20 minutes of `pdb` / `dd` to diagnose. The lesson: the test for "is this production-ready" is not "does it pass the load test" but "when the load test breaks it, do the metrics tell you why." If they don't, the implementation is not done.

**Lesson 4: fail closed, but fail with the right status code.** All four implementations correctly failed closed under overload — no unauthenticated request reached a protected handler. But two of them (Express, Laravel) returned 500 instead of 503, which caused the load balancer to retry against the same saturated pool, amplifying the failure. The lesson: the difference between 500 and 503 is not pedantic — it is the difference between "back off" and "try again immediately," and the LB's retry policy depends on it. Every auth middleware should map `pool-exhausted` to 503, `token-invalid` to 401, `token-expired` to 401 with a `WWW-Authenticate: Bearer error="invalid_token"` header, and `internal-error` to 500. Returning 500 for a pool exhaustion is a bug, even though the client sees the same outcome.

**Lesson 5: the second auth scheme is where the framework earns its keep.** The first auth scheme (bearer JWT) takes 38 minutes in Express and 4h 35m in Spring — Express wins. The second auth scheme (cookie session for the browser, bearer JWT for the API, both populating the same `Principal`) takes 1h 50m in Express and 1h 20m in Spring — Spring wins. The third auth scheme (OAuth2 login with GitHub) takes 3h in Express (you write the state-token dance yourself) and 45m in Spring (`spring-boot-starter-oauth2-client` gives you the whole flow). The crossover happens at scheme #2. If you will only ever have one auth scheme, pick the simplest framework. If you will have two or more, pick the one with the deepest auth story. The milestone's most important output is this crossover chart, not the RPS numbers.
