# 08.00. Map of Content: Distributed Systems

## Overview

This section covers **08. Distributed Systems Architecture and Big Data Compute**. It is part of the unified backend engineering vault described in [[00.01. Master Architectural Mental Model]]. The notes below are organized by concept, not by framework. Each concept is taught once at the conceptual level, then implemented deeply in the Spring Boot anchor stack, then rapidly translated into Express, Django, Flask, and Laravel as deltas.

Section 08 sits in the upper half of the layered mental model: above the operating system (02), networking (03), and runtime (04) layers that define what a single process can do, and above the framework (05), persistence (06), and messaging (07) layers that define how a single service is built. Once you have more than one process that needs to agree on state — a database replica, a Kubernetes control plane, a Kafka cluster, a Spark job across a hundred workers, a multi-service business transaction — you are in distributed-systems territory, and the rules change. The fallacies of distributed computing are unforgiving: the network is not reliable, latency is not zero, clocks do not agree, and a process can fail at any moment between two of your writes. Section 08 teaches the conceptual machinery — consensus protocols, transaction patterns, and partitioned compute engines — that production backends use to reason about those realities.

The section is divided into three sub-sections that build on each other. **08.01** establishes the physical and logical reality: why the fallacies exist, why clocks drift, why logical clocks exist, and how a group of nodes can agree on a single value despite failures (Paxos and Raft). **08.02** takes the consensus primitive and asks the next question — how do you implement a multi-service business transaction when each service owns its own database and 2PC is too blocking? The answer is the Saga pattern, with its two orchestration styles. **08.03** pivots to data-plane compute: how a single logical job is split across hundreds of JVMs and terabytes of data, why shuffles dominate wall-clock time, and how the same engine handles both bounded batch workloads and unbounded streaming workloads through micro-batching.

## Section Map

### 08.01. Distributed Systems Foundations and Consensus

- [[08.01.01. Core Fallacies and Physical Realities]] — The Peter Deutsch eight fallacies (network is reliable, latency is zero, bandwidth is infinite, network is secure, topology doesn't change, there is one administrator, transport cost is zero, network is homogeneous), the physical realities behind them (speed of light ~5 µs/km in fiber, packet loss, partition, clock drift ~17 ppm on commodity crystals, NTP ±10 ms bounded, GPS/atomic TrueTime), the CAP and PACELC bounds, and the logical-clock machinery (Lamport timestamps for causal ordering, vector clocks for detecting concurrent writes) that lets distributed nodes reason about "happened-before" without trusting wall-clock time.
- [[08.01.02. Consensus Protocols Raft and Paxos]] — The consensus problem (multiple nodes agreeing on a single value or ordered log despite crashes, packet loss, and reordering); Paxos (the classic Lamport 1998 algorithm with prepare/promise/accept/accepted phases and the multi-Paxos optimization that elects a stable leader to amortize the prepare round); Raft (Ongaro & Ousterhout 2014, designed for understandability with term-based leader election, RequestVote and AppendEntries RPCs, the commit index, and the five safety properties); why etcd, Consul, and Kafka KRaft all picked Raft; and quorum math (N/2 + 1) and split-brain prevention.

### 08.02. Distributed Transactions and Saga Patterns

- [[08.02.01. The Two-Phase Commit (2PC) Protocol]] — The coordinator/cohort participant model; Phase 1 (PREPARE) where cohorts write the intent to their WAL and vote YES/NO while holding row locks; Phase 2 (COMMIT/ABORT) where the coordinator decides globally and broadcasts the verdict; the blocking failure mode where a coordinator crash after Phase 1 leaves cohorts holding locks indefinitely (the "prepared transaction" Postgres `pg_prepared_xacts` entry); the three-phase commit (3PC) non-blocking alternative with its CanCommit/PreCommit/DoCommit rounds; and why 2PC is rarely used in microservices — synchronous, slow, and tightly coupled.
- [[08.02.02. The Saga Architectural Pattern]] — Decomposing a distributed transaction into a sequence of local ACID transactions, each with an explicit compensating transaction; the choreography style (each service publishes a domain event, the next service reacts — no central coordinator, simple to start, hard to debug as the chain grows); the orchestration style (a central orchestrator service — Temporal, Camunda, AWS Step Functions — sends commands, owns the state machine, and triggers compensations in reverse order); why compensations are not rollbacks (you cannot un-charge a credit card, you issue a refund); and the production reality of idempotency, retries, and the "semantic lock."

### 08.03. Distributed Data Processing with Apache Spark

- [[08.03.01. Batch vs. Stream Processing Foundations]] — Bounded data (batch — finite input, runs to completion, e.g., nightly ETL over yesterday's parquet files) vs. unbounded data (stream — infinite input, runs forever, e.g., real-time fraud alerts from Kafka); the latency/throughput tradeoff (batch optimizes MB/s, stream optimizes end-to-end latency); the four windowing strategies (tumbling, hopping, sliding, session); event-time vs. processing-time and the skew between them; and the watermark mechanism for handling late-arriving data without unbounded state.
- [[08.03.02. Apache Spark Architecture and Compute Engine]] — The driver (SparkContext / SparkSession, the DAG scheduler that builds stages from RDD lineage, the task scheduler that ships tasks to executors); the executors (JVM processes with cached partitions and slots); the cluster manager (Standalone, YARN, Mesos, Kubernetes); the RDD abstraction (immutable, partitioned, lineage-tracked for fault recovery); the DataFrame/Dataset unified structured APIs; the Catalyst optimizer (logical plan → optimized logical plan → physical plan); and the Tungsten execution engine (off-heap memory, cache-aware data layouts, whole-stage code generation).
- [[08.03.03. Spark Operations Partitioning and Shuffling]] — Transformations (lazy — `map`, `filter`, `select`, `join`) vs. actions (eager — `collect`, `count`, `write`); narrow dependencies (1:1 partition mapping, no network I/O, pipeline-able) vs. wide dependencies (M:N partition mapping, requires shuffle); the shuffle write phase (data spilled to local disk sorted by `(partition_id, key)`, hash- or sort-based partitioners); the shuffle read phase (executors fetch blocks across the network from the source nodes or the external shuffle service); why shuffles dominate wall-clock time (network + disk I/O + serialization) and the practical mitigations (`reduceByKey` over `groupByKey`, broadcast joins for small dimensions, `coalesce` over `repartition` for downscaling).
- [[08.03.04. Spark Structured Streaming]] — The micro-batch engine (default, processes new data every `trigger.interval`, e.g., 10 seconds — high throughput, ~100 ms floor latency) vs. the continuous engine (experimental since Spark 2.3, long-running tasks, sub-millisecond latency, restricted operator set); event-time processing with `window(event_time, "5 minutes")`; the watermark (`withWatermark("event_time", "10 minutes")`) that drops data older than the threshold and lets the engine close windows and free state; the three output modes (append — only emit finalized rows, update — emit changed rows, complete — emit the entire result table every trigger); and the sink integrations (Kafka, console, file, `foreach` / `foreachBatch` for arbitrary side effects).

## Conceptual Roadmap

Section 08 is structured as a three-act story. Each act answers a question that the previous act raised, and the notes should be read in the order the questions are asked.

**Act I — What is the medium we are operating in? (08.01)** Before you can build anything distributed, you have to internalize the physical reality. The fallacies of distributed computing (08.01.01) are not a checklist — they are eight ways that engineers have repeatedly gotten burned because they assumed the distributed system behaves like a local system. The first half of the note establishes the physics (speed of light, packet loss, partition, clock drift); the second half establishes the logical machinery (Lamport timestamps, vector clocks) that lets you reason about ordering without trusting the wall clock. Only once you accept the fallacies can you reason about consensus (08.01.02): Paxos is the mathematically proven baseline, Raft is the engineering-friendly version that real systems (etcd, Consul, Kafka KRaft) actually ship.

**Act II — How do multiple services agree on a business outcome? (08.02)** Consensus solves the narrow problem of agreeing on a single value. A distributed business transaction — "book a flight, reserve a hotel, charge the credit card, emit a ticket" — is a different beast: each step is owned by a different service with its own database, and 2PC (08.02.01) is the textbook answer that nobody uses in microservices because it blocks. The Saga pattern (08.02.02) is the production answer: trade ACID isolation for eventual consistency, and accept that you must write explicit compensating actions for every step. The choreography vs. orchestration choice is a real architectural fork — choreography scales better but is harder to debug; orchestration is debuggable but the orchestrator becomes a critical piece of infrastructure.

**Act III — How do you compute over data that does not fit on one machine? (08.03)** Once you have accepted that data lives in partitions across many nodes, the question becomes how to express computation over those partitions. Spark's answer is the RDD / DataFrame abstraction with lazy evaluation (08.03.02): you describe the computation as a DAG, the engine decides the physical plan via Catalyst, and Tungsten generates JVM bytecode to avoid object allocation. The dominant cost is the shuffle (08.03.03) — narrow transformations pipeline for free, wide transformations pay the network + disk tax. Structured Streaming (08.03.04) is the same engine treated as an unbounded table: micro-batches give you high throughput at ~100 ms latency, the continuous engine drops to sub-millisecond, and watermarks let you reason about event time even when the source is out of order.

The cross-cutting theme across all three acts is the trade between strong consistency and operational viability. A single-machine ACID transaction is the easiest mental model — every read sees the latest write, every commit is atomic, every failure is a clean rollback — but it does not survive the moment you need a second machine. Section 08 is, in effect, the engineering response to that realization: every protocol, every pattern, every engine in the section accepts some form of weakened consistency (eventual, causal, read-your-writes) in exchange for the ability to keep running when a node, a network, or a datacenter fails. The fallacies are the reasons you cannot have the easy model back. The consensus protocols are how you agree on a single order despite the fallacies. The Saga is how you transact business across services that cannot agree on a single order. Spark is how you compute over data that has been partitioned across machines for the same reason. Read the section with that lens and the otherwise-disparate topics form a single coherent picture: distributed systems engineering is the discipline of choosing which consistency guarantee to weaken, by how much, and in exchange for what.

## How to Read This Section

1. **Read the concept notes first.** They establish the mental model. Read 08.01.01 before 08.01.02 — you cannot understand why Raft exists without first understanding why the wall clock is unreliable. Read 08.02.01 before 08.02.02 — Sagas are a reaction to 2PC's blocking failure mode. Read 08.03.01 → 08.03.02 → 08.03.03 → 08.03.04 in numeric order; each builds on the previous.
2. **Implement the anchor.** Where the Spring Boot anchor exists, build the real, load-tested implementation. For 08.02 (Sagas), this means wiring Temporal or Camunda into a real workflow with at least three participants and at least one compensation path.
3. **Run the delta pass.** Open the other ecosystems and implement the same feature as a translation exercise. The delta notes show how Express, Django/Flask, and Laravel express the same distributed-transaction primitive — usually via a different orchestration library, but the conceptual shape is identical.
4. **Connect.** Follow the Related Notes links to reinforce the cross-ecosystem graph. 08.01 (consensus) underpins 07.01 (Kafka ISR) and 06.05 (database replication); 08.02 (sagas) underpins 13.x (capstone projects); 08.03 (Spark) underpins 07.04 (Kafka Streams) and the analytics projects in section 13.

> [!tip] Reading order
> If you are new to this section, follow the numeric ordering. If you are revising, jump directly to whichever concept you forgot — but always re-read 08.01.01 (the fallacies) first, because every other note in this section assumes you have internalized them.
