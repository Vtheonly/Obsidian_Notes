# 08.01.01. Core Fallacies and Physical Realities

## What This Is

Every "distributed systems" course starts with the same warning, and it is the same warning for a reason: every production incident in a distributed system is a rediscovery of one of the fallacies. The fallacies are not theoretical — they are eight specific assumptions that feel true when you are writing code on your laptop with a localhost database and a single-node Redis, and that quietly break the moment you cross a real network. This note collects the eight fallacies that Peter Deutsch and his colleagues at Sun Microsystems articulated in the mid-1990s, together with the physical realities behind them: the speed of light in fiber (~5 µs/km, which makes cross-region RTT a hard physical floor), the ~17 ppm drift of commodity quartz crystals, the asymmetric failure modes of packet loss vs. packet delay, and the CAP/PACELC bounds that follow.

The note then covers the clock machinery that distributed systems use to paper over those realities — physical clocks (NTP, GPS, atomic clocks, Google's TrueTime), and the logical clocks (Lamport timestamps, vector clocks) that abandon wall-clock time entirely and instead derive ordering from the message-passing graph itself. Logical clocks are the conceptual bridge to the next note, [[08.01.02. Consensus Protocols Raft and Paxos]]: consensus protocols are what you build once you have accepted that you cannot trust the wall clock and the network is going to fail.

## The Details

### The Fallacies of Distributed Computing: The network is reliable, latency is zero, bandwidth is in...

Peter Deutsch and colleagues at Sun Microsystems in the early 1990s enumerated eight assumptions that engineers new to distributed systems reliably make and that reliably produce outages. The fallacies are listed here in canonical order, with the physical reality that breaks each assumption. Read them as a checklist, not as an exhaustive list — they are the starting point, not the ending point, of distributed-systems paranoia.

```text
#  Fallacy                          Physical reality that breaks it
─  ───────────────────────────────  ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
1  The network is reliable          Cables get unplugged, switches reboot, BGP routes get hijacked,
                                   AWS us-east-1 has a multi-hour outage ~1-2x/year. TCP retransmits
                                   mask packet loss but do not eliminate it; 0.1% loss on a 10 GbE
                                   link cuts effective throughput to ~6 Gbps (Mathis equation).
2  Latency is zero                  Speed of light in fiber = ~5 µs/km. NYC→London = 70 ms RTT floor
                                   (one-way 35 ms). NYC→Tokyo = 110 ms RTT. The "user-perceived"
                                   latency budget is 100 ms for "instant"; cross-region synchronous
                                   RPC already consumes the entire budget before any work happens.
3  Bandwidth is infinite            10 GbE NIC, but a single TCP flow tops out ~3 Gbps over a 70 ms
                                   RTT link without tuning (BDP = 10 Gbps × 0.070 s = 87.5 MB window).
                                   Spark shuffle of 10 TB across 200 executors saturates the rack
                                   network for ~30 minutes if partitioning is wrong.
4  The network is secure            TLS is necessary but not sufficient. SSRF, DNS rebinding,
                                   compromised BGP, the SMTP-level plaintext issue, supply-chain
                                   attacks on the NPM/PyPI registry, the 2014 Heartbleed bug.
5  Topology doesn't change          Autoscaler adds/removes nodes. Kubernetes rolling deploy cycles
                                   pods every few minutes. Service mesh reconfigures mTLS peers.
                                   A cached IP address from a DNS lookup is stale by the time you
                                   use it; TTLs are a hint, not a contract.
6  There is one administrator       Your microservice calls Stripe, Twilio, SendGrid, and a legacy
                                   SOAP service owned by a team in another timezone. You cannot
                                   coordinate deploys with all of them. Their outage is your outage.
7  Transport cost is zero           Serializing JSON costs CPU; deserializing it costs more. TLS
                                   handshake = 1 RTT (TLS 1.3) + asymmetric crypto. Protobuf is
                                   ~5x faster to parse than JSON. A 1 KB JSON payload over HTTP/2
                                   has ~30-50 bytes of HPACK overhead; over HTTP/1.1 ~800 bytes.
8  The network is homogeneous       IPv4 + IPv6 dual stack. HTTP/1.1 + HTTP/2 + HTTP/3. Some services
                                   speak gRPC, some REST, some SOAP. MTU 1500 vs. 9000 (jumbo
                                   frames). Asymmetric routing. PMTU black holes. Path MTU
                                   discovery is itself a distributed protocol that fails.
```

Each fallacy produces a recognizable incident signature. Fallacy #1 (network is reliable) shows up as `ConnectionRefused` storms after an AWS AZ outage; the fix is retries with exponential backoff and jitter, plus a circuit breaker (see Hystrix / Resilience4j). Fallacy #2 (latency is zero) shows up as a 250 ms "fast" API call that the team cannot explain — they are calling a service in another region synchronously where they should be batching or async-publishing. Fallacy #3 (bandwidth is infinite) shows up as a 95th-percentile latency spike at the top of every hour when the nightly Spark job starts shuffling 50 TB across the same switch the user-facing API uses.

The Mathis equation (Mathis, Semke, Mahdavi, Ott 1997, "The Macroscopic Behavior of the TCP Congestion Avoidance Algorithm") gives the formal upper bound on TCP throughput under random packet loss: `throughput ≤ (MSS × C) / (RTT × √p)`, where MSS is the maximum segment size (~1460 bytes after TCP/IP headers), RTT is the round-trip time, p is the packet loss probability, and C is a constant ~1.22 capturing the TCP Reno congestion-window dynamics. For a cross-region link with 70 ms RTT and 0.1% loss (a typical long-haul internet path), the upper bound is `(1460 × 8 × 1.22) / (0.070 × √0.001) ≈ 6.5 Mbps` — three orders of magnitude below the link's 10 Gbps capacity. This is why even small amounts of packet loss are catastrophic for high-throughput TCP: 0.01% loss on the same 70 ms RTT link caps a single TCP flow at ~20 Mbps; 1% loss caps it at ~2 Mbps. The Mathis bound explains why "just retry on failure" is a fundamentally limited strategy — retransmits consume the bandwidth the original transmission would have used, and the throughput ceiling does not rise. Production mitigations are (a) multiple parallel TCP connections (HTTP/1.1 connection pools, S3 multi-part upload), (b) larger initial congestion windows (Linux `net.ipv4.tcp_init_cwnd`, default 10, tunable up to ~100 for high-BDP datacenter links), and (c) switching to UDP-based protocols (QUIC, HTTP/3) that implement their own congestion control without TCP's loss-based backoff and that survive a single packet loss without stalling the entire flow.

The CAP theorem (Brewer's conjecture, 2000; Gilbert-Lynch proof, 2002) is the formal consequence of fallacy #1: in the presence of a network partition (P), a distributed system must choose either availability (A) or consistency (C). Most production systems pick AP and reconcile later — Cassandra, Dynamo, Kafka (default `acks=1`). The PACELC extension (Abadi 2010) adds the "else" branch: even when there is no partition, the system must choose between latency (L) and consistency (C). This is why etcd (Raft, CP+EL) is slower than Cassandra (AP+EL) for writes — etcd must round-trip to a quorum and persist to a majority of WALs before acknowledging, while Cassandra can acknowledge after one replica's local write. The PACELC choice is per-operation, not per-system: Cassandra lets you pick `CONSISTENCY ONE` (PA/EL, fast) or `CONSISTENCY ALL` (PC/EC, safe) per query; MongoDB lets you pick `w:1` vs `w:majority,j:true` per write; PostgreSQL lets you pick `synchronous_commit=off` vs `on` per transaction. The production mistake is to assume the database's default is correct for every operation — financial transactions need PC/EC, social-media like-counters can tolerate PA/EL, and using the wrong one for either is a bug.

The production failure mode for the fallacies as a class is the "this can never happen" incident: an engineer builds a feature assuming the network, the clock, and the topology are all stable, and the feature works perfectly in staging where those assumptions happen to hold. In production the assumption breaks once a week, the feature silently corrupts data or hangs, and the team spends three days in a postmortem concluding "we should have used a retry policy." The mitigation is not a single library — it is the cultural discipline of asking, for every cross-network call, "what happens when this fails, and what happens when this is slow?"

### Clock Synchronization: Physical Clocks, NTP Clock Drift, Monotonic Clocks vs. Wall-Clock Time, an...

A commodity computer keeps time with a quartz crystal oscillator that drifts. The drift rate of a typical TXCO (temperature-compensated crystal oscillator) is ~17 parts per million, which is roughly 1.5 seconds per day. Two machines in the same rack, synchronized to the same NTP server, can disagree by 10–50 ms at any given instant; two machines on opposite sides of the planet, synchronized only to their stratum-1 NTP sources, can disagree by 100–250 ms. This is the physical reality that breaks every naive distributed algorithm that compares timestamps across nodes.

```text
NTP stratum hierarchy:
  Stratum 0   ──  Atomic clock (Cesium), GPS receiver, PTP grandmaster (physical reference)
                   │
  Stratum 1   ──  time.cloudflare.com, time.windows.com, pool.ntp.org servers
                   │   (directly attached to stratum 0)
  Stratum 2   ──  corporate NTP servers, sync from stratum 1
                   │
  Stratum 3+  ──  application servers, sync from stratum 2

Drift:   ~17 ppm (≈ 1.5 s/day) on commodity crystals, ~0.01 ppm on OCXO,
         ~10⁻¹³ on rubidium, ~10⁻¹⁵ on cesium fountain.
NTP accuracy: ±10 ms LAN, ±100 ms internet (bounded by network asymmetry).
PTP (IEEE 1588): sub-microsecond on LAN with hardware timestamping.
```

NTP (Network Time Protocol, RFC 5905) corrects drift gradually by slewing the clock — speeding or slowing the crystal by up to 500 ppm — to avoid backward jumps. `ntpd` will refuse to step the clock if the offset exceeds 128 ms without operator approval; `chronyd` (the default on RHEL/CentOS 8+) is more aggressive and will step. The 2017 Cloudflare incident is the canonical "what happens when NTP does a backward step": on a leap-second insertion at 2017-01-01 00:00:00 UTC, Cloudflare's Riak and PostgreSQL instances saw system calls that computed negative durations (`futex` with `tv_sec` going from 23:59:60 to 23:59:59), which caused goroutine spinlocks and 1% of DNS queries to fail for an hour. The modern mitigation is leap-second smearing (Google's 24-hour linear smear, Amazon's identical approach, UTC-SLS in Bloomberg terminals).

There are two clock APIs in POSIX, and conflating them is one of the most common distributed-systems bugs. `CLOCK_REALTIME` (exposed in Java as `System.currentTimeMillis()`, in Python as `time.time()`, in Go as `time.Now()`'s wall component) is the wall clock — it is adjusted by NTP and can jump backward. `CLOCK_MONOTONIC` (`System.nanoTime()` in Java, `time.monotonic()` in Python, `time.Since(start)` in Go) is a monotonically increasing counter that is never adjusted. Measuring elapsed time with `CLOCK_REALTIME` produces negative durations when NTP slews or steps the clock backward; measuring it with `CLOCK_MONOTONIC` is correct. Wall-clock is for "what time is it now" — log timestamps, scheduled job triggers, TTL expiry. Monotonic clock is for "how long did this take" — timeouts, retry backoffs, performance metrics.

```java
// WRONG — wall-clock can go backward under NTP slewing
long start = System.currentTimeMillis();
doWork();
long elapsedMs = System.currentTimeMillis() - start;   // can be negative

// CORRECT — monotonic clock is never adjusted
long start = System.nanoTime();
doWork();
long elapsedMs = TimeUnit.NANOSECONDS.toMillis(System.nanoTime() - start);
```

Google's Spanner sidesteps the whole problem with TrueTime, an API that returns a time interval `[earliest, latest]` rather than a point. The interval's width ε is bounded by GPS and atomic clocks physically installed in every datacenter — Google publishes ε < 7 ms in production (down from a 14 ms bound at launch). Spanner commits a transaction only after waiting out the uncertainty window (`commit-wait`), so that any two timestamps assigned by TrueTime are externally consistent: if `t1 < t2` then the transaction at `t1` truly committed before the one at `t2` started. This is the only way to build a globally-consistent externally-synchronous database without logical clocks, and the cost is a 7 ms floor on commit latency and a multi-million-dollar GPS installation per datacenter. For everyone else, the answer is the next H3: stop trusting the wall clock and start using logical clocks.

The production failure mode for clock bugs is subtle data corruption. A common pattern: a service uses `Date.now()` to assign timestamps to events, stores them in a database, and queries "give me all events since the last 10 seconds." When NTP steps the clock backward by 200 ms, events written after the step appear to predate events written before the step, and the query silently drops them. The metric that reveals it is a sudden gap in the count of events ingested per minute, coincident with an NTP correction logged in `/var/log/chrony/tracking.log`. The fix is either monotonic clocks for elapsed time, or logical clocks (next H3) for cross-node ordering.

### Logical Clocks: Lamport Timestamps (causal ordering) and Vector Clocks (detecting concurrent writ...

Logical clocks abandon wall-clock time entirely. The insight, due to Leslie Lamport's 1978 paper "Time, Clocks, and the Ordering of Events in a Distributed System," is that you do not need to know "what time is it" — you need to know "did event A happen before event B." The happened-before relation, written `A → B`, is defined inductively: (1) on a single process, events are ordered by program order; (2) if A is the send of a message and B is the receipt of that message, then `A → B`; (3) the relation is transitive. If neither `A → B` nor `B → A`, the events are **concurrent** — they could have happened in either order from any observer's perspective, and the system has to resolve the conflict some other way (last-writer-wins, application merge, CRDT).

A Lamport timestamp assigns a single integer to each event such that `A → B` implies `L(A) < L(B)`. The rule is: each process maintains a local counter `L`; before producing any event, the process increments `L`; when sending a message, the process includes its current `L` in the message; on receiving a message with timestamp `L_m`, the process sets `L = max(L, L_m) + 1`. The counter only ever increases, and the partial order of happened-before is preserved. The catch: `L(A) < L(B)` does **not** imply `A → B` — Lamport timestamps give you a total order that is consistent with causality, but they cannot tell you whether two events were causally related or concurrent. For that, you need vector clocks.

```text
Lamport timestamp, single counter per process:

   P1:  L=1     L=2            L=3
        ●───────●──────────────●
                │ m1
                ▼
   P2:          ● L=max(0,2)+1=3     L=4
                │                    │ m2
                ▼                    │
   P3:          ● L=max(0,3)+1=4     ● L=max(4,4)+1=5

   Total order: P1@1 < P1@2 < P2@3 < P3@4 < P2@4 < P3@5
   But: P2@4 and P3@4 are concurrent — neither caused the other.
   Lamport can't tell you that; it just gives you a number to break ties.
```

A vector clock (Mattern 1989, Fidge 1988) assigns an N-length vector `V` to each event, where N is the number of processes. Each process maintains its own vector; before producing an event, process `i` increments `V[i]`; on sending a message, the process includes its current vector; on receiving vector `V_m`, the process sets `V[j] = max(V[j], V_m[j])` for all `j`, then increments `V[i]`. The comparison: `V(A) < V(B)` iff every component of `V(A)` is ≤ the corresponding component of `V(B)` and at least one is strictly less; in that case `A → B`. If neither `V(A) < V(B)` nor `V(B) < V(A)`, the events are concurrent — the vector clock detects this directly. The cost is O(N) space per event and O(N) comparison time, which becomes painful above ~100 processes; in practice, systems use **version vectors** (which only track the per-replica write count for the replicas that wrote, not the full N-vector) or **dotted version vectors** to keep the size manageable.

```text
Vector clock, N=3 processes:

   P1:  [1,0,0]              [2,0,0]                [3,0,0]
        ●─────────────────────●──────────────────────●
                              │ m1
                              ▼
   P2:                       ●[2,1,0]    [2,2,0]
                              │            │ m2
                              ▼            │
   P3:                       ●[2,1,1]     ●[2,2,2]
                                             │
                                             ▼ (concurrent write on P1)
   P1:        ...                            [3,0,0]  ← CONCURRENT with [2,2,2]

   [2,2,2] vs [3,0,0]:  neither ≤ the other  →  concurrent, conflict.
```

Dynamo (DeCandia et al. 2007), Riak, and Cassandra (with `dclocal_read_repair_chance` and LWW) all use vector clocks or version vectors to detect concurrent writes during a network partition. The conflict is then surfaced to the application as a sibling list (Riak) or resolved by last-writer-wins on a wall-clock timestamp (Cassandra default, which loses data during clock skew — see the previous H3). The git version-control system uses a related mechanism (commit DAGs) to detect concurrent branches; CRDTs (Conflict-free Replicated Data Types, Shapiro et al. 2011) go further by structuring the data so that concurrent writes always have a deterministic merge — used by Redis CRDTs (Active-Active), Riak Data Types, and Automerge.

The production failure mode for logical-clock misuse is "lost update." A common pattern: two clients read the same key, both update it, both write it back. Without a vector clock, the second write silently overwrites the first; the user's data is lost and nobody notices for a week. With a vector clock, the database sees `[2,1,1]` and `[2,2,1]` (concurrent) and either (a) keeps both as siblings and asks the application to merge, or (b) applies last-writer-wins on a wall clock and loses the update anyway. The metric that reveals it is a sudden drop in the version-number delta between reads and writes on the same key. The fix is either CRDTs (for simple data types like counters and sets) or application-level merge logic (for complex documents), and accepting that conflict resolution is a first-class engineering concern rather than a database feature.

## Key Takeaways

1. **The eight fallacies are not a list — they are eight ways to lose data.** Each one corresponds to a class of production incident: connection storms (#1), cross-region latency (#2), shuffle saturation (#3), TLS bypass (#4), stale DNS (#5), uncoordinated deploys (#6), serialization cost (#7), protocol mismatch (#8).
2. **Wall-clock is unsafe for ordering across nodes.** NTP can step backward, leap seconds break arithmetic, and two nodes' clocks can disagree by 100 ms even when both are "synchronized." Use monotonic clocks for elapsed time and logical clocks for cross-node ordering.
3. **CAP and PACELC are the formal consequences of fallacy #1.** A partition forces a choice between availability and consistency; even without a partition, every write chooses between latency and consistency. There is no distributed system that escapes this tradeoff — etcd pays it in 7+ ms commit latency, Cassandra pays it in eventual-consistency semantics.
