# 4.4 Image Degradation and Noise Models

> **Prerequisites:** [[4.2 The Fourier Transform]], [[4.3 Frequency Domain Filtering]]
> **Connections:** [[3.3 Non-Linear Smoothing Filters]] (median for salt-and-pepper), [[5.5 Deep Learning for Imaging]] (DnCNN for learned denoising)

Real images are degraded by noise, blur, and other distortions. Understanding the **noise model** — what type of noise, how it is added, what it looks like — is essential for choosing the right restoration method. This note covers the common noise types and the degradation model that unifies them.

---

## The degradation model

A degraded image $g$ is modeled as the original image $f$ passed through a degradation system $H$ plus noise $n$:

$$g(x, y) = H[f(x, y)] + n(x, y)$$

- $H$ is the **degradation function** (usually a blur — motion blur, defocus, atmospheric turbulence).
- $n$ is the **noise** (random, signal-independent or signal-dependent).
- $f$ is the original (unknown) image; $g$ is what we observe.

**Restoration** is the problem of estimating $f$ from $g$, given (or estimating) $H$ and $n$. This is **ill-posed** — many $f$'s could produce the same $g$ — so restoration requires assumptions about $f$ (smoothness, sparsity) or about the noise (distribution).

### Enhancement vs. restoration

- **Enhancement:** improve the image's appearance subjectively (contrast, sharpness) — no model of degradation.
- **Restoration:** recover the original image using a model of how it was degraded — objective, model-based.

Restoration is harder but more principled. If you know the degradation model, you can invert it (or approximately invert it); if you don't, you're just guessing.

---

## Noise models

### 1. Gaussian (additive) noise

$$n(x, y) \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)$$

Each pixel has independent Gaussian noise added. The noise is the same regardless of the pixel's intensity (signal-independent).

- **Source:** thermal noise in the sensor, electronic noise in the amplifier.
- **Appearance:** grainy, uniform across the image.
- **Best filter:** Gaussian low-pass (linear, optimal under Gaussian noise assumptions), or wavelet thresholding.

### 2. Salt-and-pepper (impulsive) noise

Random pixels are set to 0 (pepper) or 255 (salt), with probability $p$.

- **Source:** faulty pixels in the sensor, bit errors in transmission, dust on the lens.
- **Appearance:** scattered black and white dots.
- **Best filter:** **median filter** (see [[3.3 Non-Linear Smoothing Filters]]). Linear filters (Gaussian, mean) fail because averaging with 0 or 255 pulls the result toward the extreme. The median ignores the extreme values entirely.

### 3. Speckle (multiplicative) noise

$$g = f \cdot (1 + n), \quad n \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)$$

The noise is **multiplied** by the signal — brighter pixels have more noise. This is fundamentally different from additive noise.

- **Source:** coherent imaging systems (ultrasound, SAR radar, holography). The coherent illumination causes interference patterns (speckle).
- **Appearance:** granular, "salt-and-pepper-like" but proportional to intensity.
- **Best filter:** log-transform (converts multiplicative to additive), then filter, then exp-transform. Or specialized adaptive filters (Lee, Frost).

### 4. Poisson (shot) noise

$$n \sim \text{Poisson}(\lambda)$$

where $\lambda$ is the expected photon count. The noise variance equals the mean — brighter pixels have more noise, but the *relative* noise decreases as $\sqrt{\lambda}/\lambda = 1/\sqrt{\lambda}$.

- **Source:** low-light imaging (few photons), fluorescence microscopy.
- **Appearance:** grainy in dark regions (where photon counts are low), clean in bright regions.
- **Best filter:** Poisson denoising (specialized, often based on variance-stabilizing transforms like the Anscombe transform, then Gaussian denoising).

### 5. Uniform noise

$n \sim \text{Uniform}(a, b)$ — rarely physical, sometimes used as a model for quantization noise.

---

## Noise identification

How do you know which noise model applies? 

1. **Look at the noise profile:** if the noise is uniform across intensity, it's additive (Gaussian). If it's proportional to intensity, it's multiplicative (speckle). If it's extreme dots, it's impulsive (salt-and-pepper).
2. **Look at the histogram:** Gaussian noise broadens the histogram symmetrically. Salt-and-pepper adds spikes at 0 and 255. Speckle broadens the histogram proportionally to intensity.
3. **Look at the frequency spectrum:** periodic noise appears as spikes. White noise (Gaussian) spreads across all frequencies. Speckle has a characteristic structure.

## Blur models (the $H$ in $g = Hf + n$)

### Motion blur

When the camera or subject moves during exposure, the image is blurred along the direction of motion. In the frequency domain, motion blur appears as a **directional sinc pattern** — the spectrum is attenuated along lines perpendicular to the motion.

### Defocus blur

When the lens is out of focus, point sources become disks (the circle of confusion). In the frequency domain, defocus blur attenuates high frequencies (the blur is a low-pass filter).

### Atmospheric turbulence

In long-range imaging (astronomy, surveillance), atmospheric turbulence causes a time-varying blur. Modeled as a Gaussian low-pass whose strength depends on the turbulence.

## Restoration methods

### Inverse filtering

If we know $H$, we can (try to) invert it: $\hat{f} = \mathcal{F}^{-1}\{G / H\}$. The problem: where $H$ is near zero (high frequencies, for a blur), the division $G/H$ **amplifies noise** catastrophically. Inverse filtering is useless in the presence of noise.

### Wiener filtering

The **Wiener filter** is the optimal linear filter for restoring a signal degraded by a known blur and additive noise. It balances de-blurring against noise amplification:

$$\hat{F} = \frac{H^*}{|H|^2 + S_n/S_f} G$$

where $S_n$ is the noise power spectrum and $S_f$ is the signal power spectrum. The term $S_n/S_f$ (the noise-to-signal ratio) regularizes the division — where the noise is high, the filter backs off (does not try to de-blur).

The Wiener filter is the classical solution to the deblurring problem. It is optimal in the mean-squared-error sense, under Gaussian assumptions. Modern methods (deep learning — see [[5.5 Deep Learning for Imaging]]) outperform it, but it remains the baseline.

---

## What you should walk away with

1. The degradation model: $g = H[f] + n$. Restoration estimates $f$ from $g$ given $H$ and $n$.
2. **Gaussian noise:** additive, uniform — use linear filters (Gaussian low-pass).
3. **Salt-and-pepper noise:** impulsive, extreme — use **median filter** (linear filters fail).
4. **Speckle noise:** multiplicative, proportional to intensity — log-transform + filter, or adaptive filters.
5. **Poisson noise:** signal-dependent, worse in dark regions — variance-stabilizing transforms.
6. **Blur models:** motion (directional), defocus (low-pass), atmospheric (Gaussian low-pass).
7. **Inverse filtering** amplifies noise (useless in practice); **Wiener filtering** balances de-blurring against noise (the classical optimal solution).
