# 3.7 Geometric Transformations and Interpolation

> **Prerequisites:** [[1.6 Pixel Relationships and Distance Metrics]], [[3.1 The Mechanics of Spatial Filtering]]
> **Connections:** [[3.8 Feature Descriptors - SIFT, SURF, ORB, HOG]] (descriptors must be invariant to these transforms)

Geometric transformations change the **spatial arrangement** of pixels — rotating, scaling, translating, or warping an image. They are essential for image registration, data augmentation (see [[07. Data Augmentation]]), panorama stitching, and rectifying perspective distortion. This note covers the common transformations and the critical issue of **interpolation** — how to compute pixel values at non-integer locations.

---

## The core problem: where do output pixels come from?

A geometric transformation defines a mapping from **input coordinates** $(x, y)$ to **output coordinates** $(x', y')$. But there is a subtlety: the output grid is discrete (integer pixel positions), while the transformation maps to continuous coordinates. So we use **inverse mapping**: for each output pixel $(x', y')$, find the corresponding input location $(x, y) = T^{-1}(x', y')$, and **sample** the input image at that location.

The problem: $(x, y)$ is usually a non-integer location, and the input image only has values at integer positions. **Interpolation** estimates the value at the non-integer location from the surrounding integer-position values.

---

## The transformations

### 1. Translation

Shift the image by $(t_x, t_y)$:

$$\begin{pmatrix} x' \\ y' \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & t_x \\ 0 & 1 & t_y \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}$$

The "1" in the third row/column uses **homogeneous coordinates** — a trick that lets us express all affine transforms as matrix multiplications (otherwise translation would require addition, breaking the matrix formulation).

### 2. Scaling (resizing)

Scale by factors $(s_x, s_y)$:

$$\begin{pmatrix} x' \\ y' \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} s_x & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & s_y & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}$$

- $s_x = s_y = 2$: zoom in (image gets larger).
- $s_x = s_y = 0.5$: shrink (image gets smaller).
- $s_x \neq s_y$: non-uniform scaling (stretches the aspect ratio).

### 3. Rotation

Rotate by angle $\theta$ (counterclockwise):

$$\begin{pmatrix} x' \\ y' \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta & 0 \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}$$

**Note:** in image coordinates, the $y$-axis often points down (not up as in math), so rotation directions may appear reversed.

### 4. Affine transformation (general)

The combination of translation, scaling, rotation, and **shear** — any transformation that preserves parallel lines (parallel lines remain parallel after the transform). The general affine matrix has 6 degrees of freedom:

$$\begin{pmatrix} x' \\ y' \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} a & b & c \\ d & e & f \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}$$

An affine transform can be determined from **3 point correspondences** (3 points before + 3 points after). This is used in image registration: find 3 matching landmarks, compute the affine transform, warp one image to align with the other.

### 5. Perspective transformation (homography)

The most general 2D transformation — preserves straight lines but not parallelism (parallel lines can converge). Has 8 degrees of freedom:

$$\begin{pmatrix} x' \\ y' \\ w' \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} h_{11} & h_{12} & h_{13} \\ h_{21} & h_{22} & h_{23} \\ h_{31} & h_{32} & h_{33} \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}, \quad \text{then } (x', y') = (x'/w', y'/w')$$

The division by $w'$ (the homogeneous coordinate) is what allows perspective effects — distant objects appear smaller. A perspective transform is determined from **4 point correspondences**.

**Use cases:**
- **Perspective correction:** photograph a document at an angle, then rectify it to a rectangle (4 corners → 4 corners).
- **Panorama stitching:** align multiple photos of the same scene into a panorama.
- **Augmented reality:** project virtual objects onto a real-world plane.

```python
import cv2
import numpy as np

# Affine transform from 3 point correspondences
src = np.float32([[50,50],[200,50],[50,200]])
dst = np.float32([[10,100],[200,50],[100,250]])
M = cv2.getAffineTransform(src, dst)
warped = cv2.warpAffine(image, M, (width, height))

# Perspective transform from 4 point correspondences
src = np.float32([[0,0],[300,0],[0,300],[300,300]])
dst = np.float32([[50,80],[250,20],[100,280],[280,260]])
M = cv2.getPerspectiveTransform(src, dst)
warped = cv2.warpPerspective(image, M, (width, height))
```

## Interpolation — the critical detail

When the inverse mapping lands on a non-integer input location, we must **interpolate** to estimate the pixel value. The choice of interpolation method dramatically affects image quality.

### Nearest-neighbor interpolation

Round to the nearest integer coordinate and use that pixel's value.

- **Pros:** fastest; preserves exact pixel values (no averaging).
- **Cons:** produces blocky, jagged artifacts, especially when upscaling.
- **Use:** when speed matters more than quality, or when you must preserve exact values (e.g., label masks in segmentation).

### Bilinear interpolation

Linearly interpolate in $x$ then in $y$ (equivalently, fit a plane through the 4 nearest pixels):

$$\text{value}(x, y) = \sum_{i=0}^{1} \sum_{j=0}^{1} \text{value}(\lfloor x \rfloor + i, \lfloor y \rfloor + j) \cdot (1 - |x - \lfloor x \rfloor - i|) \cdot (1 - |y - \lfloor y \rfloor - j|)$$

- **Pros:** smooth, fast, good quality. The default for most image resizing.
- **Cons:** can blur sharp edges slightly.
- **Use:** the standard choice for resizing and geometric transforms.

### Bicubic interpolation

Fit a cubic polynomial through the 16 nearest pixels (4×4 neighborhood). The cubic interpolation kernel (often the Catmull-Rom spline) produces smoother results than bilinear.

- **Pros:** smoother than bilinear, preserves edges better, less blurring.
- **Cons:** slower (16 pixels vs 4); can introduce slight "ringing" overshoot near sharp edges.
- **Use:** high-quality resizing (e.g., Photoshop's "bicubic" option), scientific imaging.

### Lanczos interpolation

Use a sinc-function-based kernel over a larger neighborhood (typically 6×6 or 8×8). The theoretically optimal resampling filter for band-limited signals.

- **Pros:** sharpest results, minimal aliasing.
- **Cons:** slowest; can produce ringing artifacts.
- **Use:** high-end image processing, photography, video scaling.

### Comparison

| Method | Neighborhood | Speed | Quality | Aliasing | Use case |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Nearest | 1×1 | Fastest | Poorest | Severe | Label masks, speed-critical |
| Bilinear | 2×2 | Fast | Good | Moderate | Default for most tasks |
| Bicubic | 4×4 | Medium | Better | Low | High-quality resizing |
| Lanczos | 6×6 / 8×8 | Slow | Best | Minimal | Professional photography |

**Downsampling warning:** when shrinking an image, you are removing high frequencies that the smaller image cannot represent. If you do not **low-pass filter** before downsampling, those high frequencies **alias** — they fold back as spurious low-frequency patterns (moiré, jaggies). Bilinear and bicubic handle this somewhat (they average over a neighborhood), but for large downsampling ratios, you should explicitly blur first (or use an area-based resampling method like OpenCV's `INTER_AREA`).

## Practical applications

### Data augmentation

Random rotation, scaling, and cropping are standard augmentations for training CNNs (see [[07. Data Augmentation]]). The CNN should be **invariant** to these transforms — a cat is a cat whether it is rotated 15° or scaled 1.2×. By augmenting the training data with random geometric transforms, the CNN learns this invariance.

### Image registration

Align two images of the same scene taken from different positions or at different times. Find corresponding points (e.g., via SIFT — see [[3.8 Feature Descriptors - SIFT, SURF, ORB, HOG]]), compute the affine or perspective transform between them, and warp one image to match the other.

### Document rectification

Photograph a document at an angle, detect the 4 corners, compute the perspective transform to a rectangle, and warp. This is how document-scanning apps (CamScanner, Adobe Scan) work.

### Panorama stitching

Take multiple overlapping photos, find matching feature points between adjacent photos, compute the perspective transforms, and warp all photos into a common coordinate frame. Blend the overlaps. This is how panorama modes in phone cameras work.

## What you should walk away with

1. Geometric transforms map input coordinates to output coordinates; we use **inverse mapping** (for each output pixel, find the input location and sample).
2. **Translation, scaling, rotation** are affine transforms (preserve parallel lines); **perspective** transforms (preserve straight lines but not parallelism) are more general.
3. Affine is determined by 3 point correspondences; perspective by 4. Both use homogeneous coordinates.
4. **Interpolation** estimates pixel values at non-integer locations: nearest (blocky), bilinear (smooth, default), bicubic (smoother), Lanczos (sharpest).
5. **Downsampling** requires low-pass filtering first to avoid aliasing.
6. Applications: data augmentation, image registration, document rectification, panorama stitching.
