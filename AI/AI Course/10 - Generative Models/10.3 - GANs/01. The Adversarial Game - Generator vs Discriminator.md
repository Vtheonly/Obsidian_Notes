## The core idea

A **Generative Adversarial Network (GAN)** (Goodfellow et al. 2014) frames generation as a **two-player game** between two neural networks:

- The **generator** $G$ takes random noise $z \sim p_z$ and produces a fake sample $G(z)$. Its goal: produce samples so realistic that the discriminator cannot distinguish them from real.
- The **discriminator** $D$ takes a sample (real or fake) and outputs the probability that it is real. Its goal: correctly distinguish real from fake.

They are trained simultaneously, in adversarial fashion:

- The generator is trained to **fool** the discriminator (maximize the probability that $D$ classifies $G(z)$ as real).
- The discriminator is trained to **catch** the generator (correctly classify real as real and fake as fake).

At equilibrium (if training converges), the generator produces samples indistinguishable from real, and the discriminator is at 50% accuracy (random guessing). At this point, the generator has learned the data distribution.

---

## The minimax objective

Formally, the GAN objective is:

$$\min_G \max_D V(D, G) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p_{data}}[\log D(x)] + \mathbb{E}_{z \sim p_z}[\log(1 - D(G(z)))]$$

Reading this carefully:

- $D(x)$ is the discriminator's probability that real sample $x$ is real. The first term rewards $D$ for classifying real samples correctly ($D(x) \to 1$).
- $D(G(z))$ is the discriminator's probability that fake sample $G(z)$ is real. The second term rewards $D$ for classifying fake samples as fake ($D(G(z)) \to 0$, so $1 - D(G(z)) \to 1$, so $\log(1 - D(G(z))) \to 0$).
- The $\max_D$ means the discriminator is trained to maximize this (correctly distinguish real from fake).
- The $\min_G$ means the generator is trained to minimize this (fool the discriminator — make $D(G(z)) \to 1$, which makes $\log(1 - D(G(z))) \to -\infty$).

The two networks are trained alternately: update $D$ a few times, then update $G$ a few times, repeat. This is the GAN training loop.

---

## Why this works — the equilibrium

At the theoretical equilibrium, the generator's distribution $p_g$ matches the data distribution $p_{data}$, and the discriminator outputs 0.5 (cannot distinguish). At this point:

- $D(x) = 0.5$ for all $x$ (real or fake).
- $p_g = p_{data}$ (the generator produces samples from the true distribution).

This is the **Nash equilibrium** of the adversarial game. The generator has learned to produce samples indistinguishable from real — it has learned the data distribution.

In practice, this equilibrium is hard to reach (see [[02. Training Dynamics and Mode Collapse]]), but when training works, GANs produce samples of stunning quality — sharp, realistic images that VAEs cannot match.

---

## Why GANs produce sharper samples than VAEs

The VAE's KL regularization forces the latent space to be a broad Gaussian, and the decoder must handle a range of latents per input — producing blurry "average" samples. The GAN has no such regularization. The generator's only constraint is to fool the discriminator, which judges **realism**, not reconstruction. So the generator can produce sharp, specific samples without averaging.

Concretely: a VAE decoder trained on faces must produce a face for *any* latent in the Gaussian — including latents between training examples, where it has no data. It produces an "average face" for those latents, which is blurry. A GAN generator only needs to produce *some* realistic face for each noise input — it does not need to cover the entire latent space uniformly, so it can afford to produce sharp, specific faces.

This is the key advantage of the adversarial loss over the reconstruction+KL loss: it optimizes for **realism** directly, rather than for reconstruction of a specific input.

## The architecture

A standard DCGAN (Deep Convolutional GAN, Radford et al. 2015):

**Generator:**
- Input: noise vector $z \in \mathbb{R}^{100}$, sampled from $\mathcal{N}(0, I)$.
- Architecture: series of **transposed convolutions** (see [[advanced CNN operators]]) that progressively upsample: $100 \to 4 \times 4 \times 512 \to 8 \times 8 \times 256 \to 16 \times 16 \times 128 \to 32 \times 32 \times 64 \to 64 \times 64 \times 3$.
- Each layer followed by BatchNorm and ReLU (except the last, which uses Tanh to produce pixel values in $[-1, 1]$).

**Discriminator:**
- Input: image (real or fake), $64 \times 64 \times 3$.
- Architecture: mirror of the generator — series of strided convolutions that downsample: $64 \times 64 \times 3 \to 32 \times 32 \times 64 \to \ldots \to 4 \times 4 \times 512 \to 1$.
- Each layer followed by LeakyReLU (not ReLU — LeakyReLU prevents dying neurons in the discriminator).
- Output: a single scalar (the probability that the input is real), via Sigmoid.

The generator uses transposed convolutions to upsample; the discriminator uses regular convolutions to classify. Both are standard CNNs, just trained against each other rather than against a fixed label.

---

## Conditional GANs

The basic GAN generates random samples — you cannot control what it generates. A **conditional GAN (cGAN)** (Mirza and Osindero, 2014) conditions both the generator and the discriminator on a label $y$:

- Generator: $G(z, y)$ — takes both noise and a label.
- Discriminator: $D(x, y)$ — takes both a sample and a label.

This lets you generate samples of a specific class: feed noise $z$ and label $y = \text{"cat"}$, and the generator produces a cat. Conditional GANs are the foundation of controllable generation — pix2pix (image-to-image translation), CycleGAN (unpaired style transfer), and many modern controlled-generation systems.

---

## What you should walk away with

1. A GAN is a two-player game: a generator (produces fakes) and a discriminator (catches fakes), trained adversarially.
2. The minimax objective: the discriminator maximizes correct classification, the generator minimizes it (fools the discriminator).
3. At equilibrium, the generator produces samples indistinguishable from real — it has learned the data distribution.
4. GANs produce sharper samples than VAEs because the adversarial loss optimizes realism directly, without the blurring-causing KL regularization.
5. The architecture: generator uses transposed convolutions to upsample; discriminator uses regular convolutions to classify.
6. Conditional GANs add a label input to both networks, enabling controlled generation.
