# 01. From Autoencoder to VAE — Learning a Distribution

## The problem: autoencoders cannot generate

Recall from [[01. The Autoencoder - Compression as Learning]]: a trained autoencoder can reconstruct its input, but its latent space is an irregular point cloud. Sampling a random latent vector and decoding it produces garbage, because most of the latent space is "empty" — no training example mapped there.

The **Variational Autoencoder (VAE)** (Kingma and Welling, 2013) fixes this by forcing the latent space to be a **known distribution** — specifically, a standard Gaussian $\mathcal{N}(0, I)$. Once the latent space is Gaussian, you can sample $z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$ and decode it into a valid new example. The VAE is a **generative** model — it can produce new examples, not just reconstruct old ones.

---

## The key change: the encoder outputs a distribution, not a point

In a vanilla autoencoder, the encoder outputs a single point $z = f_\phi(x)$. In a VAE, the encoder outputs the **parameters of a distribution** — specifically, the mean $\mu$ and log-variance $\log \sigma^2$ of a Gaussian:

$$\mu = f_\phi^\mu(x), \quad \log \sigma^2 = f_\phi^\sigma(x)$$

The latent $z$ is then **sampled** from this distribution:

$$z \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2 I)$$

This is the core idea: instead of mapping each input to a fixed point in latent space, the VAE maps each input to a **distribution** over latent space. The decoder then decodes a sample from this distribution.

Why? Because forcing each input to a *distribution* rather than a *point* smooths the latent space. The distributions for nearby inputs overlap, creating a continuous latent manifold. Sampling from the standard Gaussian $\mathcal{N}(0, I)$ lands in regions covered by some input's distribution, so the decoder produces valid examples.

---

## The two-part loss

The VAE loss has two terms:

$$\mathcal{L}_{VAE} = \mathcal{L}_{recon} + \mathcal{L}_{KL}$$

### Reconstruction loss

Same as a vanilla autoencoder — measure how well the decoder reconstructs the input from the sampled $z$:

$$\mathcal{L}_{recon} = \|x - g_\theta(z)\|^2 \quad \text{(or BCE, depending on data type)}$$

This term encourages the encoder to preserve input information in $z$ and the decoder to reconstruct from $z$.

### KL divergence loss

This is the new term. It measures how far the encoder's distribution $\mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$ is from the prior $\mathcal{N}(0, I)$:

$$\mathcal{L}_{KL} = D_{KL}\left(\mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2) \| \mathcal{N}(0, I)\right) = \frac{1}{2} \sum_{i=1}^k \left(\mu_i^2 + \sigma_i^2 - \log \sigma_i^2 - 1\right)$$

This term pushes the encoder's distributions toward the standard Gaussian — small means, unit variances. Without it, the encoder could output arbitrary distributions far from $\mathcal{N}(0, I)$, and sampling from $\mathcal{N}(0, I)$ would still produce garbage.

### The tradeoff

The two terms are in tension:

- **Reconstruction** wants the encoder to preserve as much input information as possible — which means narrow distributions (small $\sigma$) centered at the best encoding of the input. This is what a vanilla autoencoder does.
- **KL** wants the encoder's distributions to match the prior — broad ($\sigma \approx 1$), centered at zero ($\mu \approx 0$). This loses input information but makes the latent space nicely distributed for sampling.

The VAE finds a balance: distributions that are informative enough to reconstruct (small-ish $\sigma$) but close enough to the prior to enable sampling (not too far from $\mathcal{N}(0, I)$). The balance is controlled by the relative weight of the two terms — a hyperparameter often called the **$\beta$** coefficient (in $\beta$-VAE, a weighted variant).

## Why this works — the probabilistic interpretation

The VAE is not just an autoencoder with a regularization term — it is a **probabilistic model** with a principled foundation. The model defines a joint distribution:

$$p(x, z) = p(z) \cdot p_\theta(x | z)$$

where $p(z) = \mathcal{N}(0, I)$ is the prior and $p_\theta(x|z)$ is the decoder (the likelihood of $x$ given latent $z$). The encoder $q_\phi(z|x)$ is an **approximate posterior** — an approximation to the true posterior $p(z|x)$, which is intractable.

The VAE loss is the **negative evidence lower bound (ELBO)**:

$$\log p(x) \geq \mathbb{E}_{z \sim q_\phi(z|x)}[\log p_\theta(x|z)] - D_{KL}(q_\phi(z|x) \| p(z))$$

The first term is the reconstruction loss (expected log-likelihood); the second is the KL divergence. Maximizing the ELBO maximizes a lower bound on the data likelihood $\log p(x)$, which is the proper objective for a generative model.

This probabilistic framing is what makes the VAE a **generative model** rather than just a regularized autoencoder. The autoencoder is a deterministic compressor; the VAE is a probabilistic model of the data distribution, trained by maximizing a lower bound on the likelihood.

## The sampling problem — why we need the reparameterization trick

There is a technical problem: the latent $z$ is **sampled** from $\mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$. Sampling is a stochastic operation — you cannot backpropagate through it. But we need to backpropagate through $z$ to train the encoder (which produces $\mu$ and $\sigma$).

This is the **reparameterization problem**, and its solution — the **reparameterization trick** — is the technical innovation that made VAEs trainable. We cover it in the next note.

---

## What you should walk away with

1. A VAE's encoder outputs the parameters ($\mu$, $\sigma$) of a Gaussian distribution, not a point. The latent $z$ is sampled from this distribution.
2. The loss has two terms: reconstruction (preserve input information) and KL divergence (push the latent distribution toward the prior $\mathcal{N}(0, I)$).
3. The two terms are in tension: reconstruction wants narrow, informative distributions; KL wants broad, prior-like distributions.
4. The VAE is a probabilistic model trained to maximize the ELBO — a lower bound on the data likelihood. This is what makes it a generative model.
5. The technical challenge is backpropagating through the sampling step — solved by the reparameterization trick (next note).
