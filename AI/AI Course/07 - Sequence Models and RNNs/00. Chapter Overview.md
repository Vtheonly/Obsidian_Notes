## Why this chapter exists

Everything you have built so far — MLPs, CNNs — treats every input as an **independent, fixed-shape object**. A CNN looks at one image and emits one label. An MLP looks at one feature vector and emits one prediction. There is no notion of *order*, no notion of *history*, no notion that the meaning of an input depends on what came before it.

But a huge fraction of real-world data is **sequential**:

- A sentence is a sequence of words whose meaning depends on order ("the dog bit the man" vs "the man bit the dog").
- A time series is a sequence of measurements whose future values depend on past values.
- A spoken word is a sequence of audio frames.
- A stock price is a sequence of ticks.
- A protein is a sequence of amino acids whose 3D shape depends on the order.

A plain MLP cannot handle this gracefully. If you feed it a fixed-size window of the past, you must choose the window size in advance, and the model has no way to *remember* important events from further back. If you feed it a variable-length sequence by flattening it, you destroy the order information and you cannot handle sequences of different lengths.

**Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs)** were invented to fix this. The defining idea is breathtakingly simple: **the network has a hidden state that is updated at every time step, so the same weights are reused across time and the past persists into the present.** This single idea — weight sharing across time — is the conceptual bridge between CNNs (which share weights across space) and Transformers (which share weights across positions).

This chapter builds that idea carefully, explains why vanilla RNNs fail in practice, how LSTMs and GRUs patch the failure, and how the Encoder–Decoder pattern turned RNNs into translation machines — and then hit a wall that only attention could break.

## How to read this chapter

The chapter is sequential by construction — each note assumes the previous one. But the **intuition** is more important than the math. If you understand *why* a vanilla RNN forgets, *why* an LSTM remembers, and *why* a single fixed-size context vector becomes a bottleneck, you understand 90% of what you need to appreciate the Transformer. The remaining 10% is just notation.

The last note — *From RNNs to Attention* — is the door into [[08 - Attention and Transformers]]. Do not skip it.
