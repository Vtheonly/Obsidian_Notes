# 2.4 Information Theory Basics

## Why Information Theory Matters

Information theory provides the mathematical foundation for understanding what "learning" means in a machine learning context. When we train a model, we are essentially trying to extract information from data and compress it into a set of parameters. The loss function, whether MSE, cross-entropy, or heteroscedastic, is an information-theoretic measure of how much "surprise" remains after the model has made its prediction.

## Entropy: Measuring Uncertainty

**Entropy** measures the average amount of "surprise" or uncertainty in a random variable. For a continuous (Gaussian) random variable, entropy depends only on its variance. A distribution with large variance has high entropy (high uncertainty). A distribution with small variance has low entropy (low uncertainty).

When the LSTM in this project outputs `log_var = s`, it is specifying the entropy of its predictive distribution. A large s means high entropy (the model is uncertain). A small s means low entropy (the model is confident).

## The Heteroscedastic Loss as Negative Log-Likelihood

The heteroscedastic loss used in the solar model is exactly the negative log-likelihood of a Gaussian distribution N(y_hat, exp(s)). In information-theoretic terms, training this model minimizes the cross-entropy between the true distribution of residuals and the model's predicted distribution. The model learns to allocate its "information budget" efficiently: spending more bits (wider intervals) on uncertain predictions and fewer bits (narrower intervals) on confident ones.

## Mutual Information and Feature Relevance

**Mutual information** measures how much knowing one variable reduces uncertainty about another. In the feature engineering step, we are effectively trying to maximize the mutual information between our engineered features and the target variable. For example: `hour_sin` and `hour_cos` have high mutual information with solar generation; `wind_cubed` has higher mutual information with wind power than `wind_speed` alone; `is_real` has mutual information with the error distribution.

## The Information Bottleneck Principle

The **information bottleneck** principle states that a good representation should compress X as much as possible while preserving as much information about Y as possible. This explains several design choices in the project: the LSTM hidden dimension (64) constrains how much temporal information can be stored; the `is_real` feature is a single bit that carries crucial information; and the physics features pre-compute nonlinear relationships, allowing the model to use its limited capacity for residual patterns.

## The Bias-Variance Tradeoff from an Information-Theoretic View

From an information-theoretic perspective: **Underfitting** means the representation Z discards too much relevant information; **Overfitting** means the representation Z retains too much irrelevant information about the training data. Regularization techniques (Dropout, weight decay, early stopping) all serve to restrict the information that the model can memorize.

## Key Takeaways

- Entropy quantifies uncertainty; for a Gaussian, it depends only on variance
- The heteroscedastic loss is the negative log-likelihood of a Gaussian, equivalent to minimizing cross-entropy
- Mutual information justifies feature engineering: we want features that maximize I(features; target)
- The information bottleneck principle explains model capacity choices
- Overfitting means memorizing irrelevant information; regularization prevents this

> **Important Reminder**: When a model outputs a very confident prediction (tiny sigma) that is wrong, it is making a strong information-theoretic claim: "I am almost certain the answer is X." Being confidently wrong is worse than being uncertain. The heteroscedastic loss penalizes this by increasing the loss when confidence is high but the error is large.
