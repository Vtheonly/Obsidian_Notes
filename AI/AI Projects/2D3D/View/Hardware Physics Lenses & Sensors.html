The Light Cone

Plain English Explanation

Why can't we use a simple pinhole camera for everything? Because the hole has to be extremely tiny to make a sharp image. But a tiny hole blocks almost all light! The picture would be pitch black.

The Physical Geometry

A single physical point in 3D space emits rays of light in every direction. A lens acts as a collector, catching a wide cone of these scattered rays and bending them back to meet at a single focal spot on the sensor plane.

The Thin Lens Physics Model

How focal distance, object placement, and sensor alignment dictate focus

To capture sharp details without reducing brightness, glass lenses utilize refraction (bending light waves as they pass through different glass thicknesses). This physical property is modeled using the Thin Lens Equation:

$$\frac{1}{f} = \frac{1}{d_o} + \frac{1}{d_i}$$
$f$ Focal Length of the Lens (Physical curvature property)
$d_o$ Object Distance (How far the object is from lens)
$d_i$ Image Distance (Where rays perfectly converge)

Focusing Demystified

Notice that for any fixed focal length $f$, moving an object closer (decreasing $d_o$) forces the required image plane distance $d_i$ further back. To keep objects sharp at different distances, physical cameras must mechanically slide the physical lens elements backward and forward relative to the static silicon sensor.

Aperture and Depth of Field

A larger physical aperture (small f-number like $f/1.4$) creates a steeper cone of converging light rays. Because the cone is steep, if the sensor plane is even slightly misaligned, the rays spread out over a wide circle, creating a large, blurry footprint called the Circle of Confusion (bokeh).

Silicon Sensor Discretization

Transforming physical continuous rays into digital pixel values

Once the glass lens projects a sharp image, it strikes the image sensor. The sensor is not an infinitely detailed canvas; it is a grid of individual light-measuring chambers (pixels):

$$\text{Digital Pixel Coordinate} = \left( \text{round}\left(\frac{x}{s_x}\right) + c_x, \; \text{round}\left(\frac{y}{s_y}\right) + c_y \right)$$

$s_x, s_y$: Pixel pitch (the actual micro-sized width/height of a single pixel on the silicon chip, often measured in microns like $1.4\ \mu\text{m}$).

round(): The sampling process. This conversion chops up smooth details, throwing away sub-pixel details.

Crop Factors: Sensors Sizes Explained

A Full Frame sensor ($36 \times 24\text{ mm}$) captures the entire circular projection of a lens. A smaller APS-C or mobile sensor acts like an physical scissor, trimming the edges of the projected circle. Because it only records the middle portion of the scene, it creates a narrow field of view, making the image look digitally zoomed-in (cropped).
