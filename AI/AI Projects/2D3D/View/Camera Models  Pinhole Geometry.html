Meet the 3D Point

Plain English Breakdown

We place a physical object in front of our camera. In computer vision, we call this the **World Coordinate Point** and represent it using three basic numbers: width, height, and depth.

How the Math views this

Let's define the object's physical 3D coordinate as:
$P = (X, Y, Z)$
Where $X$ represents horizontal position, $Y$ vertical height, and $Z$ depth (how far away the object is straight in front of our camera lens).

The Mathematical Projection Equations

From similar triangles to standard camera intrinsics

By looking at our visualizer from the top, the ratio of height-to-distance remains constant across matching triangles. This geometry results in two elegant, fundamental equations:

$$x = f \frac{X}{Z} \quad \text{and} \quad y = f \frac{Y}{Z}$$
$f$ Focal Length (Sensor Distance)
$Z$ Physical Depth (Divisor)
$X, Y$ Physical Width & Height

Perspective Foreshortening (The Depth Shrink Effect)

Notice how the physical depth coordinate $Z$ sits cleanly in the denominator of the division. As $Z$ grows larger (the object travels far away), the overall pixel coordinate $(x, y)$ becomes smaller. This division is what naturally generates perspective foreshortening in photos!

Scale Ambiguity (The Depth Illusion)

Because we divide by $Z$, we lose absolute scale. An object with a height of $2\text{m}$ at a depth of $10\text{m}$ yields $\frac{2}{10} = 0.2$. An object of $4\text{m}$ at a depth of $20\text{m}$ also yields $\frac{4}{20} = 0.2$. On a single photo's flat pixels, these two completely different objects look identical!

The Intrinsic Matrix $K$

How pixel offsets and hardware scaling are represented

In digital systems, sensors don't measure in physical millimeters; they measure in pixels. Also, pixel coordinates start from the top-left corner $(0,0)$ of your screen, not the center of the camera center. To map millimeters to pixels, we use the Intrinsic Matrix, traditionally represented as $K$:

$$K = \begin{bmatrix} f_x & 0 & c_x \\ 0 & f_y & c_y \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

$f_x, f_y$: Focal lengths converted and scaled into horizontal & vertical pixel dimensions.

$c_x, c_y$: The Principal Point. This shifts $(0,0)$ from the sensor's center to the image top-left pixel corner.

See how real physical glass lenses bend and warp straight light rays

Distortion Parameters

Radial Coeff ($k_1$) 0.0

Positive values create barrel distortion; negative creates pincushion.

Radial Coeff ($k_2$) 0.0

Fine-tunes warping further away from lens center.

Tangential Coeff ($p_1$) 0.0

Simulates physical lens tilt/misalignment horizontally.

Why this matters: In professional pipelines (like COLMAP or OpenCV photogrammetry), images must be mathematically "undistorted" before calculations. If ignored, camera projection lines will completely miss each other, rendering 3D reconstruction impossible.
