# 3. Tactical Claims

> **A tactical claim in one sentence**: A claim that the position contains a specific tactical pattern — fork, pin, skewer, discovered attack, double check, or zugzwang — that the verifier must reconstruct from the board state.

Tactical claims are harder than move descriptions and captures because they require the verifier to **recognize a pattern**, not just look up a move. They are also where the verifier's symbolic layer earns its keep: an LLM alone is terrible at detecting pins and skewers.

---

## 3.1 The Pattern Catalog

The verifier supports the following tactical patterns in v2 (v1 handles only forks and pins):

| Pattern | Definition | Required board facts |
|---|---|---|
| **Fork** | One piece attacks two or more valuable pieces simultaneously. | Attack map. |
| **Pin** | A piece is pinned to a more valuable piece (often the king) along a line. | Attack map + line geometry. |
| **Skewer** | A valuable piece is attacked and must move, exposing a less valuable piece behind it. | Attack map + line geometry. |
| **Discovered attack** | Moving one piece reveals an attack from another piece. | Compare attack maps before/after a move. |
| **Double check** | The king is attacked by two pieces simultaneously. | Attack map on the king's square. |
| **Zugzwang** | Any move weakens the position; the player would prefer to pass. | Engine eval + move analysis. |

For each pattern, the verifier has a dedicated handler that reconstructs the pattern from the facts database and emits a verdict.

---

## 3.2 The Claim Schema (Generic)

Tactical claims share a common schema:

```json
{
  "claim_type": "tactical_fork | tactical_pin | tactical_skewer | ...",
  "params": {
    "attacker_color": "white | black | null",
    "attacking_piece": "knight | bishop | rook | queen | pawn | null",
    "attacking_square": "e5 | null",
    "target_color": "white | black | null",
    "target_pieces": ["king", "queen"] | null,
    "target_squares": ["e8", "d8"] | null
  },
  "target_ply_hint": null
}
```

The pattern type determines which fields are required:

- **Fork**: `attacking_piece` and `target_pieces` (at least two).
- **Pin**: `attacking_piece`, `target_pieces[0]` (the pinned piece), `target_pieces[1]` (the piece it is pinned to, usually the king).
- **Skewer**: `attacking_piece`, `target_pieces[0]` (the front piece), `target_pieces[1]` (the back piece).
- **Discovered attack**: `attacking_piece` (the moving piece) and `discovered_attacker_piece` (the piece whose attack is revealed).
- **Double check**: just `attacker_color` and `target_color` (must be opposite).

## 3.3 Fork Verification

### 3.3.1 Definition

A fork exists when one piece attacks two or more enemy pieces simultaneously, where at least two of the attacked pieces are "valuable" (king, queen, rook, or sometimes knight/bishop). Pawns attacking two pieces is also a fork (the "royal fork" is a knight attacking king and queen).

### 3.3.2 Algorithm

```text
1. Identify the attacker: claim.attacking_piece and claim.attacking_square.
   If null, scan all of attacker_color's pieces.
2. For each candidate attacker, look up attack_map_after[attacker_square].
3. Count the number of enemy valuable pieces in the attack set.
4. If >= 2 valuable pieces are attacked: fork confirmed.
5. Compare to claim.target_pieces (if specified): all claim.target_pieces
   must be in the attacked set.
```

### 3.3.3 Worked example

**Claim**: `tactical_fork` with `attacking_piece: "knight", attacking_square: "e5", target_pieces: ["king", "queen"]`
**Facts**: At ply 14 (after `Ne5`), the knight on e5 attacks the black king on g8 and the black queen on d7.

Wait — knights on e5 attack g8 (yes, that's a knight move) and d7 (also a knight move). Confirmed.

**Verdict**: Verified. "At ply 14, the white knight on e5 forks the black king on g8 and the black queen on d7."

### 3.3.4 Common LLM mistakes

- **Calling any double attack a fork.** A pin or skewer is also a "double attack" but is not a fork. The LLM sometimes labels pins as forks. The verifier's verdict should reflect the actual pattern: "This is a pin, not a fork — see pin verification for details."
- **Inventing the attacker's square.** "The knight forks king and queen" without saying where the knight is. The verifier must search all knights of the attacker's color.
- **Counting pawns as forked pieces.** A knight attacking two pawns is technically a double attack but is rarely called a fork. The verifier should require at least two valuable pieces for a `Verified` verdict; otherwise, `Supported (soft)`.

## 3.4 Pin Verification

### 3.4.1 Definition

A pin exists when a piece cannot move without exposing a more valuable piece behind it to attack. The most common pin is "absolute pin" — a piece pinned to the king, which cannot legally move out of the line.

### 3.4.2 Algorithm

```text
1. Identify the attacker (the pinner) and the pinned piece.
2. Check that all three (pinner, pinned, back piece) lie on a line:
   - same rank (rook pin)
   - same file (rook pin)
   - same diagonal (bishop pin)
   - same diagonal/rank/file (queen pin)
3. Check that there is exactly one piece between pinner and back piece
   (the pinned piece).
4. Check that the back piece is more valuable than the pinned piece
   (king > queen > rook > bishop/knight > pawn).
5. If the back piece is the king, this is an absolute pin (the pinned
   piece cannot move legally). Otherwise, it is a relative pin.
```

python-chess has built-in pin detection: `board.pin(color, square)` returns the ray of squares forming the pin. Use it.

### 3.4.3 Worked example

**Claim**: `tactical_pin` with `attacking_piece: "bishop", attacking_square: "g5", target_pieces: ["knight", "king"]`
**Facts**: At ply 18, the white bishop on g5 pins the black knight on f6 to the black king on e7.

Check:
- g5, f6, e7 are on the same diagonal? g5→f6 is one diagonal step; f6→e7 is one diagonal step. Yes, same diagonal.
- Is there exactly one piece between g5 and e7? Yes, the knight on f6.
- Is the back piece (king) more valuable than the pinned piece (knight)? Yes.
- Is the back piece the king? Yes — absolute pin.

**Verdict**: Verified. "At ply 18, the white bishop on g5 absolutely pins the black knight on f6 to the black king on e7."

### 3.4.4 Common mistakes

- **Calling any attack along a line a pin.** A rook attacking a queen along a rank is just an attack, not a pin, unless there is a piece between them that cannot move.
- **Confusing absolute and relative pins.** Absolute pins (to the king) restrict legal moves; relative pins (to a queen, say) do not restrict legal moves but make moving tactically bad. The verifier should distinguish these in the explanation.
- **Missing the "more valuable" requirement.** A rook pinned to a pawn is not really a pin (the rook is more valuable than the pawn behind it). Some authors call this a "reverse pin" but it is not standard.

## 3.5 Skewer Verification

### 3.5.1 Definition

A skewer exists when a valuable piece is attacked and must move, exposing a less valuable piece behind it to capture. The skewer is the inverse of the pin: in a pin, the front piece cannot move; in a skewer, the front piece must move.

### 3.5.2 Algorithm

```text
1. Identify the attacker and the front piece (the attacked piece).
2. Check that all three (attacker, front, back) lie on a line.
3. Check that there is no piece between attacker and front (direct attack).
4. Check that the back piece is less valuable than the front piece.
5. Check that the front piece must move (e.g., it is the king, or
   moving it is the only legal response).
```

### 3.5.3 Worked example

**Claim**: `tactical_skewer` with `attacking_piece: "bishop", attacking_square: "b2", target_pieces: ["king", "rook"]`
**Facts**: At ply 22, the white bishop on b2 attacks the black king on g8. The king must move, exposing the black rook on h7 to capture.

Wait — b2 to g8 is the long diagonal. b2, c3, d4, e5, f6, g7, h8. The king is on g8, not on the diagonal. Let me reconsider. Actually, the king on g8 is not on the b2-h8 diagonal. The example needs adjustment.

Let me redo: bishop on b2, black king on g7, black rook on h8. b2-g7 is the diagonal (b2, c3, d4, e5, f6, g7 — yes). The king on g7 is attacked; it must move (e.g., to g8 or f8); then the rook on h8 is exposed.

**Verdict**: Verified. "At ply 22, the white bishop on b2 skewers the black king on g7 and the black rook on h8. The king must move, exposing the rook to capture."

### 3.5.4 Common mistakes

- **Confusing skewer with pin.** The verifier should always check: does the front piece move (skewer) or is it stuck (pin)? If the front piece is the king, it is a skewer (king must move out of check). If the back piece is the king, it is a pin.
- **Inventing the back piece.** Authors sometimes say "the bishop skewers the king" without specifying what is behind. The verifier should mark this as `Ambiguous` unless the engine identifies a clear back piece.

## 3.6 Discovered Attack Verification

### 3.6.1 Definition

A discovered attack occurs when moving one piece reveals an attack from another piece. The most powerful form is the discovered check, where the moved piece also gives check (double check) or where the unmoved piece gives check.

### 3.6.2 Algorithm

```text
1. Compare attack_map_before and attack_map_after for the moved piece's color.
2. Find squares that were not attacked before but are attacked after.
3. For each newly attacked square, check whether a valuable enemy piece is on it.
4. If yes: discovered attack confirmed.
5. If the newly attacked square is the enemy king: discovered check.
```

### 3.6.3 Worked example

**Claim**: `discovered_attack` with `attacking_piece: "knight"` (the moving piece), `discovered_attacker_piece: "rook"` (the piece whose attack is revealed)
**Facts**: At ply 16, White moves the knight from d1 to e3. The white rook on d3 was previously blocked by the knight; now it attacks the black queen on d8.

Check:
- attack_map_before (ply 15): black queen on d8 was not attacked by white rook on d3 (knight on d1 blocked).
- attack_map_after (ply 16): black queen on d8 is attacked by white rook on d3.
- Conclusion: discovered attack.

**Verdict**: Verified. "At ply 16, the white knight's move from d1 to e3 reveals a discovered attack by the white rook on d3 against the black queen on d8."

### 3.6.4 Common mistakes

- **Confusing discovered attack with double attack.** A double attack is one piece attacking two things; a discovered attack is two pieces, one moving and one revealing.
- **Missing the discovered check pattern.** If the revealed attack is on the enemy king, that is a discovered check, which is much stronger. The verifier should explicitly mention "discovered check" in the verdict.

## 3.7 Double Check Verification

### 3.7.1 Definition

Double check is a specific case where the enemy king is attacked by two pieces simultaneously. It is almost always a discovered check (one piece moves, giving check directly, while also revealing a check from another piece).

### 3.7.2 Algorithm

```text
1. Get attack_map_after for the mover's color.
2. Get the enemy king's square.
3. Count attackers on the king's square.
4. If exactly two attackers: double check confirmed.
5. The king MUST move (no other legal response in double check).
```

### 3.7.3 Common mistakes

- **Calling any check double check.** A single check is just a check. The verifier must count exactly two attackers.
- **Confusing with fork.** A fork attacks two pieces with one piece. A double check attacks one piece (the king) with two pieces.

---

## 3.8 Soft Tactics and the Confidence Score

For tactics that the verifier detects but with some uncertainty (e.g., a "fork" where the second piece is only a pawn), the verdict is `Supported (soft)` rather than `Verified`. The explanation should clarify the softness:

**Verdict**: Supported (soft). "At ply 14, the white knight on e5 attacks the black king on g8 and the black pawn on d7. This is technically a double attack but is rarely called a fork because the pawn is low-value."

The soft verdict is appropriate here because the claim is not strictly wrong — the knight does attack two pieces — but the standard chess usage of "fork" implies two valuable pieces.

---

## 3.9 Tips for Authors

- **Specify the attacker's square** when possible: "The knight on e5 forks king and queen" is unambiguous.
- **Specify the target pieces**: "forks the king and queen" rather than "forks two pieces".
- **Distinguish pin from skewer**: pin = front piece cannot move; skewer = front piece must move.
- **Mention absolute vs. relative pins**: "absolutely pinned to the king" vs. "pinned to the queen".
- **For discovered attacks, name both pieces**: "the knight's move reveals a rook attack on the queen".

---

## 3.10 Key Reminders

- Tactical claims require pattern recognition, not just move lookup. The attack map is the key data structure.
- Patterns: fork, pin, skewer, discovered attack, double check, zugzwang. v1 supports fork and pin only.
- Each pattern has its own handler with a specific algorithm.
- Fork: one piece attacks two valuable pieces simultaneously.
- Pin: a piece is stuck because moving it exposes a more valuable piece behind. Absolute pins (to king) restrict legal moves.
- Skewer: a valuable piece is attacked and must move, exposing a less valuable piece.
- Discovered attack: compare attack maps before and after a move to find newly revealed attacks.
- Double check: two attackers on the enemy king. King must move.
- Use python-chess's built-in `board.pin()` and `board.attackers()` rather than reimplementing.
- Common LLM mistakes: confusing fork with pin, calling any double attack a fork, inventing the attacker's square, missing the "more valuable" requirement for pins.
- Soft tactics (e.g., a fork of one valuable and one low-value piece) get `Supported (soft)` rather than `Verified`.
