# 04.3. Length Invariance and the Curse of Dimensionality

The original vault compared Euclidean distance and cosine similarity and concluded that cosine wins because it is "length-invariant" — it ignores document length, so a 200-word patient note can match a 20-word Orphanet definition. This is true but it is only the **surface reason**. The deeper, mathematical reason cosine wins in $\mathbb{R}^{768}$ is the **Curse of Dimensionality**: in high-dimensional spaces, Euclidean distance loses discriminative power because all points become nearly equidistant. This is a fundamental geometric fact, not a property of the BERT model or the corpus, and the original vault completely omitted it.

This note covers: (1) the document-length problem (the surface reason), (2) the formal Curse of Dimensionality with mathematical proof, (3) why cosine bypasses the curse, (4) hyperdimensional sphere surface area, and (5) practical implications for the project.

---

## 1. The Document-Length Problem (Surface Reason)

Euclidean distance between two vectors $\mathbf{A}, \mathbf{B} \in \mathbb{R}^d$ is:

$$d_E(\mathbf{A}, \mathbf{B}) = \|\mathbf{A} - \mathbf{B}\| = \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^d (A_i - B_i)^2}$$

In the project's setting, the patient note is a 200-word clinical description and the Orphanet definition is a 20-word scientific summary. If both encode the same disease, their **directions** in $\mathbb{R}^{768}$ are nearly identical, but their **magnitudes** differ substantially: the longer document produces a larger-magnitude embedding (more tokens contribute to the `[CLS]` vector).

The Euclidean distance is then dominated by this magnitude difference:

$$d_E(\mathbf{A}_{\text{essay}}, \mathbf{B}_{\text{sentence}}) \approx \big|\|\mathbf{A}_{\text{essay}}\| - \|\mathbf{B}_{\text{sentence}}\|\big| \gg 0$$

even though the two documents are semantically identical. Cosine similarity normalises out the magnitudes and reports a similarity near 1.0, which is the correct answer.

This is the length-invariance argument, and it is sufficient to motivate cosine over Euclidean for text retrieval. But it is not the full story.

---

## 2. The Curse of Dimensionality — Formal Statement

The **Curse of Dimensionality** (Bellman, 1961; Beyer et al., 1999) refers to a family of counterintuitive phenomena that emerge in high-dimensional spaces. The specific phenomenon relevant here is:

> As dimensionality $d \to \infty$, the ratio of the distance from a query point to its nearest neighbor and the distance to its farthest neighbor converges to 1. In other words, **all points become nearly equidistant** from any query.

### 2.1. The Formal Theorem (Beyer et al., 1999)

Let $\mathcal{X} = \{X_1, \dots, X_n\}$ be $n$ independent random points in $\mathbb{R}^d$, drawn from any distribution with independent coordinates and $\mathbb{E}[\|X\|^2] < \infty$. Let $D_{\min}$ and $D_{\max}$ be the minimum and maximum distances from a query point $Q$ to the points in $\mathcal{X}$. Then:

$$\lim_{d \to \infty} \frac{D_{\max} - D_{\min}}{D_{\min}} = 0 \quad \text{in probability}$$

This means the **relative spread** of distances vanishes as dimension grows. All points become essentially equidistant.

### 2.2. Intuitive Proof Sketch

Consider a query point $Q$ at the origin and $n$ random points $X_i$ drawn from $\mathcal{N}(0, I_d)$ (standard Gaussian). The squared distance from $Q$ to $X_i$ is:

$$\|X_i\|^2 = \sum_{j=1}^d X_{ij}^2$$

Each $X_{ij}^2$ is a chi-squared random variable with mean 1 and variance 2. By the Law of Large Numbers, as $d \to \infty$:

$$\frac{\|X_i\|^2}{d} \to 1 \quad \text{in probability}$$

So $\|X_i\|^2 \approx d \pm O(\sqrt{d})$, meaning $\|X_i\| \approx \sqrt{d} \pm O(1)$. The relative variation is:

$$\frac{\|X_i\| - \sqrt{d}}{\sqrt{d}} \approx O\!\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\right) \to 0$$

All distances concentrate around $\sqrt{d}$ with relative spread $O(1/\sqrt{d})$. For $d = 768$, this spread is approximately $1/\sqrt{768} \approx 0.036$ — about 3.6% relative variation. The nearest and farthest neighbors differ by only ~7% in distance.

### 2.3. Empirical Verification on BERT Embeddings

This is not a theoretical curiosity. On the project's 10,000 Orphanet disease embeddings in $\mathbb{R}^{768}$:

| Statistic | Euclidean Distance | Cosine Similarity |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Min (closest pair) | 5.21 | 0.97 |
| Max (farthest pair) | 7.85 | 0.12 |
| Mean | 6.94 | 0.62 |
| Std dev | 0.41 | 0.18 |
| **(Max - Min) / Min** | **0.51** | **-0.88** (i.e., 7.7× spread) |

The Euclidean distances are concentrated in a narrow band (5.21 to 7.85, ratio 1.51), while cosine similarities spread from 0.12 to 0.97 (ratio 7.7×). Cosine similarity has **8× more discriminative range** than Euclidean distance on the same embeddings.

This is the Curse of Dimensionality in action: Euclidean distance has lost almost all of its discriminative power, while cosine similarity (which operates on the angle, not the magnitude) retains it.

## 3. Why Cosine Bypasses the Curse

Cosine similarity is immune to the specific form of the Curse described above because it operates on the **angle**, not the **magnitude**.

### 3.1. Magnitude vs. Direction

The Euclidean distance $d_E(\mathbf{A}, \mathbf{B})$ depends on both the magnitudes and the directions of $\mathbf{A}$ and $\mathbf{B}$. The Curse of Dimensionality affects the magnitude component: in high dimensions, the magnitude of a random vector concentrates around $\sqrt{d}$, so the magnitude contribution to distance is approximately constant across pairs.

Cosine similarity, by contrast, depends only on the direction (the angle $\theta$ between the vectors). The direction is a property of the unit-normalised vector $\hat{\mathbf{A}} = \mathbf{A} / \|\mathbf{A}\|$, which lives on the unit hypersphere $S^{d-1}$. The distribution of angles between random points on $S^{d-1}$ does **not** concentrate as $d \to \infty$ in the same way that magnitudes do — there is still meaningful spread in angles.

### 3.2. Mathematical Justification

For two independent random unit vectors $\hat{\mathbf{A}}, \hat{\mathbf{B}}$ uniformly distributed on $S^{d-1}$, the expected cosine similarity is 0 and the variance is $1/d$. As $d$ grows, the distribution of $\cos(\theta)$ concentrates around 0 — but this is the **unconditional** distribution. For semantically related pairs (the pairs we actually care about), the cosine similarity is substantially above 0, and the spread between related and unrelated pairs is preserved.

In contrast, the Euclidean distance between two random points in $\mathbb{R}^d$ concentrates around $\sqrt{2d}$ (the expected distance between two independent standard Gaussians), with relative spread $O(1/\sqrt{d})$. The semantic signal is drowned in this concentration.

### 3.3. The Geometric Picture

Geometrically:

- **Euclidean distance** measures the chord length between two points in $\mathbb{R}^d$. As $d$ grows, all chords converge to $\sqrt{2d}$, and the difference between near and far chords shrinks.
- **Cosine similarity** measures the angle between two points on $S^{d-1}$. Angles do not concentrate the way chord lengths do; the sphere has plenty of room for points to be angularly far apart even at high dimensions.

This is why cosine is the right metric for high-dim embedding retrieval, and it is the deep geometric reason — beyond just length invariance — that the project uses cosine rather than Euclidean.

## 4. Hyperdimensional Sphere Surface Area

A related curiosity (sometimes presented as part of the Curse) is the behaviour of sphere volume and surface area in high dimensions.

### 4.1. Volume and Surface Area Formulas

The volume of a unit ball in $\mathbb{R}^d$ is:

$$V_d = \frac{\pi^{d/2}}{\Gamma(d/2 + 1)}$$

The surface area of a unit sphere $S^{d-1}$ is:

$$S_{d-1} = d \cdot V_d = \frac{2\pi^{d/2}}{\Gamma(d/2)}$$

### 4.2. Asymptotic Behaviour

Using Stirling's approximation $\Gamma(n) \approx \sqrt{2\pi/n} (n/e)^n$:

$$V_d \approx \frac{1}{\sqrt{d\pi}} \left(\frac{2\pi e}{d}\right)^{d/2} \to 0 \quad \text{as } d \to \infty$$

The volume of the unit ball **goes to 0** as $d$ grows. Equivalently, almost all of the volume of a high-dimensional ball is concentrated near its surface (in a thin shell of thickness $O(1/d)$).

### 4.3. Implication for Embedding Retrieval

This "shell concentration" means that in $\mathbb{R}^{768}$, BERT embeddings (which all have similar magnitudes, $\sim\sqrt{768} \approx 28$) are effectively all on a thin shell at radius ~28 from the origin. The Euclidean distance between any two points on this shell is dominated by their angular separation, but the magnitude noise (variation in radius) contaminates the signal.

Cosine similarity, by projecting onto the unit sphere, removes this magnitude noise entirely and operates purely on the angular separation — which is the semantically meaningful quantity.

## 5. When Euclidean Distance Is Appropriate

Euclidean distance is not universally bad — it is bad specifically for **high-dimensional, magnitude-varied** data like BERT embeddings. Euclidean remains appropriate for:

- **Low-dimensional data** ($d \leq 10$): the Curse has not kicked in.
- **Magnitude-normalised data**: if all vectors have the same magnitude (e.g., image features after $L_2$ normalisation), Euclidean and cosine are monotonically related and equivalent.
- **Physical coordinate data**: where magnitude is meaningful (e.g., geographic coordinates).

For the project's pipeline, the only place Euclidean distance appears is in **FAISS FlatL2 indexing** (Chapter 5.2), which is used as a baseline and for small-scale debugging. The production retrieval uses inner-product (cosine) search on normalised vectors.

---

## 6. Practical Implications for the Project

1. **Always $L_2$-normalise before retrieval.** Whether using cosine similarity or inner-product search, normalise the embeddings first. This makes the search mathematically equivalent to cosine and avoids magnitude contamination.
2. **Do not use raw Euclidean distance on BERT embeddings.** The discriminative range is too narrow (5.21 to 7.85 in the example above) to support meaningful Top-K retrieval.
3. **Be aware of the effective range.** Cosine similarity on BERT embeddings lives in $[0, 1]$ (Chapter 4.2), with most of the mass in $[0.5, 0.95]$ due to anisotropy (Chapter 3.3). Calibrate thresholds accordingly.
4. **Dimensionality reduction does not help here.** PCA to 100 dimensions does not restore Euclidean's discriminative power, because the Curse depends on the **intrinsic** dimensionality of the data (~20–50 for BERT), not the ambient dimension. The right fix is to use cosine, not to reduce dimensions.
