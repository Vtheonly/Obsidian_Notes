# 04.2. Rigorous Cosine Similarity Derivation

Cosine similarity is the mathematical heart of the project's Stage 2 retrieval. The original vault presented the formula $\cos(\theta) = (\mathbf{A} \cdot \mathbf{B}) / (\|\mathbf{A}\| \|\mathbf{B}\|)$ and walked through the dot product and magnitude computations, but it had two gaps that this note fills:

1. It never showed that **cosine similarity is equivalent to the dot product of $L_2$-normalised vectors** — the derivation that makes the connection to length-invariance rigorous.
2. It stated that the output is "always between -1.0 and 1.0" without noting that for BERT-family models with non-negative activations, the **effective range is $[0, 1]$**, never reaching the negative half.

This note covers: (1) the fundamental formula, (2) the equivalence to normalised dot product, (3) the non-negative activation bound, (4) why angle matters more than distance, and (5) the geometric interpretation on the unit hypersphere.

---

## 1. The Fundamental Formula

For two vectors $\mathbf{A}, \mathbf{B} \in \mathbb{R}^d$:

$$\text{Cosine Similarity} = \cos(\theta) = \frac{\mathbf{A} \cdot \mathbf{B}}{\|\mathbf{A}\| \|\mathbf{B}\|}$$

where:

- $\mathbf{A} \cdot \mathbf{B} = \sum_{i=1}^d A_i B_i$ is the dot product
- $\|\mathbf{A}\| = \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^d A_i^2}$ is the $L_2$ norm (magnitude)
- $\theta$ is the angle between $\mathbf{A}$ and $\mathbf{B}$ when placed tail-to-tail at the origin

### 1.1. Geometric Meaning

The dot product $\mathbf{A} \cdot \mathbf{B}$ is a measure of **alignment**: it is large and positive when the vectors point in similar directions, zero when they are orthogonal, and large and negative when they point in opposite directions. Dividing by the magnitudes normalises out the lengths, leaving only the angular component.

So $\cos(\theta) \in [-1, 1]$:

- $\cos(\theta) = 1$: vectors point in the same direction ($\theta = 0°$).
- $\cos(\theta) = 0$: vectors are orthogonal ($\theta = 90°$).
- $\cos(\theta) = -1$: vectors point in opposite directions ($\theta = 180°$).

---

## 2. Equivalence to Normalised Dot Product

The original vault never showed the algebraic identity that makes cosine similarity equivalent to a dot product on the unit sphere. Here is the derivation.

### 2.1. Define the Normalised Vectors

Let:

$$\hat{\mathbf{A}} = \frac{\mathbf{A}}{\|\mathbf{A}\|}, \qquad \hat{\mathbf{B}} = \frac{\mathbf{B}}{\|\mathbf{B}\|}$$

These are the **$L_2$-normalised** versions of $\mathbf{A}$ and $\mathbf{B}$ — they have unit length ($\|\hat{\mathbf{A}}\| = \|\hat{\mathbf{B}}\| = 1$) and point in the same direction as the originals.

### 2.2. The Dot Product of Normalised Vectors

$$\hat{\mathbf{A}} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{B}} = \frac{\mathbf{A}}{\|\mathbf{A}\|} \cdot \frac{\mathbf{B}}{\|\mathbf{B}\|} = \frac{\mathbf{A} \cdot \mathbf{B}}{\|\mathbf{A}\| \|\mathbf{B}\|} = \cos(\theta)$$

**This is the key identity**: cosine similarity is just the dot product, computed after normalising both vectors to unit length.

### 2.3. Practical Implication

In code, this means there are two equivalent ways to compute cosine similarity:

```python
# Method 1: Direct formula
cos_sim = np.dot(A, B) / (np.linalg.norm(A) * np.linalg.norm(B))

# Method 2: Normalise first, then dot product
A_hat = A / np.linalg.norm(A)
B_hat = B / np.linalg.norm(B)
cos_sim = np.dot(A_hat, B_hat)
```

Method 2 is preferred in production because:

- **Pre-computation.** If you have a database of $N$ disease vectors and will query them many times, normalise them once at index time. Each query is then just a dot product — no per-query magnitude computation.
- **Vectorised BLAS.** Dot products are highly optimised in BLAS libraries; the division is not.
- **FAISS compatibility.** FAISS indexes (Chapter 5.2) work with normalised vectors and use inner-product search, which is mathematically equivalent to cosine similarity on normalised vectors.

## 3. The Non-Negative Activation Bound — Why $[-1, 1]$ Is Often $[0, 1]$

The original vault stated that cosine similarity's output is "always between -1.0 and 1.0." This is true in linear algebra but is **frequently false in practice** for BERT-family models.

### 3.1. The Cause: Non-Negative Activations

BERT uses GELU activations in its feed-forward layers. GELU is approximately:

$$\text{GELU}(x) \approx \begin{cases} x & \text{if } x \gg 0 \\ 0 & \text{if } x \ll 0 \end{cases}$$

So GELU's output is **biased toward non-negative values**. After 12 layers of GELU-activated feed-forward transformations, the final hidden states (and the `[CLS]` vector) are predominantly non-negative: most coordinates $h_i \geq 0$.

### 3.2. The Mathematical Consequence

If two vectors $\mathbf{A}, \mathbf{B}$ have all non-negative coordinates ($A_i \geq 0$ and $B_i \geq 0$ for all $i$), then:

$$\mathbf{A} \cdot \mathbf{B} = \sum_{i=1}^d A_i B_i \geq 0$$

because each term $A_i B_i$ is the product of two non-negative numbers and is therefore non-negative. Since $\|\mathbf{A}\|, \|\mathbf{B}\| > 0$, the cosine similarity is:

$$\cos(\theta) = \frac{\mathbf{A} \cdot \mathbf{B}}{\|\mathbf{A}\| \|\mathbf{B}\|} \geq 0$$

**Cosine similarity of BERT embeddings is therefore bounded in $[0, 1]$, not $[-1, 1]$.** The negative half of the range is never reached in practice.

### 3.3. Why This Matters

This bound has three practical consequences:

1. **Calibration.** A cosine similarity of 0.5 does not mean "moderately similar" — it means "as unrelated as the geometry allows." Reviewers accustomed to interpreting cosine on the $[-1, 1]$ scale will misread BERT scores.
2. **Thresholding.** A threshold of 0.7 is much more permissive on the $[0, 1]$ scale than on the $[-1, 1]$ scale. The project's 0.9 threshold (Chapter 3.3) is near the top of the achievable range.
3. **Whitening restores the range.** BERT-Whitening (Chapter 3.3) centers the embeddings (subtracts the mean), which restores the ability to produce negative cosine similarities. After whitening, the range is closer to $[-1, 1]$ and scores are more interpretable.

### 3.4. Verification

You can verify this empirically on the project's BioBERT embeddings:

```python
# Compute cosine similarities of 10,000 random pairs
sims = [cosine(embeddings[i], embeddings[j]) for i, j in random_pairs]
print(f"Min: {min(sims):.3f}")  # Expect ~0.2, not -1.0
print(f"Max: {max(sims):.3f}")  # Expect ~0.99
print(f"Mean: {np.mean(sims):.3f}")  # Expect ~0.6-0.7 (anisotropy floor)
```

The minimum will be substantially above 0 if the embeddings are anisotropic (Chapter 3.3), and after whitening it should approach 0.

## 4. Why Angle Matters More Than Distance

The original vault's example was correct in spirit: a 500-word essay on Albinism and a 1-sentence definition of Albinism should be considered similar, even though their raw vectors have very different magnitudes. The mathematical reason is:

### 4.1. Document Length Affects Magnitude

If each token contributes a roughly equal-magnitude vector to the `[CLS]` representation (which is approximately true for mean-pooled sentence vectors), then:

$$\|\mathbf{A}_{\text{essay}}\| \approx \sqrt{N_{\text{essay}}} \cdot \bar{h}, \qquad \|\mathbf{A}_{\text{sentence}}\| \approx \sqrt{N_{\text{sentence}}} \cdot \bar{h}$$

where $N$ is the token count and $\bar{h}$ is the average per-token magnitude. For a 500-word essay vs. a 20-word sentence, the magnitude ratio is $\sqrt{500/20} \approx 5$. The Euclidean distance is dominated by this magnitude difference, even if the directions are identical.

### 4.2. Cosine Ignores Magnitude

Cosine similarity normalises out the magnitudes:

$$\cos(\theta) = \frac{\mathbf{A} \cdot \mathbf{B}}{\|\mathbf{A}\| \|\mathbf{B}\|}$$

The numerator scales as $N_{\text{essay}} \cdot N_{\text{sentence}} \cdot \bar{h}^2$ (assuming the average per-token contribution is similar), and the denominator scales as $\sqrt{N_{\text{essay}} N_{\text{sentence}}} \cdot \bar{h}^2$. The $N$ terms cancel, leaving only the directional alignment.

This is why cosine similarity is called **length-invariant**: the score does not depend on the document length, only on the semantic direction.

### 4.3. When Magnitude Does Matter

There are settings where magnitude carries information:

- **Sentence-level confidence.** A longer, more detailed clinical note may have a higher-magnitude embedding because it contains more diagnostic signal. In this case, magnitude correlates with "how much information is in the note."
- **Embedding norm as a quality signal.** Some studies find that BERT embeddings with very low norms correspond to out-of-distribution inputs (gibberish, non-text tokens).

For the project's retrieval task, we **do not** want magnitude to influence the score — a short patient note should be able to match a long Orphanet definition. Cosine similarity is therefore the correct choice.

## 5. Geometric Interpretation on the Unit Hypersphere

When we compute cosine similarity, we are effectively projecting all embeddings onto the **unit hypersphere** $S^{767} \subset \mathbb{R}^{768}$ and measuring the geodesic distance (arc length) between them.

### 5.1. The Unit Hypersphere

The unit hypersphere in $\mathbb{R}^d$ is the set of all vectors with $\|\mathbf{x}\| = 1$:

$$S^{d-1} = \{\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^d : \|\mathbf{x}\| = 1\}$$

For $d = 768$, this is $S^{767}$ — a 767-dimensional surface embedded in 768-dim space.

### 5.2. Cosine as Geodesic Distance

On the unit hypersphere, the geodesic distance (shortest arc along the sphere's surface) between two points $\hat{\mathbf{A}}$ and $\hat{\mathbf{B}}$ is:

$$d_{\text{geo}}(\hat{\mathbf{A}}, \hat{\mathbf{B}}) = \arccos(\hat{\mathbf{A}} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{B}}) = \arccos(\cos(\theta)) = \theta$$

So cosine similarity is a monotonic transformation of geodesic distance on the unit hypersphere. Cosine similarity 1.0 corresponds to geodesic distance 0 (same point); cosine similarity 0 corresponds to geodesic distance $\pi/2$ (orthogonal); cosine similarity -1 corresponds to geodesic distance $\pi$ (antipodal).

### 5.3. Why the Hypersphere Picture Matters

The hypersphere picture is the right mental model for BERT embeddings after normalisation. All embeddings live on $S^{767}$, and the retrieval task is "find the $K$ nearest neighbors on the sphere." This is also why FAISS indexes (Chapter 5.2) work: they implement efficient nearest-neighbor search on the sphere (or in Euclidean space, which is equivalent after normalisation).

The picture also explains anisotropy (Chapter 3.3): if all embeddings cluster in a narrow cone on the sphere, the geodesic distances between them are all small, and the cosine similarities are all near 1 — exactly the "0.9 trap."
