# 04.1. 768-D Geometry and Manifolds

The `model.encode(text)` function in this project transforms a clinical sentence into a point in a **768-dimensional space**. The original vault used a "crumpled piece of paper" analogy for the Manifold Hypothesis and noted that high dimensions are "usually bad for computers" but did not provide a formal definition, did not explain why 768 is the right number, and did not cover the **dimensionality-reduction algorithms (t-SNE, UMAP)** that are the standard tools for visualising and validating these spaces. This note fills all three gaps.

This chapter covers: (1) what the 768 dimensions actually represent, (2) the formal Manifold Hypothesis, (3) why 768 is the chosen dimensionality, (4) t-SNE and UMAP for visualisation, and (5) how the project uses dimensionality reduction to verify embedding quality.

---

## 1. What the 768 Dimensions Represent

When BioBERT encodes the term *"Cystic Fibrosis"* into a 768-dimensional vector, each of the 768 numbers is an **abstract learned feature**. These features are not interpretable in isolation — there is no axis labelled "Anatomical Location" or "Pathological Severity" — but they collectively encode the model's representation of the term's meaning.

The 768 features emerge from the architecture: BERT-Base has a hidden dimension of 768, which means every layer's output (including the final `[CLS]` vector) is a 768-dimensional vector. The number 768 is a design choice — large enough to capture rich semantic distinctions, small enough to fit in GPU memory and train in reasonable time.

### 1.1. Are the Dimensions Interpretable?

Research on **probing classifiers** (Tenney et al., 2019) has shown that individual attention heads and individual hidden dimensions do encode partial, statistically detectable linguistic features:

- Some heads specialise for syntactic dependencies (subject–verb, object–verb).
- Some hidden dimensions correlate with part-of-speech categories.
- Some heads attend to negation, coreference, or entity boundaries.

But these are statistical tendencies, not human-readable labels. You cannot point to dimension 137 and say "this is the Anatomical Location axis." The model's features are distributed and entangled — meaning is encoded in the **joint configuration** of all 768 numbers, not in any individual coordinate.

### 1.2. The Geometric Picture

Geometrically, each BioBERT embedding is a single point in $\mathbb{R}^{768}$. The set of all patient-note embeddings forms a **cloud** in this space, as does the set of all Orphanet-definition embeddings. The project's success depends on the patient-note cloud and the correct-disease-definition cloud overlapping closely, while the wrong-disease clouds are far away.

This is the "embedding space geometry" picture that motivates the entire Stage 2 retrieval: cosine similarity is just the angle between two points in this 768-dim space, and the Top-K retrieval is finding the $K$ disease-definition points closest (by angle) to the patient-note point.

## 2. The Formal Manifold Hypothesis

The **Manifold Hypothesis** is the foundational assumption that makes high-dimensional machine learning tractable. Formally:

> Real-world high-dimensional data $\mathcal{X} \subset \mathbb{R}^D$ is concentrated near a low-dimensional manifold $\mathcal{M}$ of intrinsic dimension $d \ll D$, embedded in the ambient space $\mathbb{R}^D$ via some smooth (possibly nonlinear) map $f: \mathcal{M} \to \mathbb{R}^D$.

For BioBERT embeddings: $D = 768$, and the intrinsic dimension $d$ is empirically estimated to be in the range 10–50 (depending on the corpus and the estimation method). This means the 768-dimensional embeddings live on a ~20-dimensional "surface" inside the 768-dim space.

### 2.1. The "Crumpled Paper" Analogy, Made Precise

The crumpled-paper analogy is:

1. Start with a flat 2D sheet of paper. This is the **intrinsic manifold** $\mathcal{M}$ — the "true" semantic space where similar concepts are nearby.
2. Crumple the paper into a ball. This is the **embedding map** $f: \mathcal{M} \to \mathbb{R}^3$ — the 768-dim BioBERT representation.
3. Two points that were close on the flat paper may now be far apart in 3D space (because the crumpling pushed them to opposite sides of the ball), and vice versa.

BERT's training objective (MLM) implicitly **learns** an embedding map $f$ that "un-crumples" the semantic relationships. After training, two semantically similar concepts should be close in $\mathbb{R}^{768}$ even if their surface forms are different.

### 2.2. Why the Manifold Hypothesis Matters Practically

If the Manifold Hypothesis holds (which it empirically does for BERT embeddings), then:

1. **Dimensionality reduction is possible.** We can project $\mathbb{R}^{768}$ down to $\mathbb{R}^{2}$ or $\mathbb{R}^{3}$ for visualisation without losing the essential structure (§4 below).
2. **Distance metrics are meaningful.** Cosine similarity on $\mathbb{R}^{768}$ is a good proxy for semantic similarity because the embeddings are concentrated near a low-dim manifold where the metric is well-behaved.
3. **Anisotropy is detectable.** If the manifold is "tilted" relative to the origin (the narrow-cone phenomenon, Chapter 3.3), this is visible in the eigenvalue spectrum of the embedding covariance matrix.

### 2.3. Estimating the Intrinsic Dimension

The intrinsic dimension $d$ can be estimated by:

- **Principal Component Analysis (PCA)**: Compute the eigenvalues of the covariance matrix; the number of eigenvalues above a threshold (e.g., 1% of the largest) is an estimate of $d$.
- **Maximum Likelihood Estimation (Levina & Bickel, 2004)**: For each point, look at the distances to its $k$ nearest neighbours; the intrinsic dimension is estimated from the rate of growth of these distances.
- **TwoNN (Facco et al., 2017)**: A more robust estimator based on the ratio of distances to the two nearest neighbours.

For BERT embeddings, these methods consistently estimate $d \approx 20$–$50$, confirming that the 768-dim representation is highly redundant.

## 3. Why 768? The Dimensionality Tradeoff

The choice of $d_{\text{model}} = 768$ is a tradeoff between capacity and cost:

### 3.1. Larger Dimensions

- **Pros**: More capacity to encode fine semantic distinctions. Higher ceiling on task performance.
- **Cons**: Larger embedding tables ($|V| \times d_{\text{model}}$ parameters). Quadratic growth in feed-forward layer parameters ($d \times 4d$). Slower training and inference. More prone to overfitting on small datasets.

### 3.2. Smaller dimensions

- **Pros**: Faster training and inference. Lower memory footprint. Less prone to overfitting.
- **Cons**: Less capacity. May not be able to distinguish rare diseases with similar presentations.

### 3.3. The BERT-Base Choice

BERT-Base uses $d_{\text{model}} = 768$ because:

- It is large enough to encode the syntactic and semantic distinctions needed for general English NLP.
- It fits in 16GB GPU memory with batch size 32 for sequence length 512.
- It produces a model of 110M parameters, which is large enough to be capable but small enough to fine-tune on a single GPU.

BERT-Large uses $d_{\text{model}} = 1024$ (24 layers, 16 heads, 340M parameters) for higher capacity at ~3× the compute cost. Modern LLMs (LLaMA-2-70B, GPT-4) use $d_{\text{model}} = 8192$ or higher, but this is enabled by orders of magnitude more compute.

For the project's pipeline, BioBERT-Base (768) is sufficient because the multi-stage architecture compensates for the embedding's limited capacity. The Knowledge Graph (Stage 3) provides the discrete verification that the embedding cannot, so the embedding's job is just to produce a good Top-20 shortlist.

## 4. Dimensionality Reduction for Visualisation

Because the Manifold Hypothesis holds (the 768-dim data lives on a ~20-dim manifold), we can project the embeddings down to 2 or 3 dimensions for visualisation. Two algorithms dominate this task: **t-SNE** and **UMAP**.

### 4.1. PCA — The Linear Baseline

PCA is the simplest dimensionality reduction: it computes the principal components of the covariance matrix and projects onto the top $k$ eigenvectors. PCA is **linear**, so it can only capture linear structure. It is fast (seconds for 10k embeddings) and deterministic, but it cannot visualise the nonlinear manifold structure that BERT embeddings exhibit.

PCA is typically used as a preprocessing step before t-SNE or UMAP (to reduce from 768 to 50 dimensions, which makes the nonlinear methods much faster).

### 4.2. t-SNE — The Nonlinear Classic

t-SNE (t-Distributed Stochastic Neighbor Embedding, van der Maaten & Hinton, 2008) is a nonlinear method that preserves **local neighbourhoods**. It works by:

1. In high-dim space, compute a Gaussian probability distribution over pairs of points: $p_{ij} \propto \exp(-\|x_i - x_j\|^2 / 2\sigma_i^2)$. This captures which points are "neighbors."
2. In low-dim space, compute a Student-t distribution over pairs: $q_{ij} \propto (1 + \|y_i - y_j\|^2)^{-1}$.
3. Minimise the KL divergence $D_{\text{KL}}(P \| Q)$ between the high-dim and low-dim distributions via gradient descent.

The result is a 2D (or 3D) scatter plot in which semantically similar embeddings cluster together. t-SNE is the standard visualisation tool for embedding quality checks.

**Limitations**:
- $O(N^2)$ complexity in the number of points; does not scale beyond ~50k embeddings without approximations (Barnes-Hut t-SNE).
- The global structure is not preserved — cluster sizes and inter-cluster distances are not meaningful.
- Stochastic: different runs produce different plots. The perplexity hyperparameter (typically 5–50) must be tuned.

### 4.3. UMAP — The Modern Default

UMAP (Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection, McInnes et al., 2018) is a more recent method that preserves both **local and global structure** better than t-SNE, scales to millions of points, and is deterministic given a seed.

UMAP works by:

1. Constructing a fuzzy topological representation of the high-dim data (a weighted k-nearest-neighbour graph).
2. Constructing a similar graph in low-dim space.
3. Minimising the cross-entropy between the two graph structures via gradient descent.

UMAP's advantages over t-SNE:

- **Faster**: $O(N \log N)$ vs. $O(N^2)$.
- **Preserves global structure**: inter-cluster distances are meaningful.
- **Deterministic**: same seed → same plot.
- **Transform**: a UMAP projection fitted on one dataset can be applied to new points (t-SNE cannot).

For the project, UMAP is the preferred visualisation tool.

### 4.4. Using Visualisation to Verify Embedding Quality

A standard sanity check is to:

1. Encode ~1,000 patient notes and ~1,000 Orphanet disease definitions with BioBERT.
2. Project all 2,000 embeddings to 2D with UMAP.
3. Color-code by semantic category (e.g., "Albinism family", "Marfan family", "Huntington family", "unrelated").

If the embeddings are good, each disease family should form a distinguishable cluster, and patient notes should sit near their corresponding disease-definition cluster. If the embeddings are bad (e.g., anisotropy has collapsed everything into one blob), the UMAP plot will show no clustering, and the project needs BERT-Whitening (Chapter 3.3) or a different embedding model.

## 5. The Curse of Dimensionality (Setup for Chapter 4.3)

A second phenomenon — distinct from the Manifold Hypothesis but equally important — is the **Curse of Dimensionality**: in high-dimensional spaces, distance metrics behave counterintuitively. Specifically, the ratio of the nearest and farthest distances from any query point approaches 1 as dimension grows, which means **all points become nearly equidistant**. This is the deep geometric reason why Euclidean distance fails for BERT embeddings and why cosine similarity is preferred. The full mathematical treatment is in [[04.3. Length Invariance and the Curse of Dimensionality]].

## 6. Practical Implications for the Project

1. **The 768-dim embeddings are not arbitrary.** They are the output of a learned map from a low-dim semantic manifold into $\mathbb{R}^{768}$, and their geometry is meaningful.
2. **Visualisation is feasible.** UMAP plots are a standard diagnostic tool; the project should generate them as part of every embedding-model evaluation.
3. **The intrinsic dimension is ~20–50.** This means the 768-dim representation is highly redundant, and dimensionality reduction (PCA, autoencoders) could in principle compress it without much information loss. In practice, the cost of operating on 768-dim vectors is low enough that compression is not necessary.
4. **Anisotropy lives in the eigenvalue spectrum.** The narrow-cone phenomenon (Chapter 3.3) is visible as a small number of dominant eigenvalues in the covariance matrix; BERT-Whitening equalises these eigenvalues.
