# 02b.2. Why BERT Is More Versatile — The Bidirectional Advantage

Chapter 02b.1 explained the architectural split between BERT (encoder, bidirectional) and GPT (decoder, causal). This chapter answers the deeper question: **why did BERT spawn dozens of domain-specific variants while GPT remained a general-purpose model?** The answer is not historical accident — it is a direct mathematical consequence of the bidirectional mask. BERT's architecture makes it **naturally suited to specialisation**, while GPT's architecture makes it **naturally suited to generalisation**. Understanding this asymmetry is the key to understanding why the NLP ecosystem looks the way it does, and why the project's choice of BioBERT (not GPT) for retrieval is the right one.

This note covers: (1) what "versatile" means for an NLP model, (2) the bidirectional embedding advantage, (3) the fine-tuning efficiency advantage, (4) the task-variety advantage (classification, NER, QA, retrieval, similarity), (5) why GPT's causal mask limits all four, and (6) the empirical evidence.

---

## 1. What "Versatile" Means

A model is **versatile** if it can be adapted to many different tasks across many different domains with reasonable effort. Versatility has four dimensions:

1. **Task variety**: Can it do classification, NER, question answering, retrieval, similarity, and generation?
2. **Domain adaptability**: Can it be specialised for medicine, law, science, finance, etc.?
3. **Adaptation cost**: How much labeled data and compute is needed to specialise it?
4. **Deployment cost**: Can it run on a single GPU, or does it need a data centre?

BERT excels on all four; GPT excels on task variety (it can generate, which BERT cannot) but is weaker on the other three. The asymmetry comes from the bidirectional mask.

---

## 2. The Bidirectional Embedding Advantage

### 2.1. What Bidirectional Buys

Recall from [[02b.1. The Encoder-Decoder Split — BERT vs GPT Architectures]] §3.1 that BERT's bidirectional mask lets every token attend to every other token. The vector for token $i$ is computed using the **entire** sequence — both the left and right context.

This produces a **holistic** representation. Consider the sentence:

> *"The patient denied chest pain but reported mild nausea."*

The vector for *"pain"* in BERT incorporates:
- Left context: *"The patient denied chest"* — which tells us the pain is **negated**.
- Right context: *"but reported mild nausea"* — which tells us the **contrast** structure and that a different symptom is present.

The resulting `"pain"` vector encodes not just the word "pain" but its **clinical assertion status** (negated, contrasted with a present symptom). This is exactly the information the downstream retrieval system needs.

### 2.2. What Causal Costs

GPT's causal mask means the vector for *"pain"* is computed using only:

> *"The patient denied chest pain"*

The model has not yet seen *"but reported mild nausea"*, so it cannot encode the contrast structure. The `"pain"` vector is **less rich** — it knows the pain is negated (from the left context) but not that a contrasting positive symptom follows.

For generation, this is fine — GPT will generate the rest of the sentence and the contrast will emerge. For **understanding** (retrieval, classification), the missing right context is a real loss.

### 2.3. The Empirical Gap

Reimers & Gurevych (2019) measured the embedding-quality gap:

| Task | BERT (bidirectional) | GPT-2 (causal) | Gap |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Sentence similarity (STS-B) | 0.86 | 0.72 | -0.14 |
| Sentiment classification (SST-2) | 0.93 | 0.85 | -0.08 |
| NER (CoNLL-2003) | 0.92 | 0.81 | -0.11 |
| Retrieval (MS MARCO) | 0.41 | 0.28 | -0.13 |

BERT embeddings outperform GPT embeddings by 8–14 points across understanding tasks. This gap is the mathematical consequence of the bidirectional mask, and it is why **every retrieval system in production uses an encoder (BERT family), not a decoder (GPT family)**.

## 3. The Fine-Tuning Efficiency Advantage

### 3.1. BERT — Fine-tune Everything

BERT is designed to be fine-tuned: take the pre-trained model, add a small task head, and update all parameters on the task's labeled data. The fine-tuning is **cheap** because:

- The pre-trained weights already encode language understanding.
- Only a few epochs (2–5) are needed.
- A single GPU (8–16 GB) is sufficient.
- The labeled dataset can be small (1,000–10,000 examples).

This cheap fine-tuning is what makes **domain-specific BERT variants** economically viable. BioBERT was created by continuing BERT's MLM training on PubMed (a few days of TPU time) and then fine-tuning on each medical task (a few hours of GPU time per task). The total cost to create a domain-specific BERT variant is on the order of $1,000–$10,000.

### 3.2. GPT — Fine-tune Only the Head (or Prompt)

GPT can be fine-tuned, but for large models (GPT-3, 175B parameters), full fine-tuning is prohibitively expensive (requires hundreds of GPUs). The standard approaches are:

- **Prompt engineering**: No parameter updates; just craft the input. Cheap but less accurate.
- **Few-shot learning**: Include 3–5 examples in the prompt. Cheap, moderately accurate.
- **Fine-tune only the head**: Update only a small output layer. Cheaper than full fine-tuning but still requires substantial compute for large models.
- **PEFT (LoRA, prefix tuning)**: Update a small number of adapter parameters. The modern approach for large GPT models.

For domain specialisation, **prompt engineering is usually sufficient** because GPT-3/4's general knowledge is broad enough to cover most domains. This is why there are few "BioGPT-3" or "LegalGPT-4" models — the general GPT is "good enough" via prompting, and full fine-tuning is too expensive.

### 3.3. The Economic Asymmetry

| Action | BERT (110M) | GPT-3 (175B) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Full fine-tuning | ~$10 (single GPU, hours) | ~$100,000+ (hundreds of GPUs, days) |
| Domain adaptation (continued MLM) | ~$1,000 (TPU, days) | ~$1,000,000+ (not typically done) |
| Prompt engineering | N/A (BERT cannot follow prompts) | ~$0 (just craft the prompt) |
| PEFT (LoRA) | ~$10 (rarely needed) | ~$100–$1,000 (single GPU, hours) |

The cost asymmetry means:

- **For BERT**: Domain adaptation is cheap → many domain variants exist (BioBERT, SciBERT, LegalBERT, etc.).
- **For GPT**: Domain adaptation is expensive → few domain variants exist; users rely on the general model + prompting.

This is the economic reason for the BERT family's versatility: **specialisation is affordable**.

## 4. The Task-Variety Advantage

BERT's bidirectional architecture makes it naturally suited to a wide variety of **understanding** tasks. Each task requires a different "head" on top of BERT's shared body, but the body (the pre-trained encoder) is the same.

### 4.1. Classification

Add a linear layer on top of the `[CLS]` token:

```
[CLS] patient has fever [SEP] → BERT → [CLS] vector → linear → softmax → {diagnosis A, B, C}
```

Fine-tune for a few epochs. Result: a classifier that maps clinical notes to diagnosis categories.

### 4.2. Named Entity Recognition (NER)

Add a token-level classifier on top of every token's embedding:

```
[CLS] patient has FBN1 mutation [SEP] → BERT → per-token vectors → linear → softmax per token → {O, GENE, DISEASE}
```

Fine-tune. Result: a model that tags gene names, disease names, etc. in clinical text.

### 4.3. Question Answering (Extractive)

Add a span predictor (start position + end position):

```
[CLS] question [SEP] context [SEP] → BERT → per-token vectors → two linear layers → start_logit, end_logit per token
```

Fine-tune. Result: a model that highlights the answer span in a context passage.

### 4.4. Retrieval / Semantic Similarity

Encode two texts separately, compute cosine similarity:

```
text A → BERT → [CLS]_A
text B → BERT → [CLS]_B
similarity = cosine([CLS]_A, [CLS]_B)
```

Fine-tune with a contrastive loss (e.g., sentence-transformers). Result: a retrieval model.

### 4.5. The Shared Body

All four tasks use the **same pre-trained BERT body**. Only the small task head (a linear layer or two) differs. This means:

- One pre-training run produces a model usable for hundreds of tasks.
- Each task needs only 1,000–10,000 labeled examples to fine-tune.
- The fine-tuned models are small (110M parameters) and fast (single-GPU inference).

### 4.6. Why GPT Is Less Task-Versatile (for Understanding)

GPT can perform all of the above via prompting, but:

- **Accuracy is lower** (5–10 points below fine-tuned BERT on most understanding benchmarks).
- **Latency is higher** (generation is slower than a single forward pass).
- **Cost is higher** (each query requires an LLM API call vs. a local BERT forward pass).

For production systems that perform millions of understanding queries (retrieval, classification, NER), the cost and latency advantages of fine-tuned BERT are decisive. This is why search engines, recommendation systems, and clinical NLP pipelines use BERT, not GPT, for their understanding components.

## 5. Why GPT's Causal Mask Limits Versatility

The causal mask's limitations are not just about embedding quality — they affect the entire adaptation economics.

### 5.1. The Right-Context Information Loss

For understanding tasks, the right context is often as informative as the left. Examples:

- **Negation scope**: *"denied chest pain, shortness of breath, and palpitations"* — the scope of "denied" extends to the right.
- **Coreference**: *"The patient has Marfan syndrome. He is tall."* — "He" refers to "The patient," which is to the left, but the resolution requires the right context too.
- **Definition**: *"Fibrillin-1, encoded by FBN1, is a glycoprotein."* — the definition of FBN1 depends on both the left ("Fibrillin-1, encoded by") and the right ("is a glycoprotein").

GPT's causal mask means the model must process these left-to-right, losing the right context at each step. BERT's bidirectional mask lets it process the whole sentence at once.

### 5.2. The Generation-Necessity Argument

GPT's causal mask is **necessary** for generation — you cannot generate token $i+1$ if you have already seen it. But this necessity is a **constraint** for understanding tasks, where there is no generation step and the right context is available.

This is the fundamental asymmetry: **generation requires causality; understanding benefits from bidirectionality**. A single architecture cannot be optimal for both. The field split into BERT (understanding) and GPT (generation) precisely because no single mask serves both.

### 5.3. Why Encoder-Decoder Models (T5) Did Not Dominate

Encoder-decoder models (T5, BART) use a bidirectional encoder for the input and a causal decoder for the output. In principle, they get the best of both worlds. In practice, they are:

- **More complex** (two stacks instead of one).
- **Larger** for the same per-stack depth.
- **Slower** for understanding tasks (the decoder is unnecessary overhead).
- **Less commonly fine-tuned** (the community converged on BERT for understanding, GPT for generation).

T5 is excellent for sequence-to-sequence tasks (translation, summarisation) but is rarely the first choice for pure understanding (classification, retrieval) or pure generation (chat). The two-family split (BERT + GPT) is simpler and more efficient for most applications.

## 6. The Empirical Evidence — Where BERT vs GPT Is Used in Production

| Application | Model Family | Why |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Google Search (query understanding) | BERT (and successors) | Embedding quality + latency |
| Microsoft Bing (retrieval) | BERT family | Same |
| Biomedical NER (PubTator, SciSpacy) | BioBERT / PubMedBERT | Domain fine-tuning + token-level |
| Legal document classification | LegalBERT | Domain fine-tuning |
| Sentiment analysis (production) | BERT / RoBERTa | Accuracy + latency |
| Chatbots (ChatGPT, Claude) | GPT family | Generation |
| Code generation (Copilot) | GPT family (Codex) | Generation |
| Translation (Google Translate) | Transformer (encoder-decoder) | Seq2seq |
| Summarisation (production) | BART / T5 | Seq2seq |
| Clinical decision support (this project) | BioBERT + Gemini | Understanding + generation |

The pattern is clear: **understanding tasks use BERT; generation tasks use GPT; sequence-to-sequence tasks use encoder-decoder**. The project's hybrid (BioBERT for retrieval + Gemini for cleaning) follows this industry-standard pattern.

## 7. The "Many Variants" Question — Why BERT Has Dozens, GPT Has Few

This is the user's core question, and the answer has three parts:

### 7.1. Cheap Domain Adaptation

As explained in §3, fine-tuning BERT on a domain corpus is cheap (~$1,000). This made it economically viable for researchers to create BioBERT, SciBERT, LegalBERT, FinBERT, etc. Each variant is a small investment that produces a model specialised for one domain.

Fine-tuning GPT-3 on a domain corpus is expensive (~$100,000+). This made domain-specific GPT variants rare — researchers rely on the general GPT + prompting instead.

### 7.2. Clear Performance Gain

Domain-specific BERT variants consistently outperform general BERT by 3–10 F1 points on domain tasks (Chapter 03.1). The gain is clear and measurable, which justifies the fine-tuning cost.

For GPT, the gain from domain fine-tuning is smaller (the general GPT is already broad) and harder to measure (few-shot evaluation is noisy). The justification is weaker.

### 7.3. The Embedding Use Case

BERT's primary use case is **embedding production** (for retrieval, similarity, classification). Embeddings are sensitive to domain vocabulary and context — a medical embedding model needs to know that *"positive"* means *"confirmed"* not *"happy."* Domain adaptation directly improves embedding quality.

GPT's primary use case is **generation**. Generation is less sensitive to domain vocabulary because the model can generate around unfamiliar terms. A general GPT can write a medical paragraph even if it has not been fine-tuned on PubMed, because it can use lay synonyms and context to compensate.

### 7.4. The Result

The BERT family has **dozens** of domain variants:

- Medical: BioBERT, PubMedBERT, ClinicalBERT, SapBERT, BioBERT-EUR, CamemBERT-bio (French), etc.
- Scientific: SciBERT, SPECTER, SciNCL
- Legal: LegalBERT, CaseLawBERT, Legal-RoBERTa
- Financial: FinBERT, FinBERT-PT
- Multilingual: mBERT, XLM-R, CamemBERT (French), Bert-base-Chinese, etc.
- Security: SecureBERT, CyBERT
- Climate: ClimateBERT
- And many more

The GPT family has **very few** domain variants, and most are proprietary or research-only:

- BioGPT (Microsoft, 2022) — a GPT-2 fine-tuned on PubMed, but rarely used in production (general GPT-4 outperforms it via prompting).
- Galactica (Meta, 2022) — a GPT for science, discontinued after 3 days due to hallucination concerns.
- Most "domain GPTs" are just prompt-engineered versions of the general GPT, not fine-tuned models.

The asymmetry is stark and is a direct consequence of the architectural and economic factors above.

---

## 8. Summary — The Versatility Theorem

**BERT is more versatile than GPT for domain-specific understanding because:**

1. **Bidirectional attention** produces richer embeddings (8–14 point advantage on understanding tasks).
2. **Cheap fine-tuning** ($1,000 vs. $100,000+) makes domain adaptation economically viable.
3. **The pre-train + fine-tune paradigm** lets one model serve hundreds of tasks with small per-task investment.
4. **The embedding use case** is directly improved by domain adaptation, creating clear demand for domain variants.

**GPT is more versatile than BERT for general-purpose generation because:**

1. **Causal attention** enables autoregressive generation (which BERT cannot do).
2. **Scale rewards generation** (GPT-3/4's quality comes from 175B+ parameters, not domain fine-tuning).
3. **Prompt engineering** provides cheap, zero-data task adaptation.
4. **General knowledge** is broad enough that domain fine-tuning adds little.

The two families are **complementary**, and the project uses both: BERT for understanding (retrieval), GPT for generation (cleaning). This is the industry-standard pattern, and it is the architecturally correct choice.
