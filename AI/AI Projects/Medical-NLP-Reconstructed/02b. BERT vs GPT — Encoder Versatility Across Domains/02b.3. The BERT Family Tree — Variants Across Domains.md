# 02b.3. The BERT Family Tree — Variants Across Domains

Within five years of BERT's release (October 2018), the NLP community had created **over 50 domain-specific BERT variants** spanning medicine, science, law, finance, chemistry, security, climate, software engineering, and dozens of languages. No other architecture in NLP history has produced this kind of proliferation. This chapter surveys the family tree, explains the **three specialisation axes** that distinguish each variant, and answers the central question: **why did BERT spawn so many variants while GPT spawned so few?**

This note is the empirical companion to [[02b.2. Why BERT Is More Versatile — The Bidirectional Advantage]] (which explained the *theoretical* reason) — here we document the *actual* variants and what each one specialises for.

---

## 1. The Three Specialisation Axes

Every domain-specific BERT variant differs from vanilla BERT along one or more of three axes:

### 1.1. Axis 1 — Training Corpus

The most common specialisation: continue BERT's MLM training on a domain-specific corpus. This shifts the embeddings to reflect domain vocabulary and context.

- **BioBERT**: BERT + PubMed abstracts + PMC full-text (~18B words).
- **SciBERT**: BERT + Semantic Scholar corpus (~1.1M papers, ~3.3B words).
- **LegalBERT**: BERT + legal contracts, legislation, court opinions (~3.5 GB).
- **FinBERT**: BERT + financial reports, earnings calls, Reuters news (~4.5B tokens).

### 1.2. Axis 2 — Vocabulary

A more aggressive specialisation: train the WordPiece tokenizer **from scratch** on domain text, producing a domain-specific vocabulary. This reduces subword fragmentation for domain terms (Chapter 02.3 §6).

- **PubMedBERT**: vocabulary trained on PubMed from scratch → medical morphemes (`oculo`, `cutane`, `FBN1`) as first-class tokens.
- **SciBERT**: vocabulary trained on Semantic Scholar from scratch → scientific terms (`##tion`, `##ology`) better represented.

### 1.3. Axis 3 — Training Objective

The most research-intensive specialisation: change the pre-training objective to teach the model a domain-specific skill.

- **SapBERT**: replaced MLM with Multi-Similarity Loss on UMLS synonym pairs → entity-linking skill.
- **KEPLER**: added a knowledge-graph embedding objective → entity-aware representations.
- **LINKBERT**: added a multi-document linking objective (linking related PubMed papers) → cross-document reasoning.

### 1.4. The Combinations

Most variants combine multiple axes. PubMedBERT uses Axis 2 (from-scratch vocabulary) + Axis 1 (PubMed corpus). SapBERT uses Axis 3 (contrastive objective) + Axis 1 (UMLS corpus). The combination determines the variant's specific strengths.

## 2. The Medical Branch

The medical branch is the deepest and most relevant to this project. It includes:

### 2.1. BioBERT (Lee et al., 2019)

- **Axes**: Corpus (PubMed + PMC).
- **Vocabulary**: Inherited from BERT-Base (general English).
- **Strength**: Balanced medical reasoning; the baseline for medical NLP.
- **Project role**: The primary sentence-level cosine similarity scorer. See [[03.1. Why General AI Fails in Medicine]].

### 2.2. PubMedBERT (Gu et al., 2020)

- **Axes**: Corpus (PubMed) + Vocabulary (from scratch).
- **Strength**: Entity-level precision (medical morphemes as first-class tokens → minimal fragmentation).
- **Project role**: Entity-level matching in Stage 3. See [[03.2. ClinicalBERT, PubMedBERT, and SapBERT]] §3.

### 2.3. ClinicalBERT (Alsentzer et al., 2019)

- **Axes**: Corpus (MIMIC-III discharge summaries).
- **Strength**: Doctor shorthand, abbreviations, ICU-specific vocabulary.
- **Project role**: Parsing raw, uncleaned clinical notes. See [[03.2. ClinicalBERT, PubMedBERT, and SapBERT]] §2.

### 2.4. SapBERT (Liu et al., 2020)

- **Axes**: Corpus (UMLS) + Objective (Multi-Similarity Loss).
- **Strength**: Synonym alignment (entity linking).
- **Project role**: Matching extracted phenotypes to canonical HPO labels. See [[03.2. ClinicalBERT, PubMedBERT, and SapBERT]] §4.

### 2.5. BioBERT-EUR / CamemBERT-bio / Bert-base-Chinese-medical

- **Axes**: Corpus (medical text in a non-English language).
- **Strength**: Multilingual medical NLP.
- **Project role**: Not used (the project is English-only), but relevant for international deployment.

### 2.6. BlueBERT (Peng et al., 2019)

- **Axes**: Corpus (PubMed + MIMIC-III combined).
- **Strength**: Bridges biomedical literature and clinical notes.
- **Project role**: Alternative to BioBERT/ClinicalBERT; not used in the current pipeline.

The medical branch has at least **10 published variants**, each addressing a different aspect of clinical NLP. The project uses four of them (BioBERT, PubMedBERT, ClinicalBERT, SapBERT) plus MiniLM as a control — see [[03.2. ClinicalBERT, PubMedBERT, and SapBERT]].

## 3. The Scientific Branch

### 3.1. SciBERT (Beltagy et al., 2019)

- **Corpus**: Semantic Scholar (1.14M papers, dominated by computer science and biomedicine).
- **Vocabulary**: Trained from scratch on the scientific corpus.
- **Strength**: Scientific paper understanding; cross-disciplinary (CS + bio).
- **Use case**: Scientific NER, citation recommendation, paper classification.

### 3.2. SPECTER (Cohan et al., 2020)

- **Corpus**: Scientific papers + citation links.
- **Objective**: Contrastive learning on citation triples (paper, cited paper, random paper).
- **Strength**: Citation-aware paper embeddings.
- **Use case**: Paper recommendation, citation graph analysis.

### 3.3. SciNCL (Ostendorff et al., 2022)

- **Improvement over SPECTER**: Uses nearest-neighbour citation graphs instead of random negatives.
- **Strength**: Better paper similarity than SPECTER.
- **Use case**: Same as SPECTER.

The scientific branch is smaller than the medical branch (~5 major variants) but is the foundation for academic search engines like Semantic Scholar.

---

## 4. The Legal Branch

### 4.1. LegalBERT (Chalkidis et al., 2020)

- **Corpus**: Legal contracts, EU legislation, UK court opinions, US court opinions (~3.5 GB).
- **Vocabulary**: Both inherited and from-scratch variants were released.
- **Strength**: Legal NER, legal document classification, case-law retrieval.
- **Use case**: Legal tech (contract analysis, case retrieval, compliance).

### 4.2. CaseLawBERT (Zheng et al., 2021)

- **Corpus**: US case law specifically.
- **Strength**: Better on case-law tasks than the broader LegalBERT.
- **Use case**: Case-law retrieval, legal QA.

### 4.3. Legal-RoBERTa

- **Corpus**: Legal text, based on RoBERTa (a BERT successor) instead of BERT.
- **Strength**: Combines RoBERTa's improved training with legal domain adaptation.
- **Use case**: Same as LegalBERT.

The legal branch has ~5 variants and is the foundation for legal-tech platforms like Casetext and LexisNexis AI features.

---

## 5. The Financial Branch

### 5.1. FinBERT (Araci, 2019)

- **Corpus**: Financial news, earnings calls, Reuters articles.
- **Strength**: Financial sentiment classification (positive/negative/neutral on financial news).
- **Use case**: Algorithmic trading signals, earnings-call analysis, market sentiment monitoring.

### 5.2. FinBERT-PT (Yang et al., 2020)

- **Corpus**: Financial reports (SEC filings).
- **Strength**: Better on formal financial documents than the news-focused FinBERT.
- **Use case**: SEC filing analysis, risk detection.

The financial branch is small (~3 variants) but commercially important — financial sentiment analysis is a multi-billion-dollar application.

---

## 6. The Multilingual Branch

### 6.1. mBERT (Google, 2018)

- **Corpus**: Wikipedia in 104 languages.
- **Vocabulary**: 30k tokens shared across all 104 languages (WordPiece trained on the combined corpus).
- **Strength**: Cross-lingual transfer — fine-tune on English, deploy on French/Chinese/etc.
- **Use case**: Multilingual NER, cross-lingual retrieval.

### 6.2. XLM-R (Conneau et al., 2019)

- **Corpus**: CommonCrawl in 100 languages (~2.5TB).
- **Strength**: Better than mBERT on low-resource languages (more diverse corpus).
- **Use case**: Same as mBERT.

### 6.3. Language-Specific BERTs

Dozens of variants exist for individual languages:

- **CamemBERT** (French)
- **Bert-base-Chinese** (Chinese)
- **BERTIN** (Spanish)
- **GottBERT** (German)
- **AraBERT** (Arabic)
- **IndoBERT** (Indonesian)
- **Japanese-BERT**, **Korean-BERT**, **Turkish-BERT**, etc.

The multilingual branch is the largest by variant count (~30+ models) because each major language benefits from a dedicated vocabulary and corpus.

## 7. The Chemistry / Biology Branch

### 7.1. ChemBERTa (Chithrananda et al., 2020)

- **Corpus**: SMILES strings (chemical notation, ~77M molecules).
- **Strength**: Chemical property prediction, molecule generation.
- **Use case**: Drug discovery, toxicity prediction.

### 7.2. MolBERT

- **Corpus**: SMILES strings.
- **Strength**: Molecular embedding for similarity search.
- **Use case**: Drug candidate screening.

### 7.3. ProtBERT (Elnaggar et al., 2020)

- **Corpus**: ~217M protein sequences from UniProt.
- **Strength**: Protein structure/function prediction.
- **Use case**: Bioinformatics, protein engineering.

The chemistry/biology branch (~5 variants) operates on non-text data (SMILES strings, protein sequences) but uses the BERT architecture because the MLM objective generalises to any token sequence.

---

## 8. The Security / Software Branch

### 8.1. SecureBERT (Ken et al., 2021)

- **Corpus**: Cybersecurity text (CVE descriptions, security blogs, threat reports).
- **Strength**: Cybersecurity NER (vulnerability names, attack techniques, malware families).
- **Use case**: Threat intelligence, automated CVE analysis.

### 8.2. CyBERT (Brazil et al., 2020)

- **Corpus**: Cybersecurity text.
- **Strength**: Similar to SecureBERT.
- **Use case**: Same.

### 8.3. CodeBERT (Feng et al., 2020)

- **Corpus**: Programming languages (Python, Java, JavaScript, etc.) + natural-language comments.
- **Strength**: Code understanding (code search, documentation generation).
- **Use case**: Developer tools (search, autocomplete).

The security/software branch (~5 variants) serves DevSecOps and developer-tooling use cases.

---

## 9. The Climate / Sustainability Branch

### 9.1. ClimateBERT (Webersinke et al., 2021)

- **Corpus**: Climate-related text (IPCC reports, climate news, corporate sustainability disclosures).
- **Strength**: Climate NER (climate-related entities), climate sentiment classification.
- **Use case**: TCFD compliance reporting, climate-risk analysis.

This branch is small (~2 variants) but growing as climate disclosure regulations expand.

## 10. Why So Many BERTs and So Few GPTs?

This is the chapter's central question. The answer has three layers:

### 10.1. The Architectural Layer

As explained in [[02b.2. Why BERT Is More Versatile — The Bidirectional Advantage]], BERT's bidirectional mask produces embeddings that are directly improved by domain adaptation. Each domain variant produces measurably better embeddings for that domain — a clear, quantifiable return on investment.

GPT's causal mask produces embeddings that are less central to GPT's use case (generation). Domain-adapting GPT produces a model that generates better domain text, but the gain is smaller and harder to measure because generation quality is subjective and prompt-dependent.

### 10.2. The Economic Layer

Fine-tuning BERT (110M parameters) costs ~$1,000 of compute. This is within the budget of a single research lab, so dozens of labs created dozens of variants.

Fine-tuning GPT-3 (175B parameters) costs ~$100,000+. This is within the budget of only large companies, so few domain GPT variants exist. The few that do exist (BioGPT, Galactica) are research projects, not production tools.

### 10.3. The Use-Case Layer

BERT's primary use case — **embedding production** — is repeatable and infrastructure-critical. A retrieval system runs millions of queries per day, each requiring an embedding. The embedding model must be small, fast, and domain-specialised. This creates sustained demand for domain BERT variants.

GPT's primary use case — **generation** — is less repeatable and less infrastructure-critical. A chatbot answers one query at a time, and the general GPT is "good enough" for most domains via prompting. There is less demand for domain-specific GPT variants because the general model suffices.

### 10.4. The Contrast

| Dimension | BERT Family | GPT Family |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Domain variants published (2018–2024) | 50+ | <5 (most are research-only) |
| Average cost to create a variant | ~$1,000 | ~$100,000+ |
| Primary use case driving variants | Embedding production (retrieval, NER, classification) | Generation (chat, code, creative writing) |
| Performance gain from domain adaptation | 3–10 F1 points (clear) | 1–3 points (marginal, noisy) |
| Production adoption of domain variants | High (search engines, legal-tech, fin-tech, clinical NLP) | Low (most use general GPT + prompting) |

The contrast is stark and is the empirical confirmation of the architectural and economic arguments.

## 11. The BERT Successor Architectures

The BERT family also includes architectural improvements that go beyond the original BERT-Base:

### 11.1. RoBERTa (Liu et al., 2019)

- **Change**: Removed NSP, trained longer with bigger batches, dynamic masking.
- **Result**: 2–5 point improvement over BERT on most benchmarks.
- **Status**: The de facto standard "BERT" for general English; most domain variants are now built on RoBERTa, not vanilla BERT.

### 11.2. ALBERT (Lan et al., 2019)

- **Change**: Factorised embeddings + parameter sharing across layers.
- **Result**: Fewer parameters, comparable performance.
- **Status**: Used in resource-constrained settings.

### 11.3. DeBERTa (He et al., 2020)

- **Change**: Disentangled attention (separate content and position embeddings).
- **Result**: 3–8 point improvement over RoBERTa.
- **Status**: Current state-of-the-art for encoder-only models on GLUE/SuperGLUE.

### 11.4. Longformer (Beltagy et al., 2020)

- **Change**: Sparse attention (sliding window + global tokens) for long documents.
- **Result**: Handles 4,096–16,384 token inputs (vs. BERT's 512 limit).
- **Status**: Used for long-document tasks (legal contracts, scientific papers, full patient records).

These successors are part of the broader "BERT family" — they share the encoder-only, bidirectional, MLM-trained architecture that defines the family.

## 12. What This Means for the Project

The project uses **four BERT variants** (BioBERT, PubMedBERT, ClinicalBERT, SapBERT) because:

1. **Different variants excel at different tasks** — BioBERT for sentence retrieval, PubMedBERT for entity matching, ClinicalBERT for raw notes, SapBERT for synonym alignment.
2. **The variants are cheap to run** (110M parameters each, single-GPU inference).
3. **The variants are complementary** — their Top-K lists are unioned, improving recall.
4. **The variants are domain-specialised** — each was created by domain adaptation on a specific medical corpus.

A GPT-based alternative (e.g., using GPT-4 for all four tasks) would be:

- **Slower** (each query requires an LLM API call vs. a local forward pass).
- **More expensive** (LLM API costs vs. free local inference).
- **Less accurate** (GPT-4's embeddings are worse than BioBERT's for medical retrieval).
- **Non-deterministic** (LLM APIs are stochastic even at temperature 0, unlike a fixed BERT model).

The BERT family's versatility is not just an academic curiosity — it is the architectural reason the project can run five specialised models in parallel at low cost, which is the foundation of the multi-embedding retrieval strategy.
