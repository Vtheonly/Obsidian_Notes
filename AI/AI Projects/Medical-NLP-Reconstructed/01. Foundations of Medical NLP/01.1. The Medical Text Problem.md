# 01.1. The Medical Text Problem

Clinical narrative is widely regarded as the **"final boss" of NLP**. Unlike Wikipedia prose or news articles, a clinical note compresses an enormous amount of decision-relevant information into a few telegraphic paragraphs. The difficulty is not a single obstacle but the intersection of five mutually reinforcing problems: domain-specific vocabulary, contextual ambiguity that breaks naive sentiment models, **negation scope resolution**, **temporality and experiencer attribution**, and **word-sense disambiguation** of clinical homonyms. Each of these must be solved before any embedding model can produce a reliable vector, which is why a hybrid architecture (LLM + BioBERT + Knowledge Graph) is required rather than a single end-to-end model.

This note unpacks each problem in depth, illustrates it with real clinical sentences, and connects it to the downstream pipeline components that resolve it. Read this chapter first — every later chapter presupposes the failure modes documented here.

---

## 1. Specialized Vocabulary and the Domain Gap

Medical language is built on **Latin and Greek roots** that are essentially absent from general-domain training corpora. Terms such as *Oculocutaneous Hypopigmentation*, *Sensorineural Hearing Loss*, *Hepatosplenomegaly*, or *Pancytopenia* are dense, multi-morphemic, and rare. A general model trained on Wikipedia + BookCorpus sees these tokens with extremely low frequency, so their learned representations are statistically noisy.

The mathematical consequence is severe: in a 768-dimensional embedding space, common words like *Skin* occupy a well-determined region because the model has seen millions of contextual occurrences, but rare medical terms sit in a poorly-determined "fuzzy ball" where small perturbations of context cause large swings in the vector. This is the **representation noise floor** that motivates the move to BioBERT / PubMedBERT (Chapter 3) — the only way to reduce the noise is to flood the model with PubMed text so the rare terms become statistically well-determined.

A second, subtler consequence is **morphological productivity**. New disease names, gene symbols, and drug names are coined continuously. A model with a fixed 30k-token vocabulary cannot keep up. This is exactly the failure mode that motivates **subword tokenization** (Chapter 2.3) and the historical transition through **FastText** (Chapter 1.2) — both of which let the model compose unseen terms from reusable morphemes.

## 2. Contextual Ambiguity and the "Sentiment Trap"

In everyday English, *Positive* connotes success and *Negative* connotes failure. In clinical narrative, the polarity is inverted:

- *"Patient **positive** for hypertrophic cardiomyopathy"* — bad news; disease is present.
- *"Negative for chest pain"* — good news; symptom is absent.

A general-purpose sentence encoder will route *Positive* toward the *Happy / Good / Success* region of the vector space. When this vector is then used for similarity matching against an Orphanet disease definition, the spurious "goodness" signal corrupts the cosine score. The same problem affects *Negative*, *Depression* (mood vs. anatomical depression of a bone), *Cell* (phone vs. biological), *Stem* (plant vs. hematopoietic), and dozens of other high-frequency homonyms.

The standard solution is **domain adaptation via fine-tuning** (Chapter 3.4): by continuing pre-training on PubMed, the model's attention weights for these terms are pulled away from the lay meaning and toward the clinical meaning. This is the mathematical "semantic shift" that BioBERT performs on the vector space.

## 3. Negation Scope Resolution (Critical Gap)

The original vault treated negation as a **binary keyword swap** ("Negative vs. Positive"). This is dangerously insufficient. Clinical narrative routinely contains **multi-clause sentences with mixed polarity**, where a single negation operator scopes over several phenotypes but explicitly does *not* scope over a contrasting positive finding:

> *"Patient denied chest pain, shortness of breath, and palpitations, but reported mild nausea."*

Here the operator *denied* scopes over the first three phenotypes but **must not** extend to *nausea*. A naive keyword or token-vector approach has no notion of scope: it either marks everything in the sentence as negated (losing *nausea*) or marks nothing as negated (inserting three false-positive symptoms). Either error corrupts the downstream knowledge graph.

Three families of algorithms address this properly, in order of sophistication:

1. **NegEx (Chapman et al., 2001)** — a rule-based system that uses trigger terms (*denied*, *no*, *negative for*, *without*), a list of UMLS clinical concepts, and a fixed window to assign a *definitely negated / possibly negated / not negated* label to each concept.
2. **ConText / ConText-Java** — generalizes NegEx with three semantic categories (Negation, Temporality, Experiencer) and a forward/backward scope window determined by section headers and punctuation.
3. **Dependency-parse traversal** — modern approach: parse the sentence with a clinical spaCy model (`en_core_sci_sm` or `med7`), then walk the dependency tree from each negation trigger to its syntactic governor and propagate the negation label to all governed noun phrases. This handles arbitrarily long scopes correctly because it follows the actual syntactic structure of the sentence.

In this project's pipeline, **negation scope resolution happens inside the LLM Clinical Cleaner** (Chapter 5.1). The system prompt instructs the model to output an `assertion` field per extracted phenotype, with values in `{present, absent, family_history, historical}`. Because the LLM has implicit syntactic understanding, it correctly handles the example above by emitting:

```json
[
  {"phenotype": "HP:0000728", "label": "Chest pain",        "assertion": "absent"},
  {"phenotype": "HP:0002093", "label": "Dyspnea",           "assertion": "absent"},
  {"phenotype": "HP:0001962", "label": "Palpitations",      "assertion": "absent"},
  {"phenotype": "HP:0002017", "label": "Nausea",            "assertion": "present"}
]
```

The downstream graph builder (Chapter 8) then drops all `absent` phenotypes from the patient subgraph. Without this step, *denied chest pain* would be encoded as a positive symptom and the cosine similarity with cardiac diseases would explode — a textbook clinical NLP failure.

## 4. Temporality and Experiencer Attribution (Critical Gap)

The original vault assumed that any entity extracted from a clinical note represents an **active, current symptom of the patient**. This assumption is catastrophically wrong in real clinical narrative, which is dense with historical references and family histories:

> *"Father has a history of Huntington Disease; patient currently exhibits no chorea."*

If the LLM extracts `Huntington Disease` and `Chorea` without also recording **who** has the condition (the Experiencer) and **when** (the Temporality), the patient's record will be corrupted with an inherited disease they do not have. This is not a hypothetical: misattribution of family history is one of the most common sources of diagnostic error in rare-disease triage.

The clinical NLP literature models this with a four-dimensional **assertion schema**:

| Dimension | Values | Example |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Polarity** | `present` / `absent` | "Patient has fever" vs. "No fever" |
| **Temporality** | `current` / `historical` / `hypothetical` | "Has fever" vs. "Had fever last year" vs. "May develop fever" |
| **Experiencer** | `patient` / `family_member` / `other` | "Patient has fever" vs. "Father has Huntington Disease" |
| **Certainty** | `certain` / `probable` / `possible` | "Has fever" vs. "Likely has fever" vs. "Cannot rule out fever" |

In this project's pipeline, the LLM Clinical Cleaner (Chapter 5.1) is prompted to emit all four dimensions per phenotype. The Knowledge Graph (Chapter 8) only inserts `current + patient + certain` phenotypes into the patient subgraph; family-history phenotypes are stored on a separate `FamilyMember` node and used only for inheritance-pattern reasoning (e.g., autosomal dominant diseases where a parent's diagnosis is itself diagnostic evidence for the proband).

## 5. Syntactic Density and Doctor Shorthand

Doctors write telegraphically to save time. Verbs and subjects are routinely omitted, and abbreviations are ubiquitous:

> *"Pt c/o SOB on exertion, PMH of CHF, NKA. Pt started on Lasix 40mg PO daily."*

The full expansion is:

> *"Patient complains of shortness of breath on exertion. Past medical history of congestive heart failure. No known allergies. Patient started on furosemide (Lasix) 40 milligrams by mouth daily."*

Standard NLP parsers (spaCy, NLTK, CoreNLP) trained on Wikipedia / web text expect subject–verb–object structure. They mis-segment clinical shorthand, mis-tag abbreviations as nouns, and produce garbage dependency trees. Three concrete consequences:

1. **NegEx-style rule systems** that depend on syntactic boundaries frequently fail because the boundaries are missing.
2. **Coreference resolution** — needed to know that *Pt* and *Patient* refer to the same entity — fails because the parser cannot identify the antecedent.
3. **Abbreviation expansion** is required before any ontology lookup, otherwise `SOB` matches nothing in HPO and `PMH` matches nothing in MONDO.

The pipeline therefore performs the following normalization **before** any embedding or graph operation:

- Expand abbreviations using the **UMLS SPECIALIST Lexicon** and a project-specific acronym table (Chapter 7.3).
- Detect and expand eponyms and drug brand names (Lasix → furosemide) using RxNorm.
- Sentence-boundary detection tuned for clinical text (the `pysbd` library with a clinical extension, or a fine-tuned `medspacy` sentence splitter).

Only after this normalization does the LLM Cleaner see the text — at which point it can correctly extract phenotypes, assertions, and experiencer attributions.

## 6. Word-Sense Disambiguation (WSD) for Clinical Homonyms

The original vault assumed that the LLM Cleaner resolves all homonyms. This is too optimistic — LLMs still make disambiguation errors, and the pipeline needs an explicit backstop. Consider the abbreviation **APC**, which has at least three clinically distinct meanings:

| Expansion | Type | Source Ontology |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| *Adenomatous Polyposis Coli* | Gene | HGNC |
| *Activated Protein C* | Protein | UniProt |
| *Argon Plasma Coagulation* | Procedure | SNOMED-CT |

A vector embedding of the bare token `APC` will sit somewhere in the average of these three semantic regions — useless for retrieval. The pipeline resolves this with a **two-stage WSD backstop**:

1. **Context-driven expansion by the LLM.** The system prompt instructs the model: *"Expand abbreviations using surrounding context. If context is insufficient, do not expand — leave the abbreviation as-is and flag it as `ambiguous`."*
2. **Lexical backstop.** For terms flagged `ambiguous`, the pipeline consults the **UMLS SPECIALIST Lexicon** and the **MedlinePlus Health Topics** dictionary to score candidate expansions by co-occurrence with surrounding tokens. The highest-scoring expansion wins; ties are surfaced for human review.

This two-stage approach is necessary because LLMs are not deterministic WSD oracles (Chapter 6.3 on temperature and reproducibility) and because the cost of a wrong expansion is a corrupted graph node. The Knowledge Graph itself uses **CUIs** (Chapter 3.6) as the unambiguous pivot, so even if a homonym survives the cleaner, it will only match a single CUI in the graph and the ambiguity is contained.

## 7. Why a Hybrid Architecture Is Required

Each of the five problems above has a preferred tool:

| Problem | Best Tool | Note |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Specialized vocabulary | BioBERT / PubMedBERT | Chapter 3 |
| Sentiment polarity inversion | Domain-adapted embeddings | Chapter 3.4 |
| Negation scope | LLM Cleaner + dependency parse | Chapter 5.1 |
| Temporality / Experiencer | LLM Cleaner + 4-D assertion schema | Chapter 5.1 |
| Syntactic density & abbreviations | medspacy + UMLS SPECIALIST Lexicon | Chapter 7.3 |
| Word-sense disambiguation | LLM Cleaner + UMLS backstop | Chapters 5.1 & 7.3 |

No single model handles all five. A pure LLM hallucinates and is non-deterministic; a pure BioBERT cannot extract structured assertions; a pure rule-based pipeline dies on novel abbreviations. The **Unified Medical Knowledge Architecture** combines all three layers: LLM for extraction, BioBERT for retrieval, Knowledge Graph for verification. The remainder of this vault explains how each layer works and how they interlock.
