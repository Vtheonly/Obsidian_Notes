# 01.2. The Evolution of NLP Vectors

Understanding why the project uses **BioBERT** requires understanding the historical progression of how computers learned to "read" — and, crucially, **why each generation was forced into existence by a hard mathematical failure of the previous one**. Skipping this history leaves the reader with a black-box model: they know BioBERT works, but they cannot diagnose why a particular patient note matches the wrong disease. This note walks through five generations, each defined by a specific failure mode that motivated the next.

---

## 1. Bag-of-Words (BoW, ~1998–2010)

The earliest representation treated a document as an unordered multiset of words. The vector had one dimension per vocabulary term, and the value in each dimension was the raw count.

- **Logic**: If "Fever" appears 3 times in a note, the vector has a `3` at the "Fever" index.
- **Failure 1 — Order blindness**: *"The dog bit the man"* and *"The man bit the dog"* produce identical vectors. In a clinical setting, *"Patient denies fever"* and *"Fever denies patient"* are indistinguishable.
- **Failure 2 — Synonym blindness**: *"Heart Attack"* and *"Myocardial Infarction"* have zero overlap, even though they are the same entity. A BoW-based retriever cannot match a patient note to an Orphanet definition that uses the medical term.

BoW is still useful as a baseline (e.g., for fast keyword indexing) but it is fundamentally incompatible with semantic retrieval.

---

## 2. TF-IDF (Term Frequency × Inverse Document Frequency, ~1972–2015)

TF-IDF adds a single crucial idea: **down-weight common words, up-weight rare words**. The weight of term $t$ in document $d$ is:

$$\text{tfidf}(t, d) = \text{tf}(t, d) \times \log\!\left(\frac{N}{\text{df}(t)}\right)$$

where $N$ is the total document count and $\text{df}(t)$ is the number of documents containing $t$.

- **What it fixes**: Common words like *the*, *is*, *and* (which dominate BoW vectors) are suppressed. Rare, technically loaded words like *Hypopigmentation* or *Oculocutaneous* get high weights, which is exactly what a clinical retriever wants.
- **What it does NOT fix**: Synonyms are still disjoint. The two axes *Heart* and *Cardiac* are completely orthogonal in TF-IDF space. There is **no geometric relationship** between words.

TF-IDF is still the right tool for keyword search (e.g., Elasticsearch lookups in the Knowledge Graph's lucene index), but it cannot compute semantic similarity.

---

## 3. Static Embeddings: Word2Vec and GloVe (2013–2017)

Word2Vec (Mikolov 2013) and GloVe (Pennington 2014) were the first neural approaches to give words **geometric meaning**. Each word is mapped to a single fixed vector in a 100- to 300-dimensional space, learned by predicting context words from a target word (skip-gram) or vice versa (CBOW). The vectors satisfy striking linear-algebraic regularities:

$$\vec{v}(\text{King}) - \vec{v}(\text{Man}) + \vec{v}(\text{Woman}) \approx \vec{v}(\text{Queen})$$

For the first time, *Cardiac* and *Heart* could be near-neighbours in the same space, and a cosine similarity between two notes could exceed pure keyword overlap.

### 3.1. The Three Hard Failures of Static Embeddings

**Failure 1 — Polysemy is unresolved.** The vector for *Bank* is a single point, but the word has two completely unrelated meanings (river vs. financial). In a clinical setting, *Cold* can mean a viral infection (URTI) or a temperature sensation. A static model assigns one vector that is the **centroid of all contexts** — useless for retrieval.

**Failure 2 — Out-of-Vocabulary (OOV) terms return nothing.** Static embeddings have a fixed dictionary. Rare disease names, gene symbols, novel drug names, and typos (*Oculocutanous* instead of *Oculocutaneous*) simply do not appear in the vocabulary, and the model has no mechanism to produce a vector. The clinical domain is overrun with OOV terms; this is a fatal limitation.

**Failure 3 — No morphological awareness.** Word2Vec treats *Hypopigmentation*, *Hypopigmented*, and *Hypopigmentations* as three independent tokens. The model has no way to know they share a morphological root, so each must be learned independently from co-occurrence statistics — which is impossible for rare terms that occur only a handful of times.

---

## 4. FastText: The Subword Bridge (2016, Bojanowski et al.)

FastText is the **historically and technically critical bridge** between static embeddings and Transformers, and the original vault omitted it entirely. FastText extends Word2Vec by representing each word as a **bag of character n-grams** plus the whole-word token. For a word $w$ with n-gram set $G_w$:

$$\vec{v}(w) = \sum_{g \in G_w} \vec{z}_g$$

where each $\vec{z}_g$ is a learned vector for the n-gram $g$. For example, *Hypopigmentation* (with $n=3$) is represented as the sum of vectors for `<hy`, `hyp`, `ypo`, `pop`, `opi`, `pig`, …, `ion`, `on>`.

### 4.1. What FastText Fixes

- **OOV is no longer fatal.** A new word *Oculocutaneous* is decomposed into character n-grams, most of which (`ocu`, `cul`, `cut`, `tan`, `ane`) have been seen in other medical words. The model produces a reasonable vector by summing the n-gram vectors.
- **Morphology is shared.** *Hypopigmentation*, *Hypopigmented*, and *Hypopigmentations* share most of their n-grams, so their vectors are similar by construction.
- **Typos are tolerated.** *Oculocutanous* (one missing *e*) still shares 14 of its 15 n-grams with the correct spelling, so the cosine similarity is high.

### 4.2. What FastText Still Does NOT Fix

- **Polysemy is still unresolved.** *Cold* still has one vector.
- **No contextual composition.** The vector for *chest pain* is just the sum of the vectors for *chest* and *pain* — there is no notion of how the two words interact syntactically.

FastText is therefore the **direct conceptual ancestor** of subword tokenizers (WordPiece, BPE) used in BERT — the idea that words can be decomposed into reusable units is what makes 30k-token vocabularies able to cover millions of medical terms. But to get context, we need attention.

---

## 5. The Transformer Revolution (2017, Vaswani et al.)

The Transformer architecture introduced **self-attention**, which lets each word's vector be a function of every other word in the sentence. The vector for *Bank* in *"The river bank was muddy"* is now genuinely different from the vector for *Bank* in *"The bank approved the loan"*. Polysemy is resolved by context.

BERT (Devlin et al., 2018) packaged the Transformer encoder with two pre-training objectives — Masked Language Modeling and Next Sentence Prediction — and produced the first 768-dimensional contextual embeddings that could be downloaded and applied to any downstream task via fine-tuning. BERT is the direct ancestor of every model in Chapter 3.

### 5.1. What Transformers Fix

- **Polysemy.** The vector for *Cold* in *"patient has a cold"* differs from its vector in *"extremities cold to touch"*.
- **Long-range context.** *"Patient denied chest pain, shortness of breath, and palpitations, but reported mild nausea"* — the negation scope over *pain / dyspnea / palpitations* and the positive scope over *nausea* are both captured by attention weights.
- **Syntactic awareness.** Attention heads specialise for syntactic roles (subject, object, modifier) without explicit supervision.

### 5.2. What Transformers Still Do NOT Fix (and motivate the rest of the vault)

- **Domain vocabulary.** Out-of-the-box BERT trained on Wikipedia still fragments rare medical terms because its 30k vocabulary was not built for medicine. This motivates BioBERT / PubMedBERT (Chapter 3).
- **Synonym alignment.** BERT does not know that *High BP*, *Hypertension*, and *Elevated blood pressure* should map to the same point. This motivates SapBERT and contrastive learning (Chapter 3.2).
- **Anisotropy.** Even BioBERT's embeddings cluster in a narrow cone of $\mathbb{R}^{768}$, producing artificially high cosine similarities. This is the "0.9 Trap" of Chapter 3.3.
- **Factual verification.** A Transformer can output a confident, fluent, and *hallucinated* diagnosis. Embeddings produce "vibes"; they do not verify biological truth. This motivates the Knowledge Graph layer (Chapter 8).

---

## 6. Summary: The Mathematical Inheritance

Each generation of vector representation solved a precise mathematical failure of the previous one:

| Generation | Year | Key Failure Solved | Key Failure Remaining |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| BoW | ~1998 | (baseline) | Order, synonyms, OOV |
| TF-IDF | ~1972 | Common-word dominance | Synonyms, OOV, morphology |
| Word2Vec / GloVe | 2013 | Synonyms, geometric meaning | Polysemy, OOV, morphology |
| FastText | 2016 | OOV, morphology | Polysemy, context |
| Transformer / BERT | 2018 | Polysemy, context | Domain vocabulary, anisotropy, verification |
| BioBERT / PubMedBERT | 2019–2020 | Domain vocabulary | Anisotropy, verification, synonym alignment |
| SapBERT | 2020 | Synonym alignment | Anisotropy, verification |
| This project's hybrid | — | Anisotropy (via Phase-2 ranker), verification (via KG) | — |

Reading the vault in order, every later chapter picks up exactly the failure that the previous layer left behind. BioBERT does not replace BERT — it inherits BERT's grammar and specializes the vocabulary. The Knowledge Graph does not replace BioBERT — it inherits BioBERT's retrieval and adds verification. The Neural Ranker does not replace the Knowledge Graph — it inherits the graph's confirmed candidates and resolves the residual cosine-tie problem. **Each layer is a response to a documented failure of the previous one.**
