# 09.1. Learning-to-Rank — Pointwise, Pairwise, Listwise

The original vault's Chapter 9.1 compared "retrieval vs. ranking" qualitatively but did not place the project's pairwise approach in the broader **Learning-to-Rank (LTR)** taxonomy. This is a critical gap: without knowing the three LTR families (pointwise, pairwise, listwise) and their tradeoffs, the reader cannot evaluate **why** pairwise was chosen over listwise, and cannot diagnose when a different LTR approach would be better.

This note covers: (1) the two-stage retrieve-then-rank paradigm, (2) the three LTR families with their mathematical formulations, (3) the tradeoffs (loss function, complexity, optimisation target), (4) why the project chose pairwise, (5) when listwise (LambdaMART) would be better, and (6) the relationship to the Neural Ranker architecture.

---

## 1. The Two-Stage Retrieve-then-Rank Paradigm

The project's architecture follows the standard **two-stage retrieve-then-rank** paradigm used by Google Search, Netflix recommendations, and most production ranking systems:

- **Stage 1 (Retrieval)**: Narrow 10,000 candidates to Top-20 using a fast, pointwise scoring function (cosine similarity). Optimised for **recall** and **speed**.
- **Stage 2 (Ranking)**: Re-order the Top-20 using a slower, more expressive model (Neural Ranker). Optimised for **precision** and **order**.

### 1.1. Why Two Stages?

A single end-to-end model that scores all 10,000 candidates with the full Neural Ranker would be too slow (~10,000 neural network forward passes per query). The two-stage architecture uses the cheap pointwise scorer to do the bulk of the filtering, then applies the expensive pairwise ranker only to the surviving 20 candidates.

### 1.2. Why Stage 2 Is Necessary

Stage 1 (cosine similarity) is a **pointwise** metric: it scores each candidate independently against the query. It never compares candidates to each other. This means it cannot resolve the "0.9 trap" (Chapter 3.3) — if Disease A and Disease B both have cosine 0.92 with the patient note, Stage 1 cannot decide which is better.

Stage 2 is a **pairwise** model: it explicitly compares candidates to each other, learning the fine-grained boundary that separates the correct disease from its closest mimics. This is the architectural reason Stage 2 exists.

## 2. The Three LTR Families

Learning-to-Rank algorithms are classified by **what they optimise**:

### 2.1. Pointwise

The model scores each candidate independently and the loss is computed per candidate.

**Scoring function**: $f(q, d) \in \mathbb{R}$ — a single real-valued score for the (query, candidate) pair.

**Loss**: Regression or classification loss on individual scores. For example, MSE against a relevance label:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{pointwise}} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N (f(q_i, d_i) - y_i)^2$$

where $y_i$ is the relevance label (e.g., 0 = irrelevant, 1 = relevant, 2 = highly relevant).

**Examples**: Cosine similarity, BM25, any regression model.

**Pros**: Simple, fast, scales to millions of candidates.

**Cons**: Does not optimise the **order** of the results. Two candidates with the same score are tied, even if one is actually more relevant.

### 2.2. Pairwise

The model compares pairs of candidates and the loss is computed per pair.

**Scoring function**: $f(q, d_i, d_j) \in [0, 1]$ — the probability that $d_i$ is more relevant than $d_j$ for query $q$.

**Loss**: Binary cross-entropy on pair outcomes:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{pairwise}} = -\frac{1}{P}\sum_{(i,j) \in P} \left[y_{ij} \log f(q, d_i, d_j) + (1 - y_{ij}) \log(1 - f(q, d_i, d_j))\right]$$

where $y_{ij} = 1$ if $d_i$ is more relevant than $d_j$, and $P$ is the set of pairs.

**Examples**: RankNet (Burges et al., 2005), the project's Neural Ranker.

**Pros**: Directly optimises the order. Resolves ties by learning the fine-grained boundary.

**Cons**: Quadratic in the number of candidates ($O(K^2)$ pairs for $K$ candidates). Does not directly optimise ranking metrics (NDCG, MRR).

### 2.3. Listwise

The model scores the entire ranked list at once and the loss is computed on the list's ranking quality.

**Scoring function**: $f(q, [d_1, \dots, d_K]) \in \mathbb{R}^K$ — a vector of scores for the entire list.

**Loss**: A surrogate of a ranking metric (NDCG, MAP). For example, LambdaMART's gradient is weighted by the change in NDCG that would result from swapping each pair:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{listwise}} = \sum_{i < j} |\Delta \text{NDCG}_{ij}| \cdot \log(1 + e^{-(s_i - s_j)})$$

where $s_i$ is the score of candidate $i$ and $|\Delta \text{NDCG}_{ij}|$ is the NDCG change from swapping $i$ and $j$.

**Examples**: LambdaMART (Burges, 2010), ListNet (Cao et al., 2007), SoftRank (Taylor et al., 2008).

**Pros**: Directly optimises the ranking metric. State-of-the-art on most LTR benchmarks.

**Cons**: Complex to implement. Requires gradient computation through the ranking metric. More sensitive to hyperparameters.

## 3. Tradeoff Comparison

| Dimension | Pointwise | Pairwise | Listwise |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Loss target** | Individual scores | Pair outcomes | List ranking metric |
| **Complexity per query** | $O(K)$ | $O(K^2)$ | $O(K^2)$ or $O(K \log K)$ |
| **Optimises order?** | No | Yes (implicitly) | Yes (explicitly) |
| **Optimises NDCG?** | No | No | Yes |
| **Implementation difficulty** | Easy | Medium | Hard |
| **Data requirement** | Relevance labels | Pair labels | List-level labels |
| **SOTA on benchmarks?** | No | No | Yes |

### 3.1. The NDCG Gap

The key insight is that **pairwise does not directly optimise NDCG**. It optimises pair outcomes, which is correlated with NDCG but not identical. A pairwise model can produce a ranking that is good on pair accuracy but suboptimal on NDCG (e.g., it may not prioritise moving the most relevant item to position 1).

Listwise methods (LambdaMART) directly optimise NDCG and are therefore state-of-the-art on ranking benchmarks. The tradeoff is implementation complexity and sensitivity to hyperparameters.

---

## 4. Why the Project Chose Pairwise

The project's Neural Ranker is a **pairwise** model. The choice is justified by four considerations:

### 4.1. Small Candidate Set

The project's Stage 2 operates on $K = 20$ candidates. The pairwise cost is $K(K-1) = 380$ pairs per query — tractable. For $K = 1000$, pairwise would be 1M pairs — intractable, and listwise would be preferred.

### 4.2. Binary Relevance Labels

The project's ground truth is binary: each candidate is either the correct disease or not. There is no graded relevance (no "highly relevant" vs. "somewhat relevant"). Pairwise loss is well-suited to binary labels; listwise's NDCG advantage is smaller for binary labels.

### 4.3. Implementation Simplicity

Pairwise is much simpler to implement than listwise. The Neural Ranker is a standard MLP with BCE loss — a few hundred lines of PyTorch. LambdaMART requires gradient computation through NDCG, which is substantially more complex.

### 4.4. Sufficient Performance

On the project's test set, the pairwise ranker achieves Top-1 accuracy of 0.91. A listwise ranker might achieve 0.92–0.93 (per literature comparisons), but the 1–2 point improvement does not justify the implementation complexity for the project's current scope.

## 5. When Listwise (LambdaMART) Would Be Better

The project would migrate to a listwise ranker if:

### 5.1. Graded Relevance Becomes Necessary

If the ground truth evolves from binary ("correct disease / wrong disease") to graded ("definitely correct / probably correct / possibly correct / wrong"), listwise's NDCG optimisation becomes more valuable. Pairwise loss treats all pairs equally, which is suboptimal when some pairs are more important than others.

### 5.2. The Candidate Set Grows

If $K$ grows from 20 to 100+, the pairwise $O(K^2)$ cost becomes significant. Listwise methods can achieve similar performance with $O(K \log K)$ cost by optimising the list directly.

### 5.3. NDCG Becomes the Primary Metric

If the project's evaluation shifts from Top-1 accuracy to NDCG (Chapter 10.2), a listwise ranker that directly optimises NDCG would be preferred. This is common in production search systems where the user cares about the entire ranked list, not just position 1.

### 5.4. Production Deployment at Scale

For production deployment with millions of queries per day, the listwise ranker's better NDCG per query compounds into a substantial overall improvement. The implementation cost is amortised over the larger query volume.

## 6. The Relationship to the Neural Ranker Architecture

The project's Neural Ranker (Chapter 9.3) implements the pairwise approach:

- **Input**: Three vectors — the patient note $\mathbf{n}$, candidate $A$ $\mathbf{a}$, candidate $B$ $\mathbf{b}$.
- **Interaction features**: Vector difference $\mathbf{a} - \mathbf{b}$, Hadamard products $\mathbf{a} \odot \mathbf{b}$, $\mathbf{n} \odot \mathbf{a}$, $\mathbf{n} \odot \mathbf{b}$.
- **Output**: A single probability $P(A > B) \in [0, 1]$ via Sigmoid.
- **Loss**: Binary cross-entropy on the pair label.

This is the standard pairwise architecture (RankNet-style), with the addition of interaction features that help the model learn the fine-grained boundary between similar candidates.

### 6.1. What the Ranker Learns

The ranker learns a **non-linear decision boundary** in the interaction-feature space. This boundary encodes the project's clinical knowledge: which feature combinations (e.g., "matched phenotype count + gene overlap + cosine similarity") predict that one disease is more relevant than another.

The boundary is non-linear because no linear combination of cosine similarities can resolve the "0.9 trap" — the ranker must learn that certain feature patterns (e.g., high gene overlap + high phenotype overlap + moderate cosine) are more predictive than others, even when the cosine scores are similar.

### 6.2. Why This Is Better Than Pointwise

A pointwise ranker would score each candidate independently: $f(\mathbf{n}, \mathbf{a})$ and $f(\mathbf{n}, \mathbf{b})$. It cannot learn that *"Disease A has the FBN1 gene that the patient's note mentions, while Disease B does not"* — this is a **comparative** feature, not an absolute one. The pairwise ranker's interaction features ($\mathbf{a} - \mathbf{b}$, $\mathbf{a} \odot \mathbf{b}$) make this comparison explicit.

This is the deep architectural reason the project uses pairwise rather than pointwise ranking: the diagnostic signal is often in the **difference** between candidates, not in any candidate's absolute score.

## 7. Summary

| LTR Family | Loss | Complexity | Optimises NDCG? | Project Use |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Pointwise | Per-candidate | $O(K)$ | No | Stage 1 retrieval (cosine) |
| **Pairwise** | Per-pair (BCE) | $O(K^2)$ | No (but correlated) | **Stage 2 ranking (Neural Ranker)** |
| Listwise | Per-list (NDCG surrogate) | $O(K^2)$ or $O(K \log K)$ | Yes | Not used (future migration candidate) |

The project's choice of pairwise ranking is justified by the small candidate set ($K = 20$), binary labels, implementation simplicity, and sufficient performance. Migration to listwise (LambdaMART) is a future option if the requirements change.
