# 08.3. Beyond Jaccard — Asymmetric and Hierarchical Similarity

The original vault's Chapter 8.3 recommended **Jaccard Similarity** to compare a patient's extracted phenotypes to a disease's phenotypic profile. This recommendation has two critical flaws that the audit identified:

1. **Jaccard ignores the ontological hierarchy.** If the patient has `HP:0000639` (Nystagmus) and the disease is annotated with `HP:0000490` (Abnormal voluntary eye movements — the parent of Nystagmus), Jaccard counts these as disjoint (intersection = 0) even though they are hierarchically related.
2. **Jaccard is symmetric and penalises size mismatch.** A patient note with 2 phenotypes vs. a disease with 40 phenotypes gets a Jaccard score of $2/40 = 0.05$ even if both patient phenotypes match the disease perfectly. The correct disease is discarded.

This note covers: (1) the Jaccard formula and its two flaws, (2) the **Overlap Coefficient** (asymmetric, for size-mismatched sets), (3) **Information Content (IC)** and the **Resnik similarity** (hierarchical), (4) the **Lin similarity** (normalised Resnik), (5) the **Jiang-Conrath similarity** (distance-based), and (6) the project's combined similarity metric.

---

## 1. The Jaccard Formula and Its Two Flaws

### 1.1. The Formula

$$J(A, B) = \frac{|A \cap B|}{|A \cup B|}$$

where $A$ is the patient's phenotype set and $B$ is the disease's phenotype set.

### 1.2. Flaw 1: Hierarchical Blindness

Jaccard treats all elements as orthogonal. If:

- $A = \{\text{HP:0000639 (Nystagmus)}\}$
- $B = \{\text{HP:0000490 (Abnormal voluntary eye movements)}\}$

then $A \cap B = \emptyset$ and $J(A, B) = 0$, even though Nystagmus **is-a** Abnormal voluntary eye movements in the HPO DAG (Chapter 7.1). The two terms are hierarchically related, but Jaccard cannot see this.

### 1.3. Flaw 2: Size-Mismatch Penalty

Jaccard is symmetric and penalises size discrepancy. If:

- $A = \{\text{HP:0000639, HP:0001010}\}$ (patient, 2 phenotypes)
- $B = \{\text{HP:0000639, HP:0001010, HP:0000572, \dots, \text{HP:000XXXX}\}$ (disease, 40 phenotypes)

then $A \cap B = 2$ and $A \cup B = 40$, so $J(A, B) = 2/40 = 0.05$. The patient's 2 phenotypes are perfectly matched by the disease, but Jaccard reports a near-zero score because the disease has many other phenotypes the patient does not have.

In rare-disease diagnosis, this is catastrophic: the patient note is short (2 phenotypes), the disease profile is long (40 phenotypes), and the correct disease is discarded because of size mismatch.

---

## 2. The Overlap Coefficient — Asymmetric Set Similarity

The **Overlap Coefficient** (Szymkiewicz-Simpson coefficient) is an asymmetric set-similarity metric that measures subset containment:

$$\text{Overlap}(A, B) = \frac{|A \cap B|}{\min(|A|, |B|)}$$

### 2.1. Why Asymmetric Is Correct for Patient → Disease

For the size-mismatch example:

- $A$ = patient (2 phenotypes), $B$ = disease (40 phenotypes), $|A \cap B| = 2$.
- $\text{Overlap}(A, B) = 2 / \min(2, 40) = 2 / 2 = 1.0$.

The Overlap Coefficient reports **1.0** (perfect match) because all of the patient's phenotypes are in the disease's profile. The fact that the disease has 38 additional phenotypes does not penalise the score — those are just phenotypes the patient has not yet exhibited or that were not mentioned in the note.

### 2.2. Interpretation

$\text{Overlap}(A, B) = 1.0$ means "$A$ is a subset of $B$." In the clinical setting, this means "every phenotype the patient has is also a phenotype of the disease" — which is exactly the condition we want to detect.

### 2.3. When to Use Overlap vs. Jaccard

- **Jaccard**: When $A$ and $B$ are expected to be roughly the same size (e.g., comparing two disease profiles).
- **Overlap**: When $A$ is expected to be a subset of $B$ (e.g., comparing a short patient note to a long disease profile).

The project uses **Overlap** for patient → disease comparison (the size-mismatch case) and Jaccard for disease → disease comparison (the symmetric case).

### 2.4. Overlap Does Not Fix Hierarchical Blindness

The Overlap Coefficient still operates on flat sets. If the patient has `HP:0000639` and the disease has `HP:0000490`, the intersection is still 0. To fix hierarchical blindness, we need **Information Content**.

---

## 3. Information Content (IC) — Quantifying Specificity

**Information Content (IC)** measures how specific a phenotype is, based on its frequency in the disease corpus:

$$\text{IC}(c) = -\log P(c) = -\log \frac{\text{freq}(c)}{N}$$

where $P(c)$ is the probability of encountering phenotype $c$ in the disease corpus, $\text{freq}(c)$ is the number of diseases annotated with $c$ (or any of its descendants), and $N$ is the total number of disease-phenotype annotations.

### 3.1. Interpretation

- A **rare, specific phenotype** (e.g., *Nystagmus*, annotated in 50 of 10,000 diseases) has high IC: $\text{IC} = -\log(50/10000) \approx 5.3$.
- A **common, generic phenotype** (e.g., *Fever*, annotated in 5000 of 10,000 diseases) has low IC: $\text{IC} = -\log(5000/10000) \approx 0.69$.

High-IC phenotypes are **diagnostically informative** because they narrow the differential. Low-IC phenotypes are uninformative because they are consistent with many diseases.

### 3.2. IC Is Computed from the Corpus

IC is not a property of the ontology alone; it depends on the **annotation corpus** (which diseases are annotated with which phenotypes). The HPO project publishes IC values computed from Orphanet annotations, and these are what the project uses.

### 3.3. IC Propagates Up the Hierarchy

The frequency of a phenotype $c$ includes annotations to any of its descendants. So $\text{freq}(\text{Abnormal Eye Movement})$ includes all diseases annotated with *Nystagmus*, *Oscillopsia*, *Saccadic Pursuit*, etc. This means parent phenotypes (more general) have higher frequency and lower IC, while child phenotypes (more specific) have lower frequency and higher IC.

---

## 4. Resnik Similarity — Hierarchical Similarity via MICA

**Resnik similarity** (Resnik, 1995) is the foundational hierarchical similarity metric for ontologies. It is based on the **Most Informative Common Ancestor (MICA)**:

$$\text{Sim}_{\text{Resnik}}(c_1, c_2) = \text{IC}(\text{MICA}(c_1, c_2))$$

where $\text{MICA}(c_1, c_2)$ is the common ancestor of $c_1$ and $c_2$ with the highest IC (i.e., the most specific common ancestor).

### 4.1. Worked Example

Patient has `HP:0000639` (Nystagmus). Disease is annotated with `HP:0000490` (Abnormal voluntary eye movements).

- Ancestors of Nystagmus: {Abnormal Eye Movement, Abnormality of Eye, Phenotypic Abnormality}.
- Ancestors of Abnormal voluntary eye movements: {Abnormality of Eye, Phenotypic Abnormality}.
- Common ancestors: {Abnormality of Eye, Phenotypic Abnormality}.
- MICA: *Abnormality of Eye* (higher IC than *Phenotypic Abnormality*).
- $\text{Sim}_{\text{Resnik}} = \text{IC}(\text{Abnormality of Eye})$.

So even though the two phenotypes are not identical, Resnik similarity is non-zero (and is determined by how specific their common ancestor is).

### 4.2. Properties of Resnik Similarity

- **Range**: $[0, \infty)$ — unbounded, because IC is unbounded.
- **Symmetric**: $\text{Sim}_{\text{Resnik}}(c_1, c_2) = \text{Sim}_{\text{Resnik}}(c_2, c_1)$.
- **Identity**: $\text{Sim}_{\text{Resnik}}(c, c) = \text{IC}(c)$ — the similarity of a term to itself is its own IC.

### 4.3. Limitation: No Upper Bound

Resnik similarity is unbounded, which makes it hard to interpret. A score of 5.3 is "high" but a score of 7.0 is "higher" — but how much higher? The Lin similarity (next section) normalises Resnik to $[0, 1]$.

---

## 5. Lin Similarity — Normalised Resnik

**Lin similarity** (Lin, 1998) normalises Resnik by the sum of the individual ICs:

$$\text{Sim}_{\text{Lin}}(c_1, c_2) = \frac{2 \cdot \text{IC}(\text{MICA}(c_1, c_2))}{\text{IC}(c_1) + \text{IC}(c_2)}$$

### 5.1. Properties

- **Range**: $[0, 1]$ — bounded, interpretable.
- **Symmetric**: Yes.
- **Identity**: $\text{Sim}_{\text{Lin}}(c, c) = 1.0$.
- **Disjoint terms**: $\text{Sim}_{\text{Lin}} = 0$ if the only common ancestor is the root.

### 5.2. Why Lin Is Preferred

Lin similarity is the most widely used hierarchical similarity metric in clinical NLP because:

1. **Bounded range** makes thresholding and comparison easy.
2. **Normalisation** accounts for the specificity of both terms. Two specific terms that share a specific common ancestor get a high score; two generic terms that share a generic common ancestor get a low score (even if the IC of the common ancestor is the same).
3. **Empirically validated** to correlate with human judgements of phenotype similarity.

The project uses **Lin similarity** as the primary hierarchical similarity metric.

---

## 6. Jiang-Conrath Similarity — Distance-Based

**Jiang-Conrath similarity** (Jiang & Conrath, 1997) is defined as a distance, then converted to a similarity:

$$d_{\text{JC}}(c_1, c_2) = \text{IC}(c_1) + \text{IC}(c_2) - 2 \cdot \text{IC}(\text{MICA}(c_1, c_2))$$

$$\text{Sim}_{\text{JC}}(c_1, c_2) = \frac{1}{1 + d_{\text{JC}}(c_1, c_2)}$$

### 6.1. Properties

- **Range**: $(0, 1]$ — bounded.
- **Distance interpretation**: $d_{\text{JC}}$ is the "distance" between the two terms in IC space.
- **Identity**: $\text{Sim}_{\text{JC}}(c, c) = 1.0$.

### 6.2. When to Use Jiang-Conrath vs. Lin

Jiang-Conrath and Lin are highly correlated but differ on edge cases:

- **Lin** weights the common ancestor more heavily.
- **Jiang-Conrath** weights the individual terms' ICs more heavily.

The project uses Lin as the primary metric but reports Jiang-Conrath as a robustness check.

---

## 7. From Pairwise to Set Similarity

The above metrics are defined for **pairs of phenotypes**. To compare a patient's phenotype **set** to a disease's phenotype **set**, we need an aggregation strategy. Three are standard:

### 7.1. Best-Match Average (BMA)

For each phenotype in $A$, find the maximum similarity to any phenotype in $B$, then average:

$$\text{Sim}_{\text{BMA}}(A, B) = \frac{1}{|A|} \sum_{a \in A} \max_{b \in B} \text{Sim}_{\text{Lin}}(a, b)$$

BMA is the standard for patient → disease comparison. It is asymmetric (the direction matters), which is appropriate for the size-mismatch case.

### 7.2. Maximum Similarity

$$\text{Sim}_{\text{max}}(A, B) = \max_{a \in A, b \in B} \text{Sim}_{\text{Lin}}(a, b)$$

Maximum similarity is too permissive — a single high-similarity pair can dominate the score even if the rest are unrelated.

### 7.3. Average Similarity

$$\text{Sim}_{\text{avg}}(A, B) = \frac{1}{|A| \cdot |B|} \sum_{a \in A} \sum_{b \in B} \text{Sim}_{\text{Lin}}(a, b)$$

Average similarity is too conservative — it is dragged down by unrelated pairs.

### 7.4. The Project's Choice

The project uses **Best-Match Average** with **Lin similarity** as the pairwise metric. This is the standard in the HPO literature (Pesquita et al., 2009) and has been empirically validated to correlate with clinical judgement.

---

## 8. The Combined Similarity Metric

The project's final similarity score for a patient-disease pair is a **weighted combination** of three metrics:

$$\text{Score}(A, B) = w_1 \cdot \text{Overlap}(A, B) + w_2 \cdot \text{Sim}_{\text{BMA-Lin}}(A, B) + w_3 \cdot \text{Cosine}_{\text{BioBERT}}(A, B)$$

where:

- **Overlap** captures subset containment (the size-mismatch fix).
- **BMA-Lin** captures hierarchical similarity (the hierarchy-aware fix).
- **Cosine** captures semantic similarity (the embedding-based signal).

The weights $w_1, w_2, w_3$ are tuned on the validation set (typically $w_1 = 0.3, w_2 = 0.5, w_3 = 0.2$). The combination outperforms any single metric because each captures a different aspect of similarity that the others miss.

### 8.1. Why All Three Are Necessary

- **Overlap alone** would miss hierarchical matches (a patient with Nystagmus vs. a disease annotated with Abnormal Eye Movement).
- **Lin alone** would miss the size-mismatch case (a patient with 2 phenotypes vs. a disease with 40).
- **Cosine alone** would produce "vibes" rather than facts (Chapter 3.3).

The combination is what gives the system both recall (Overlap + Lin catch hierarchical and subset matches) and precision (Cosine + Lin filter out biologically implausible matches).
