# 03.4. BioBERT Fine-tuning Logic

This note covers the **mathematical mechanics of domain adaptation via fine-tuning** — the process by which a general BERT-Base model is transformed into BioBERT. All of these are essential for an engineer who wants to actually fine-tune a model rather than just describe one.

This note covers: (1) the transfer-learning strategy, (2) the MLM and NSP objectives with their mathematical formulations, (3) the learning-rate discipline required to avoid catastrophic forgetting, (4) weight freezing as a partial mitigation, and (5) modern PEFT techniques (LoRA, adapters, prefix tuning) that have replaced full fine-tuning in resource-constrained settings.

## 1. The Transfer-Learning Strategy

BioBERT does not start from random initialisation. It starts from **BERT-Base**'s pre-trained weights, which already encode general English grammar and a substantial amount of world knowledge. The fine-tuning process then **continues** training those weights on PubMed, with two goals:

1. **Preserve** the general-English knowledge (so the model can still parse "the patient arrived at the clinic").
2. **Specialise** the clinical vocabulary and relationships (so the model can parse "FBN1 mutation associated with Marfan syndrome").

This is **transfer learning**: the model's pre-trained weights are the starting point, and PubMed fine-tuning adjusts them to specialise for the medical domain.

### 1.1. Why Not Train from Scratch?

Training BERT-Base from scratch on PubMed (which is what PubMedBERT does) requires:

- ~14 million PubMed abstracts (~4.5B words)
- ~16 TPU-v3 days of training (per Gu et al., 2020)
- Substantial infrastructure cost

Fine-tuning BERT-Base on PubMed is **two orders of magnitude cheaper**:

- Same corpus, but only ~3 days of TPU time
- Starts from weights that already know English grammar, so it does not need to relearn syntax

The tradeoff is that fine-tuning inherits the BERT-Base vocabulary (Chapter 3.1). PubMedBERT, which pays the cost of training from scratch, gets a medical vocabulary as a reward.

## 2. The Pre-training Objectives: MLM and NSP

Both BERT-Base and BioBERT use the same two pre-training objectives. The original vault mentioned neither by name, which is a critical gap because they determine what the model actually learns.

### 2.1. Masked Language Modeling (MLM)

In MLM, a random 15% of tokens in each input sequence is selected for prediction. Of those:

- 80% are replaced with `[MASK]`
- 10% are replaced with a random token
- 10% are left unchanged

The model's task is to predict the original token at each masked position. The loss is the **cross-entropy** between the predicted distribution and the true token:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{MLM}} = -\sum_{i \in \text{masked}} \log P(x_i \mid x_{\setminus i}; \theta)$$

where $x_i$ is the true token at position $i$, $x_{\setminus i}$ is the rest of the sequence, and $\theta$ are the model parameters.

This objective forces the model to learn **contextual representations**: the vector for *bank* in *"the river bank"* must differ from the vector in *"the bank approved the loan"* because the surrounding context determines which token was masked.

### 2.2. Why the 80/10/10 Split?

The 80/10/10 split is a practical compromise:

- **80% `[MASK]`**: The model sees the explicit "predict me" signal and learns from it.
- **10% random**: Prevents the model from assuming `[MASK]` is always the prediction target. Without this, the model would learn to predict `[MASK]` only at `[MASK]` positions and ignore real tokens at inference time.
- **10% unchanged**: Forces the model to maintain representations for real tokens, not just `[MASK]`.

### 2.3. Next Sentence Prediction (NSP)

In NSP, the model is given two sentences A and B and must predict whether B follows A in the original text. The input is:

```
[CLS] sentence A [SEP] sentence B [SEP]
```

and the `[CLS]` vector is fed to a binary classifier. The loss is binary cross-entropy:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{NSP}} = -\left[y \log P(\text{isNext}) + (1 - y) \log P(\text{notNext})\right]$$

NSP was intended to teach the model discourse-level relationships (e.g., coreference, entailment). Subsequent research (RoBERTa, 2019) found that NSP provides little benefit and may even hurt performance, so RoBERTa dropped it. BERT and BioBERT retain it for historical reasons.

### 2.4. Total Loss

The total pre-training loss is the sum:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{total}} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{MLM}} + \mathcal{L}_{\text{NSP}}$$

Backpropagation updates all model parameters (embeddings, attention weights, FFN weights) to minimise this loss. Over millions of PubMed abstracts, the embeddings and attention patterns shift from general English to medical English — this is the **semantic shift** described in Chapter 3.1.

## 3. Catastrophic Forgetting and the Learning-Rate Discipline

### 3.1. The Problem

If fine-tuning uses too high a learning rate, the model's general-English knowledge is **overwritten** by PubMed-specific patterns. The model may perform better on PubMed-style inputs but lose the ability to parse general English. This is **catastrophic forgetting** — the model "forgets" what it learned in pre-training.

Concretely: a catastrophically-forgotten BioBERT may struggle to parse *"the patient arrived at the clinic this morning accompanied by his mother"* because the general-English tokens *arrived*, *clinic*, *morning*, *accompanied*, *mother* have been pushed toward PubMed-specific contexts.

### 3.2. The Standard Solution: Small Learning Rate

BioBERT fine-tuning uses a learning rate of $\eta = 2 \times 10^{-5}$ to $5 \times 10^{-5}$ — orders of magnitude smaller than BERT-Base's pre-training learning rate ($10^{-4}$ to $10^{-3}$). This ensures the weight updates are small enough to preserve general knowledge while still shifting the medical-token embeddings.

The mathematical intuition: gradient descent updates are $\Delta \theta = -\eta \nabla \mathcal{L}$. Small $\eta$ means small $\Delta \theta$ per step, so the model moves slowly through weight space and the general-knowledge basin is not escaped.

### 3.3. The Catastrophic-Forgetting Tradeoff

There is no free lunch:

- Too small $\eta$: Fine-tuning takes forever and may not converge.
- Too large $\eta$: Catastrophic forgetting.

The standard practice is **learning-rate warmup** (start at $\eta = 0$, ramp up linearly over the first 10% of steps to the target $\eta$, then decay linearly or cosine-decay back to 0). This lets the model explore early and stabilise late.

### 3.4. Weight Freezing as a Partial Mitigation

An alternative is to **freeze** the lower layers of BERT (which encode basic grammar and syntax) and only fine-tune the upper layers (which encode domain-specific semantics). Concretely:

- Freeze layers 0–6 (of 12)
- Fine-tune layers 7–11 + the embedding layer + the MLM/NSP heads

This preserves the general-English grammar in the lower layers while letting the upper layers specialise. The tradeoff is reduced capacity — the model has fewer free parameters to learn medical patterns.

In practice, BioBERT does **not** freeze any layers; it relies on the small learning rate alone. Freezing is more common in task-specific fine-tuning (e.g., fine-tuning BioBERT for NER on a small labeled corpus) where the risk of overfitting is higher.

## 4. Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT) — The Modern Alternative

Full fine-tuning updates all 110M BERT parameters, which requires:

- Storing the full model in GPU memory (~440MB in FP32, ~220MB in FP16)
- Storing optimizer state (Adam: 2× model parameters = ~880MB)
- A labeled dataset large enough to avoid overfitting

For many practical settings, this is infeasible. **Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT)** methods update only a small fraction of the parameters while achieving comparable performance. The three main families are adapters, prefix tuning, and LoRA.

### 4.1. Adapters (Houlsby et al., 2019)

Adapters are small bottleneck MLPs inserted between transformer layers. Each adapter has the form:

$$\vec{h} \to \vec{h} + W_{\text{up}} \cdot \text{ReLU}(W_{\text{down}} \cdot \vec{h})$$

where $W_{\text{down}} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \to r}$ and $W_{\text{up}} \in \mathbb{R}^{r \to d}$ with bottleneck dimension $r \ll d$ (typically $r = 64$). Only $W_{\text{up}}$ and $W_{\text{down}}$ are fine-tuned; the original BERT weights are frozen. Parameter count: ~3.5M (vs. 110M for full fine-tuning).

### 4.2. Prefix Tuning (Li & Liang, 2021)

Prefix tuning prepends a small number of **learnable "virtual tokens"** to the key and value vectors at each layer. The original BERT weights are frozen; only the prefix vectors are tuned. Parameter count: ~1–10M depending on prefix length.

### 4.3. LoRA — Low-Rank Adaptation (Hu et al., 2021)

LoRA is the current state-of-the-art PEFT method. The key idea is that fine-tuning updates $\Delta W$ to a pre-trained weight matrix $W$ are **low-rank** in practice:

$$W_{\text{fine-tuned}} = W_0 + \Delta W = W_0 + B A$$

where $A \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times d}$, $B \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times r}$, and $r \ll d$ (typically $r = 8$ or $r = 16$). Only $A$ and $B$ are fine-tuned; $W_0$ is frozen. Parameter count: ~1–10M.

The mathematical intuition: if the fine-tuning adjustment to $W$ has low intrinsic rank (which empirical studies confirm), then representing it as $BA$ captures the same update with far fewer parameters. LoRA also has the property that at inference time, $BA$ can be merged into $W$ to give a single matrix — so there is **no inference-time latency penalty**.

### 4.4. When to Use PEFT

| Method | Parameter Count | Inference Latency | Best For |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Full fine-tuning | 110M (100%) | None | Large labeled datasets, maximum performance |
| Adapter | ~3.5M (3%) | Small (extra MLP per layer) | Multi-task serving (swap adapters per task) |
| Prefix tuning | ~1–10M (1–10%) | None | Generation tasks |
| LoRA | ~1–10M (1–10%) | None (mergeable) | General-purpose, current default |

For this project, **LoRA is the recommended approach** for any task-specific fine-tuning (e.g., fine-tuning BioBERT on the project's labeled patient notes for NER). It reduces GPU memory by ~5×, enables training on a single consumer GPU, and produces performance within 1–2 F1 points of full fine-tuning on most benchmarks.

### 4.5. PEFT and Catastrophic Forgetting

PEFT methods inherently avoid catastrophic forgetting because **the pre-trained weights are frozen**. The model cannot forget general English because its general-English weights are never updated. The adapter / LoRA parameters learn only the domain-specific delta, which can be disabled (set $BA = 0$) to revert to the original model.

## 5. Practical Recipe for Fine-Tuning BioBERT on a New Corpus

For the project's pipeline, if a new clinical corpus (e.g., a hospital's patient notes) needs to be incorporated, the recommended recipe is:

1. **Start from BioBERT-v1.1 weights** (not BERT-Base — BioBERT already has the PubMed shift).
2. **Continue MLM training** on the new corpus for 1–2 epochs. Do not use NSP (it is known to provide little benefit).
3. **Use LoRA** with $r = 16$ on the query, key, value, and output projection matrices of every attention layer.
4. **Learning rate**: $5 \times 10^{-5}$ with linear warmup over 10% of steps, then linear decay to 0.
5. **Batch size**: 32 (or gradient-accumulation equivalent).
6. **Sequence length**: 512 (or 2048 with Longformer if notes are long).
7. **Evaluate** on a held-out clinical NER or retrieval benchmark; stop when performance plateaus.

This recipe costs <10% of full fine-tuning's compute budget and produces a model whose performance is within 1–2 points of full fine-tuning on standard benchmarks.
