# 03.2. ClinicalBERT, PubMedBERT, and SapBERT

This chapter compares the five embedding models used in the project's multi-embedding retrieval stage. The original vault listed the models and their training corpora but treated them as interchangeable "medical BERTs" with vague personality descriptions. This is insufficient for an engineer to choose between them. Each model has a **specific architectural reason** it exists, a **specific mathematical training objective** that gives it its strength, and a **specific failure mode** that the others compensate for. This note unpacks all three for each model.

The five models are: **ClinicalBERT**, **PubMedBERT**, **SapBERT**, **BioBERT** (baseline), and **MiniLM** (control). We cover them in pedagogical order: the baseline first, then the two domain-adapted variants (BioBERT, ClinicalBERT), then the from-scratch medical model (PubMedBERT), then the contrastive entity-linking model (SapBERT), and finally the control.

---

## 1. BioBERT — The Baseline Domain-Adapted Model

- **Architecture**: BERT-Base (12 layers, 768 hidden, 12 heads, 110M parameters).
- **Vocabulary**: Inherited unchanged from BERT-Base (30,522 tokens, general English).
- **Pre-training**: Started from BERT-Base weights, continued MLM + NSP training on PubMed abstracts + PMC full-text (~18B words). Published 2019 (Lee et al.).
- **Strength**: Balanced medical reasoning. The attention weights have been pushed to recognise clinical relationships, but the vocabulary and grammar are inherited from general BERT, so the model is robust to mixed general/medical text.
- **Weakness**: Vocabulary mismatch (Chapter 3.1). Rare medical terms are heavily fragmented because the WordPiece vocabulary is general English.
- **Project role**: The **baseline sentence-level cosine similarity scorer**. All Top-K retrieval results are compared against BioBERT's scores, and the Mann-Whitney U-test (Chapter 10.1) verifies that BioBERT significantly outperforms the MiniLM control.

---

## 2. ClinicalBERT — The MIMIC-III Specialist

- **Architecture**: BERT-Base, fine-tuned on MIMIC-III.
- **Vocabulary**: Inherited from BERT-Base.
- **Pre-training**: Continued MLM training on ~2 million MIMIC-III discharge summaries (~600M words). Published 2019 (Alsentzer et al.).
- **Strength**: MIMIC-III is **real hospital data** — ICU discharge summaries written by clinicians under time pressure. ClinicalBERT has seen doctor shorthand (`c/o`, `SOB`, `PMH`), abbreviations, fragmented syntax, and the actual messiness of clinical narrative. It is the best of the five at parsing raw, uncleaned patient notes.
- **Weakness**: MIMIC-III is ICU-heavy, so the model is biased toward critical-care vocabulary (sepsis, intubation, vasopressors). It is weaker on rare genetic diseases that do not appear in ICU notes.
- **Project role**: Used in Stage 1 to parse **raw patient notes before the LLM Cleaner**. If a note is heavily abbreviated and the LLM Cleaner would benefit from a pre-expansion, ClinicalBERT's tokenization produces cleaner subword representations than BioBERT's. (In the current pipeline, the LLM Cleaner handles this directly, but ClinicalBERT remains available as a fallback.)

---

## 3. PubMedBERT — The From-Scratch Medical Model

- **Architecture**: BERT-Base.
- **Vocabulary**: **Trained from scratch** on PubMed abstracts. 30,522 tokens, but they are medical morphemes (`oculo`, `cutane`, `pathy`, `emia`, `osis`, `FBN1`, `HTT`, etc.).
- **Pre-training**: MLM + NSP, trained from random initialisation on PubMed + PMC. Published 2020 (Gu et al.).
- **Strength**: Because the vocabulary is medical, rare terms are minimally fragmented (Chapter 3.1 §2.3). On entity-level tasks — NER, entity linking, relation extraction — PubMedBERT consistently outperforms BioBERT by 3–7 F1 points.
- **Weakness**: Less robust to mixed general/medical text. If the patient note contains a lot of non-medical prose ("The patient arrived at the clinic this morning accompanied by his mother"), PubMedBERT may underperform BioBERT because the general-English tokens are less well-determined in its vocabulary.
- **Project role**: Used for **entity-level matching** in Stage 3, when individual HPO term labels must be matched to graph nodes. Also used as a second opinion in Stage 2 retrieval for cases where BioBERT's Top-20 looks suspicious.

### 3.1. The Critical Architectural Difference

This is the single most important distinction in the chapter, and the original vault did not make it:

> **BioBERT fine-tunes BERT-Base on PubMed but keeps the general vocabulary. PubMedBERT trains the tokenizer AND the model from scratch on PubMed, so it has a medical vocabulary.**

This is why PubMedBERT outperforms BioBERT on entity-level tasks despite both being "BERT trained on PubMed." The vocabulary is the bottleneck, and only PubMedBERT addresses it.

## 4. SapBERT — The Contrastive Entity-Linking Model

- **Architecture**: BERT-Base, fine-tuned with a **contrastive metric-learning objective**.
- **Vocabulary**: Inherited from BERT-Base.
- **Pre-training**: Started from PubMedBERT weights, then fine-tuned on UMLS Metathesaurus synonym pairs using **Multi-Similarity Loss**. Published 2020 (Liu et al.).
- **Strength**: **Entity linking and synonym alignment.** SapBERT knows that *High BP*, *Hypertension*, and *Elevated blood pressure* should map to the **same point** in 768-dim space, because it was explicitly trained to minimise the distance between UMLS synonyms.
- **Weakness**: Not designed for sentence-level cosine similarity. SapBERT's `[CLS]` vector is optimised for entity-level distance, not sentence-level semantic similarity.
- **Project role**: Critical for **Stage 1 → Stage 3 handoff**, when extracted phenotypes must be matched to canonical HPO labels. If the LLM Cleaner outputs *"high blood pressure"* and the HPO dictionary contains *"Hypertension"*, only SapBERT will give them a high cosine similarity. BioBERT and PubMedBERT will give a moderate score (they know the terms are related) but not the near-1.0 score that SapBERT produces.

### 4.1. The Contrastive Learning Objective (Missing from Original Vault)

The original vault said SapBERT is "mathematically designed to bring synonyms together" but never explained the mathematics. SapBERT uses **Multi-Similarity Loss (MS Loss)**, a contrastive objective that operates on pairs of examples with positive (synonym) and negative (non-synonym) labels.

For a batch of embeddings $\{x_i\}$ with positive set $P_i$ and negative set $N_i$ for each anchor $x_i$, MS Loss is:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{MS}} = \frac{1}{B}\sum_{i=1}^{B}\left[\frac{1}{\alpha}\log\!\left(1 + \sum_{p \in P_i} e^{-\alpha(s_i - s_p + \lambda)}\right) + \frac{1}{\beta}\log\!\left(1 + \sum_{n \in N_i} e^{\beta(s_i - s_n - \lambda)}\right)\right]$$

where $s_i = \cos(x_i, x_p)$ is the cosine similarity, $\alpha, \beta$ are scaling hyperparameters, and $\lambda$ is a margin. The first term pulls positives closer; the second pushes negatives away.

Contrast this with BERT's standard MLM objective, which predicts masked tokens. MLM has no explicit signal about synonymy — it only learns that synonyms are nearby because they appear in similar contexts. SapBERT's MS Loss provides an **explicit** synonymy signal, which is why it outperforms MLM-trained models on entity linking by a wide margin.

### 4.2. The UMLS Foundation (See Chapter 3.6)

SapBERT is trained on synonym pairs from the UMLS Metathesaurus. Each pair is two strings (in any of the 200+ source vocabularies) that share a UMLS Concept Unique Identifier (CUI). For example, the CUI C0020538 has synonyms including *Hypertension* (MeSH), *High Blood Pressure* (MedlinePlus), *Elevated Blood Pressure* (SNOMED-CT), and dozens of others. SapBERT sees all of these as positive pairs during training.

Without UMLS, SapBERT would not exist. Chapter 3.6 covers UMLS in detail.

## 5. MiniLM — The General-Purpose Control

- **Architecture**: Distilled transformer, 6 layers, 384 hidden, 12 heads, ~22M parameters.
- **Vocabulary**: General English (no medical specialisation).
- **Pre-training**: General web text, distilled from a larger teacher model.
- **Strength**: Fast, lightweight, and a fair **scientific control**. If MiniLM outperforms BioBERT on the project's test set, the entire domain-adaptation premise is invalid.
- **Weakness**: No medical knowledge whatsoever.
- **Project role**: The control arm of the Mann-Whitney U-test (Chapter 10.1). The project's hypothesis is that BioBERT significantly outperforms MiniLM on rare-disease retrieval; the U-test verifies this with $p < 0.05$.

## 6. The Multi-Embedding Strategy in Production

The project runs all five models in parallel during Stage 2 retrieval:

```python
# Pseudocode for multi-embedding retrieval
patient_vec = {model: encode(patient_note, model) for model in models}
for orpha_id, disease_text in disease_db.items():
    for model in models:
        disease_vec = encode(disease_text, model)
        score = cosine(patient_vec[model], disease_vec)
        scores[orpha_id][model] = score

# Aggregate: max-pool across models
final_score[orpha_id] = max(scores[orpha_id].values())
top_20 = sort_by_score(final_score)[:20]
```

This is more expensive than single-model retrieval (5× the encoding cost) but it dramatically improves recall: any disease that one model misses but another catches remains in the Top-20. The downstream Knowledge Graph verification (Chapter 8) then filters out the false positives that this permissiveness introduces.

The tradeoff is intentional: **recall is prioritised at Stage 2, precision at Stage 3**. This is consistent with the standard two-stage retrieval paradigm (Chapter 9.1) — retrieval casts a wide net, ranking narrows it.

| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
