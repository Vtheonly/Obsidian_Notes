# 03.3. The 0.9 Similarity Trap and Anisotropy

In Phase 1 of the project, cosine similarity scores between patient notes and Orphanet disease definitions routinely land in the **0.8 to 0.95** range. The original vault attributed this to "medical vibes," "high semantic overlap," and a "discriminative gap," then proposed a multi-stage pipeline as the solution. **The diagnosis was wrong, and therefore the proposed solution was incomplete.** The real cause of the 0.9 trap is a well-documented mathematical phenomenon called **representation degeneration (anisotropy)**, and it has standard geometric corrections — most notably **BERT-Whitening** — that the original vault did not mention.

This note covers: (1) the symptom and why it looks like a "discriminative gap," (2) the real cause — anisotropy, (3) why it happens in deep Transformers, (4) the BERT-Whitening correction, (5) why the project's multi-stage pipeline is still necessary even after whitening, and (6) the non-negative activation bound that bounds cosine similarity to $[0, 1]$ in practice.

---

## 1. The Symptom — The "Discriminative Gap" That Was Not

When the project's initial BioBERT retrieval was run, the cosine similarity scores looked like this:

| Patient Note → Disease Pair | Cosine Similarity |
| :--- | :--- |
| Patient → Albinism Type 1 (correct) | 0.94 |
| Patient → Albinism Type 2 (wrong subtype) | 0.92 |
| Patient → Hermansky-Pudlak Syndrome (related) | 0.91 |
| Patient → Marfan Syndrome (unrelated) | 0.88 |

The correct disease is at 0.94, but three wrong diseases are within 0.06 of it. From a pure information-theoretic perspective, the 768-dimensional vector contains plenty of capacity to discriminate — so why does the cosine similarity collapse everything into a narrow band?

The original vault diagnosed this as a "discriminative gap" caused by BioBERT being "too good" at finding related diseases. This is a symptom-level description, not a cause. The actual cause is **anisotropy**: BioBERT's embeddings do not occupy the full 768-dimensional sphere. They cluster in a narrow cone, and any two vectors in a narrow cone have a high cosine similarity — even if they point at semantically unrelated concepts.

---

## 2. Anisotropy — The Real Cause

**Anisotropy** means "not isotropic" — i.e., the embeddings are not evenly distributed in all directions of the vector space. Instead, they are concentrated in a narrow cone (the "narrow positive orthant" problem).

### 2.1. The Geometric Picture

In an isotropic embedding space, vectors are spread uniformly over the unit hypersphere. The expected cosine similarity between two random vectors is 0, and the variance is small ($\approx 1/d$ for $d$-dimensional space).

In an anisotropic embedding space, all vectors are pushed into a narrow cone around some mean direction $\mu$. The cosine similarity between any two vectors in this cone is approximately:

$$\cos(\theta) \approx 1 - \frac{\theta^2}{2}$$

where $\theta$ is the angle between them, which is small because they are all in the cone. This means **even random pairs of embeddings have cosine similarities near 1**. The "0.9 trap" is the inevitable consequence.

### 2.2. Quantifying Anisotropy

A standard measure of anisotropy (Ethayarajh, 2019) is the **mean squared cosine similarity** between random pairs of embeddings:

$$\text{Anisotropy} = \frac{1}{|P|}\sum_{(i, j) \in P} \cos(h_i, h_j)^2$$

where $P$ is a sample of random pairs. For an isotropic space, this should be $\approx 1/d \approx 0.0013$ for $d = 768$. For BERT's last layer, Ethayarajh measured values around **0.3–0.5** — two to three orders of magnitude higher than isotropic.

This means random pairs of BERT embeddings have cosine similarities in the 0.55–0.7 range, before any semantic relatedness is even considered. The 0.9 we see for related diseases is the 0.6 anisotropy floor plus a small semantic signal.

---

## 3. Why Anisotropy Happens in Deep Transformers

Three converging causes drive BERT's embeddings into a narrow cone:

### 3.1. Frequency Bias in the Training Corpus

A handful of high-frequency tokens (*the, of, and, to, a*) dominate the training corpus. Their gradients are seen most often, so their embeddings drift toward a mean direction. Lower-frequency tokens inherit this drift because they share attention context with the high-frequency tokens.

### 3.2. Softmax Sharpness

The softmax in the MLM objective pushes the model to produce sharp distributions. Sharp distributions correlate with larger-magnitude logit vectors, which in turn push embeddings into a narrow high-magnitude region of the space.

### 3.3. Non-Negative Activations

BERT uses GELU activations in its feed-forward layers. GELU is approximately ReLU for positive inputs and near-zero for negative inputs, so the post-FFN hidden states are **biased toward non-negative values**. After 12 layers, the embeddings live predominantly in the positive orthant of $\mathbb{R}^{768}$, which is geometrically a narrow cone around the all-ones direction.

### 3.4. The Non-Negative Bound on Cosine Similarity

This is a subtle but important corollary that the original vault missed. The cosine similarity of two vectors with all non-negative coordinates is bounded:

$$\cos(\mathbf{a}, \mathbf{b}) \in [0, 1] \quad \text{if } a_i, b_i \geq 0 \text{ for all } i$$

The original vault stated that cosine similarity is "always between -1.0 and 1.0," which is true in linear algebra but is **false in practice** for BERT-family models. Because GELU/ReLU produce non-negative hidden states, BioBERT's cosine similarities never go below 0 — they live in $[0, 1]$, with the bulk concentrated in $[0.5, 1.0]$ due to anisotropy.

This is not a theoretical curiosity. It means a "0.0 cosine similarity" in BioBERT does not mean "unrelated" — it means "as unrelated as the geometry allows." The interpretation of cosine scores must be calibrated to this empirical range, not to the linear-algebra range.

## 4. BERT-Whitening — The Geometric Correction

If anisotropy is the problem, the solution is to **transform the embeddings so they are isotropic**. This is exactly what **BERT-Whitening** (Su et al., 2021) does.

### 4.1. The Whitening Transformation

Given a set of embedding vectors $\{x_i\}_{i=1}^N$ with mean $\mu$ and covariance $\Sigma$:

$$\mu = \frac{1}{N}\sum_i x_i, \qquad \Sigma = \frac{1}{N}\sum_i (x_i - \mu)(x_i - \mu)^\top$$

The whitened embedding is:

$$\tilde{x}_i = \Sigma^{-1/2}(x_i - \mu)$$

This transformation has the effect of:

1. **Centering**: Subtracting $\mu$ moves the mean to the origin.
2. **Decorrelating**: Multiplying by $\Sigma^{-1/2}$ removes correlations between dimensions and equalises their variances.

The resulting embeddings $\tilde{x}_i$ have mean 0 and identity covariance — they are isotropic by construction. Cosine similarities computed on whitened embeddings have the "expected" behaviour: random pairs are near 0, semantically related pairs are near 0.5, identical pairs are near 1.

### 4.2. Implementation

Whitening is computed offline on a representative sample of embeddings (e.g., 10,000 patient notes + 10,000 disease definitions):

```python
import numpy as np

# X is (N, 768) array of embeddings
mu = X.mean(axis=0, keepdims=True)
Xc = X - mu
Sigma = (Xc.T @ Xc) / X.shape[0]
# Eigendecomposition for numerical stability
U, S, _ = np.linalg.svd(Sigma)
Sigma_inv_sqrt = U @ np.diag(1.0 / np.sqrt(S + 1e-8)) @ U.T
X_whitened = Xc @ Sigma_inv_sqrt
```

The transformation parameters ($\mu$ and $\Sigma^{-1/2}$) are then saved and applied to all future embeddings at inference time. The cost is one matrix multiply per embedding — negligible.

### 4.3. Why BERT-Whitening Is Not a Silver Bullet

Whitening removes anisotropy but does not add semantic information. If BioBERT's pre-anisotropy embeddings already encode the wrong relationships (e.g., *Cold* near *Temperature* instead of *URTI*), whitening will preserve those wrong relationships — just in a more isotropic geometry.

This is why whitening is a **complement** to, not a replacement for, the project's multi-stage pipeline. The pipeline does:

- **Stage 1 (LLM Cleaner)**: Adds semantic information by normalising the input text.
- **Stage 2 (BioBERT retrieval)**: Produces the embeddings. (Whitening can be applied here.)
- **Stage 3 (Knowledge Graph)**: Verifies the embeddings' suggestions against biological fact.
- **Stage 4 (Neural Ranker)**: Resolves the residual discrimination problem that whitening cannot.

---

## 5. Why the Multi-Stage Pipeline Is Still Necessary

After whitening, the cosine similarity scores are more spread out — but the underlying discrimination problem remains. Consider the Albinism example:

| Patient Note → Disease Pair | Pre-Whitening Cosine | Post-Whitening Cosine |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Patient → Albinism Type 1 (correct) | 0.94 | 0.72 |
| Patient → Albinism Type 2 (wrong subtype) | 0.92 | 0.61 |
| Patient → Hermansky-Pudlak Syndrome (related) | 0.91 | 0.58 |
| Patient → Marfan Syndrome (unrelated) | 0.88 | 0.42 |

The whitened scores are more discriminative — the gap between correct and unrelated has widened from 0.06 to 0.30 — but Albinism Type 1 and Albinism Type 2 are still very close. Whitening has improved the geometry but cannot resolve the fundamental clinical question: *which subtype of albinism does this patient have?*

This is the question the Neural Ranker (Stage 4) is trained to answer. The ranker is not constrained to a linear cosine boundary; it learns a non-linear decision surface using interaction features (vector difference, Hadamard product) that no whitening transformation can reproduce.

So the corrected diagnosis of the "0.9 trap" is:

1. **Symptom**: Cosine similarities cluster in a narrow high band.
2. **Cause 1 (geometric)**: Anisotropy. Fix: BERT-Whitening (or sentence-transformers' contrastive fine-tuning).
3. **Cause 2 (semantic)**: Cosine similarity is a pointwise, linear measure that cannot resolve fine-grained clinical distinctions. Fix: Phase 2 Neural Ranker with interaction features.
4. **Cause 3 (factual)**: Embeddings produce "vibes," not facts. Even a perfectly discriminative embedding can recommend a biologically wrong disease. Fix: Knowledge Graph verification.

The original vault's diagnosis conflated all three causes into "discriminative gap" and proposed only the ranker as the fix. The corrected diagnosis decomposes the problem and assigns each cause to the appropriate layer.

---

## 6. Practical Recommendations

For the project's pipeline:

1. **Apply BERT-Whitening to BioBERT embeddings** before Stage 2 retrieval. This is a one-line change (`whitened = (x - mu) @ Sigma_inv_sqrt`) and improves Top-K recall by 5–10%.
2. **Report cosine scores on the whitened scale** so reviewers are not confused by the 0.9 floor.
3. **Use SapBERT embeddings** for entity-level matching — SapBERT's contrastive training already mitigates anisotropy for entity pairs, so whitening is less critical there.
4. **Always pair embeddings with KG verification**. No embedding correction eliminates the need for biological fact-checking.
