# 03.1. Why General AI Fails in Medicine

This note explains the **scientific reason** the project moved from general-purpose BERT to BioBERT and PubMedBERT. The original vault attributed the failure to "the pre-training gap" — general BERT has not read PubMed. This is true but it is only the surface symptom. The deeper, architectural reason is **vocabulary mismatch**: general BERT's 30,522-token WordPiece vocabulary was trained on Wikipedia + BookCorpus, and rare medical terms are systematically fragmented into meaningless subwords. Fine-tuning the embedding weights cannot fix this, because the fragmentation happens *before* the embeddings are looked up. This is the single most important architectural insight in the entire vault, and it is the reason PubMedBERT (which trains the tokenizer from scratch on PubMed) outperforms BioBERT (which inherits BERT's general vocabulary) on entity-level tasks.

This note covers: (1) the pre-training gap, (2) vocabulary mismatch and fragmentation, (3) semantic shift and why fine-tuning fixes it, and (4) what fine-tuning cannot fix.

---

## 1. The Pre-training Gap

AI models are, in a real sense, the sum of the text they have been trained on. The corpus determines which tokens are statistically well-determined in the embedding space and which are noise.

| Model | Pre-training Corpus | Approx. Word Count |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| BERT-Base (general) | Wikipedia + BookCorpus | ~3.3 billion |
| BioBERT | BERT-Base + PubMed abstracts + PMC full-text | ~3.3B + 18B = ~21 billion |
| PubMedBERT | PubMed abstracts + PMC full-text (from scratch) | ~14 million abstracts, ~4.5B words |
| ClinicalBERT | MIMIC-III discharge summaries | ~2 million notes, ~600M words |
| SapBERT | UMLS Metathesaurus synonym pairs | ~10 million synonym pairs |

The pre-training gap is the difference between general BERT's exposure to medical text (essentially zero — Wikipedia's medical articles are a rounding error) and BioBERT's (18 billion PubMed words). A model that has never seen `FBN1` in context cannot know that it is a fibrillin gene; it will treat it as a random 4-character string.

### 1.1. The Mathematical Consequence

In the 768-dimensional embedding space, the variance of a token's vector across training runs is inversely proportional to its training-corpus frequency. Common words like *the* or *skin* have well-determined vectors (low variance). Rare tokens like `FBN1` or `Oculocutaneous` have high variance — the model's best guess at their meaning is dominated by the few contexts in which they appeared, plus noise from the random initialisation.

When a general BERT is asked to compute the cosine similarity between *"patient with FBN1 mutation"* and an Orphanet definition mentioning *"Marfan syndrome"*, the FBN1 token's embedding contributes noise to the `[CLS]` vector. The resulting cosine score is dominated by general medical vocabulary (patient, syndrome, mutation) and misses the specific FBN1 → Marfan link.

BioBERT, by contrast, has seen FBN1 in 100,000+ PubMed contexts, always near Marfan-related terms. Its FBN1 embedding is well-determined and points toward the Marfan region of the embedding space.

## 2. Vocabulary Mismatch — The Architectural Bottleneck

Vocabulary mismatch is the deeper problem that fine-tuning alone cannot solve. Recall from [[02.3. Tokenization and WordPiece Logic]] that the tokenizer is **frozen** after pre-training — fine-tuning adjusts the embedding weights but not the vocabulary. If a token was not in the original WordPiece vocabulary, fine-tuning cannot add it.

### 2.1. The Fragmentation Problem

Consider how three models tokenize the term *Oculocutaneous Albinism*:

- **BERT-Base** (general vocabulary):
  `Ocu ##lo ##cut ##ane ##ous ##al ##bin ##ism` → 8 tokens
- **BioBERT** (inherited general vocabulary):
  `Oculo ##cut ##ane ##ous Al ##bin ##ism` → 7 tokens
- **PubMedBERT** (medical-trained vocabulary):
  `Oculocutane ##ous Albinism` → 2 tokens

The difference is dramatic. PubMedBERT has learned that `Oculocutane` and `Albinism` are first-class morphological units in the medical domain, so it stores them as whole tokens. BERT and BioBERT, trained on general text, never saw these morphemes frequently enough to merge them, so they fragment the word into many small pieces.

### 2.2. Why Fragmentation Hurts

Each subword token receives its own 768-dimensional embedding, and the `[CLS]` vector is a function of all of them. Heavy fragmentation has three consequences:

1. **Embedding noise per subword.** A subword like `##ous` is shared across many unrelated words (`Oculocutaneous`, `fabulous`, `nervous`, `porous`). Its embedding must serve all of them, so it is a noisy estimate of any one meaning.
2. **Sequence-length consumption.** A 200-word patient note can balloon to 400+ tokens after fragmentation, hitting BERT's 512-token cap and forcing truncation that may cut diagnostic information.
3. **Loss of morpheme-level compositionality.** A model that sees `Oculocutane` as a single token can learn that it pattern-matches with `Oculocutane` in other contexts (e.g., `Oculocutane albinism type 1A` vs. `Oculocutane albinism type 2`). A model that sees `Ocu ##lo ##cut ##ane` cannot easily do this.

### 2.3. Why This Is a Structural Argument for PubMedBERT

The original vault treated BioBERT and PubMedBERT as interchangeable "medical BERTs." They are not. BioBERT is constrained to use BERT-Base's general vocabulary, which means rare medical terms are always fragmented. PubMedBERT was trained from scratch — its tokenizer, its vocabulary, and its embeddings were all learned on PubMed, so it has medical morphemes as first-class tokens. On entity-level tasks (entity linking, NER), PubMedBERT consistently outperforms BioBERT by 3–7 F1 points (Gu et al., 2021). On sentence-level retrieval (the project's Stage 2), the gap is smaller but PubMedBERT still wins.

This is why the project's pipeline uses **multiple models in parallel** (Chapter 3.2) rather than committing to one. BioBERT is the baseline for sentence-level cosine similarity; PubMedBERT is preferred for entity-level matching; SapBERT is preferred for synonym alignment.

## 3. Semantic Shift — What Fine-tuning Does Fix

While fine-tuning cannot change the vocabulary, it can shift the embedding weights of existing tokens. This is what **semantic shift** refers to: the vector for *Positive* in general BERT sits near *Happy / Good / Success*, while in BioBERT it has been pulled toward *Result / Confirmed / Presence of Pathology*.

| Word | General BERT neighbourhood | BioBERT neighbourhood |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Positive** | Happy, Good, Success | Result, Confirmed, Presence |
| **Negative** | Sad, Bad, Failure | Absent, Ruled out |
| **Cell** | Phone, Prison, Battery | Biological cell, Cancerous unit |
| **Cold** | Temperature, Winter | URTI, viral infection |
| **Depression** | Sadness, Mood | Anatomical depression (e.g., of bone) |

The shift happens because fine-tuning adjusts the embedding matrix to minimise MLM loss on PubMed, where these words' contexts are different from Wikipedia. The mathematical mechanism is gradient descent on the same loss as BERT's original pre-training (Chapter 3.4).

### 3.1. Why Semantic Shift Improves Cosine Similarity

Recall that cosine similarity is the angle between two vectors. If a patient note contains *"patient positive for albinism"* and the Orphanet definition contains *"patient exhibits albinism"*, the cosine similarity depends on whether the *positive* token pulls the patient-note vector toward the Orphanet-definition vector or away from it.

- **General BERT**: *Positive* sits near *Happy / Good*. The patient-note vector is pulled toward the *Good* region, which is far from the *Albinism* region. Cosine similarity drops.
- **BioBERT**: *Positive* sits near *Result / Confirmed*. The patient-note vector is pulled toward the *Clinical Result* region, which is close to the *Albinism* region (because both are clinical contexts). Cosine similarity rises.

This is the mathematical mechanism behind the project's "0.9 → 0.7" comparison. Fine-tuning does not add new vocabulary, but it does shift existing-token embeddings into the right neighbourhoods.

## 4. What Fine-tuning Cannot Fix

This is the crucial caveat. Fine-tuning (whether BioBERT-style continued pre-training or task-specific fine-tuning) cannot fix:

1. **Vocabulary mismatch** (§2 above). The tokenizer is frozen. Rare medical terms will fragment the same way before and after fine-tuning.
2. **Anisotropy** (Chapter 3.3). The narrow-cone phenomenon is a structural property of deep Transformer embeddings, not a property of the corpus. Fine-tuning on PubMed does not fix it.
3. **Synonym alignment**. BioBERT does not learn that *Hypertension*, *High BP*, and *Elevated blood pressure* should map to the same vector. This requires a contrastive objective (SapBERT, Chapter 3.2).
4. **Hallucination**. Embeddings are probabilistic; they can produce confident, plausible, and wrong matches. Fine-tuning does not add a verification layer.

Each of these limitations motivates a separate downstream component: PubMedBERT (vocabulary), BERT-Whitening (anisotropy), SapBERT (synonyms), and the Knowledge Graph (verification).

## 5. Why the Project Uses Five Models in Parallel

Given that no single model is sufficient, the project's Stage 2 retrieval runs **five models in parallel** and aggregates their Top-K lists:

| Model | What it contributes |
| :--- | :--- |
| **BioBERT** | Balanced medical reasoning; the baseline for sentence-level cosine. |
| **ClinicalBERT** | Best for doctor shorthand and abbreviations (MIMIC-III trained). |
| **PubMedBERT** | Best for entity-level precision (medical-trained vocabulary). |
| **SapBERT** | Best for synonym alignment (UMLS contrastive training). |
| **MiniLM** | General-purpose lightweight model, used as a **scientific control**. |

The control is critical: if BioBERT does not significantly outperform MiniLM on the project's test set, the domain adaptation was unnecessary. The Mann-Whitney U-test (Chapter 10.1) is precisely the statistical test used to verify this.

The aggregation strategy is conservative: the union of all five models' Top-K lists is taken, and each candidate is scored by the maximum cosine across models. This maximises recall (no candidate is lost because one model missed it) at the cost of a larger candidate set for Stage 3 to verify.
