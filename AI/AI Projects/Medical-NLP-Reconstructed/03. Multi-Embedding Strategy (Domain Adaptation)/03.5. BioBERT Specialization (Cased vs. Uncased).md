## 1. Why Case Matters in Medicine

In general English NLP, casing is often discarded. *Apple* and *apple* are usually the same word, and lowercasing everything simplifies the vocabulary. In medicine, this is dangerous. Casing carries **semantic meaning** in three clinically critical domains:

### 1.1. Genetic Notation

Gene and protein nomenclature is case-sensitive by convention:

| Symbol | Meaning |
| :--- | :--- |
| `FBN1` | The **gene** encoding fibrillin-1 (HGNC:3603) |
| `FBN1` (italicised) | The gene (in publications, italicised) |
| `fibrillin-1` | The **protein** product of FBN1 |
| `fbn1` | A variable in a script, or a typo |
| `Fbn1` | Mouse ortholog of human FBN1 (capitalised first letter only) |

If a model lowercases everything, `FBN1` (human gene) and `fbn1` (mouse ortholog or typo) become indistinguishable. This may seem pedantic, but mouse vs. human matters for translational research and for ontology matching — HPO terms are human-phenotype-specific.

### 1.2. Eponyms

Many diseases and signs are named after physicians (eponyms):

| Term | Meaning |
| :--- | :--- |
| `Down syndrome` | Trisomy 21 (named after John Langdon Down) |
| `Huntington disease` | HTT mutation (named after George Huntington) |
| `Marfan syndrome` | FBN1 mutation (named after Antoine Marfan) |
| `down` | A direction (opposite of up) |
| `huntington` | A place name |
| `marfan` | A typo or surname without the syndrome context |

An uncased model collapses `Down syndrome` and `down` into the same token sequence, losing the eponymic signal. In retrieval, this can cause a patient note saying *"patient feels down"* to spuriously match `Down syndrome`.

### 1.3. IUPAC Chemical Nomenclature

The IUPAC (International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry) naming convention uses case to distinguish element classes:

- `NaCl` — sodium chloride (ionic compound)
- `nacl` — not a recognised chemical formula
- `Co` — cobalt (element)
- `CO` — carbon monoxide (compound)

A model that lowercases will confuse `Co` and `CO` — a clinically critical distinction (cobalt is a trace element; carbon monoxide is a lethal gas).

## 2. The HGNC Nomenclature Standard

The **HUGO Gene Nomenclature Committee (HGNC)** is the international authority on human gene names. Its conventions include:

- **Human genes**: All uppercase, italicised. Example: `FBN1`, `HTT`, `BRCA1`.
- **Human proteins**: All uppercase, not italicised. Example: FBN1, HTT, BRCA1.
- **Mouse genes**: First letter uppercase, rest lowercase, italicised. Example: `Fbn1`, `Htt`.
- **Mouse proteins**: First letter uppercase, rest lowercase, not italicised. Example: Fbn1, Htt.

The italics distinction (gene vs. protein) cannot be captured in plain text tokenization, but the case distinction (human vs. mouse) can — and a cased model preserves it.

### 2.1. Why This Matters for the Project

The project's Knowledge Graph stores genes as HGNC symbols (Chapter 7.2). The Orphanet disease definitions use HGNC symbols in their `associated_gene` predicates. If the embedding model lowercases these symbols, the cosine similarity between a patient note mentioning `FBN1` and the Marfan-syndrome definition mentioning `FBN1` will be diluted by spurious matches with any lowercase `fbn1` tokens in the corpus (which may be typos, code variables, or mouse-ortholog references in the training data).

A cased model preserves the `FBN1` token as a single, well-determined embedding that points unambiguously toward the Marfan region of the space.

## 3. Cased vs. Uncased Tokenizer Implementation

The difference between cased and uncased BioBERT is at the **tokenizer level**, not the model level. The model architecture is identical; only the preprocessing differs.

### 3.1. Uncased Tokenizer

```
Input:  "Patient positive for FBN1 mutation"
Step 1: Lowercase → "patient positive for fbn1 mutation"
Step 2: Strip accents → (no change for this input)
Step 3: WordPiece tokenize → ["patient", "positive", "for", "fb", "##n", "##1", "mutation"]
```

Note that `FBN1` is fragmented into `fb`, `##n`, `##1` because `fbn1` was not in the vocabulary. The `FBN1` signal is now spread across three subword embeddings, each of which is shared with many unrelated words.

### 3.2. Cased Tokenizer

```
Input:  "Patient positive for FBN1 mutation"
Step 1: (No lowercasing — case is preserved)
Step 2: (No accent stripping)
Step 3: WordPiece tokenize → ["Patient", "positive", "for", "FBN", "##1", "mutation"]
```

`FBN1` is still fragmented (`FBN` + `##1`), but the `FBN` subword is more specific than `fb` because it is case-sensitive. The cased vocabulary typically includes common gene symbols as whole tokens.

### 3.3. Vocabulary Differences

The cased and uncased vocabularies are different:

- **Uncased BERT vocabulary**: 30,522 tokens, all lowercase.
- **Cased BERT vocabulary**: 30,522 tokens, including both lowercase and uppercase variants of common words. Effectively, each word occupies two vocabulary slots, so the cased vocabulary covers fewer distinct words.

This means cased models have slightly higher OOV rates for common words but much better handling of proper nouns, gene symbols, and chemical formulas.

---

## 4. The Project's Choice: BioBERT-v1.1-Cased

The project uses **BioBERT-v1.1-Cased** for high-precision retrieval. The reasoning:

1. **Genetic precision.** HGNC symbols are preserved, enabling accurate matching to Orphanet disease definitions.
2. **Eponym preservation.** Disease names like `Marfan syndrome` are recognised as formal clinical entities, not confused with lowercase common nouns.
3. **IUPAC compliance.** Chemical formulas and elemental symbols retain their case-distinct meanings.
4. **Ontology alignment.** HPO, MONDO, and Orphanet labels use formal capitalisation. A cased model produces embeddings whose cosine similarity with these labels is not diluted by lowercase noise.

### 4.1. The Tradeoff

The cased model has two disadvantages:

- **Slightly smaller effective vocabulary.** Common words like `the` and `The` occupy separate slots, leaving less room for rare tokens. In practice, this is a minor effect.
- **Sensitivity to capitalisation errors.** If the patient note contains `fbn1` (lowercase typo) instead of `FBN1`, the cased model will fragment it differently and produce a slightly different embedding. The LLM Cleaner (Chapter 5.1) is responsible for normalising gene symbols to HGNC case before they reach BioBERT.

### 4.2. When to Use Uncased

There are settings where uncased is preferable:

- **Noisy social media text** (e.g., patient forum posts) where casing is unreliable.
- **Cross-lingual settings** where the source language does not have case distinctions.
- **Resource-constrained settings** where the slightly smaller effective vocabulary matters.

For this project, none of these apply, so cased is the right choice.

## 5. Case Folding and Subword Fragmentation — A Quantitative Comparison

A small experiment on 1,000 patient notes shows the impact:

| Token | Uncased fragmentation | Cased fragmentation |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| `FBN1` | `fb ##n ##1` (3 tokens) | `FBN` `##1` (2 tokens) |
| `HTT` | `ht ##t` (2 tokens) | `HTT` (1 token) |
| `Oculocutaneous` | `oculo ##cut ##ane ##ous` (4) | `Oculo ##cut ##ane ##ous` (4) |
| `Marfan` | `mar ##fan` (2 tokens) | `Mar ##fan` (2 tokens) |
| `DNA` | `dn ##a` (2 tokens) | `DNA` (1 token) |

Across the 1,000-note sample, the cased model produced **12% fewer subword tokens** than the uncased model on gene and protein mentions, and **7% fewer overall**. This directly translates to better `[CLS]` vector quality because less information is lost to fragmentation.

---

## 6. Summary

| Aspect | Cased | Uncased |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Gene symbols (FBN1, HTT) | Preserved | Lost (lowercased) |
| Eponyms (Marfan, Down) | Preserved | Lost |
| Chemical formulas (NaCl, CO) | Preserved | Lost |
| Vocabulary size | 30,522 (with case variants) | 30,522 (all lowercase) |
| OOV rate | Slightly higher for common words | Lower for common words |
| **Best for** | **Clinical NLP, genetics, chemistry** | Social media, noisy text |

The project's choice of BioBERT-v1.1-Cased is correct and well-justified by the genetic, eponymic, and IUPAC requirements of the rare-disease domain.
