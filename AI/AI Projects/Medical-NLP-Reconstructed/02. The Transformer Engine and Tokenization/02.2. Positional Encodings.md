# 02.2. Positional Encodings

The original vault completely omitted positional encodings. This is a critical gap because **self-attention is permutation-invariant** — without positional information, BERT cannot distinguish *"No signs of fever, confirmed fracture"* from *"Confirmed fever, no signs of fracture."* The two sentences share the same bag of tokens, so they produce identical attention weights and identical `[CLS]` vectors. In a clinical setting, this would mean the system treats "absence of fever + presence of fracture" as identical to "presence of fever + absence of fracture" — a catastrophic failure of clinical reasoning.

This note explains why positional information must be injected separately, surveys the three main families of positional encodings (sinusoidal, learned, and rotary), and discusses which are used in BERT-family models vs. modern decoder LLMs.

---

## 1. Why Self-Attention Is Permutation-Invariant

Recall the attention formula from [[02.1. Self-Attention and the QKV Math]]:

$$\text{Attention}(Q, K, V) = \text{softmax}\!\left(\frac{Q K^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right) V$$

where $Q = X W^Q$, $K = X W^K$, $V = X W^V$, and $X \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times d}$ is the input token-embedding matrix.

The critical observation is that **the rows of $X$ are unordered from the attention mechanism's perspective**. If we permute the rows of $X$ by some permutation matrix $P$ (i.e., shuffle the tokens), we get:

$$Q' = (PX) W^Q = P Q, \quad K' = P K, \quad V' = P V$$

The attention output becomes:

$$\text{softmax}\!\left(\frac{(PQ)(PK)^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right) (PV) = P \cdot \text{softmax}\!\left(\frac{Q K^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right) V$$

The output is the **same** as the unshuffled output, just with its rows permuted in the same way. The model has no way to detect that the order changed. This is mathematically equivalent to saying that self-attention is a **set operation** — it treats the input as a bag of tokens.

For language, this is unacceptable. *"The doctor saw the patient"* and *"The patient saw the doctor"* are different sentences, and a model that cannot tell them apart is useless for clinical NLP. The fix is to **add positional information to the input embeddings** so that the model can recover the token's position in the sequence.

---

## 2. Sinusoidal Positional Encodings (Vaswani et al., 2017)

The original Transformer paper proposed a fixed, non-learned positional encoding based on sine and cosine functions of different frequencies:

$$\text{PE}_{(pos, 2i)} = \sin\!\left(\frac{pos}{10000^{2i/d_{\text{model}}}}\right)$$

$$\text{PE}_{(pos, 2i+1)} = \cos\!\left(\frac{pos}{10000^{2i/d_{\text{model}}}}\right)$$

where $pos$ is the token's position in the sequence (0, 1, 2, …) and $i \in \{0, 1, \dots, d_{\text{model}}/2 - 1\}$ indexes the frequency channel.

### 2.1. Why This Works

The sinusoidal encoding has three elegant mathematical properties:

1. **Bounded**: Every value is in $[-1, 1]$, so adding it to the token embedding does not blow up the magnitudes.
2. **Unique per position**: Each position has a unique encoding vector because the multi-frequency combination acts like a Fourier decomposition of position.
3. **Relative-position generalisation**: For any fixed offset $k$, $\text{PE}(pos + k)$ can be expressed as a linear function of $\text{PE}(pos)$. This means the model can learn to attend based on **relative** positions, not just absolute ones — crucial for generalising to longer sequences than seen in training.

### 2.2. Why the Original Transformer Used Fixed (Not Learned) Encodings

Vaswani et al. chose sinusoidal encodings for two reasons:

- **Extrapolation**: A learned encoding cannot represent positions beyond the training-time maximum. Sinusoidal encodings extend naturally to arbitrary length.
- **Inductive bias**: The smooth, structured sinusoidal pattern gives the model a strong prior that nearby positions are "similar" and distant positions are "different."

In practice, follow-up experiments (notably Shaw et al., 2018) showed that for sequences within the training length, **learned positional embeddings perform comparably or slightly better**. BERT chose learned embeddings (see §3); modern long-context models (Longformer, BigBird) often return to sinusoidal or rotary variants for extrapolation.

## 3. Learned Positional Embeddings (BERT, BioBERT, PubMedBERT)

BERT replaced sinusoidal encodings with **learned absolute positional embeddings**. The model maintains an embedding matrix $E_{\text{pos}} \in \mathbb{R}^{L_{\max} \times d_{\text{model}}}$, where $L_{\max} = 512$ is the maximum sequence length. The input to the first layer is:

$$X = E_{\text{token}} + E_{\text{pos}} + E_{\text{segment}}$$

where $E_{\text{token}} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times d}$ is the WordPiece embedding lookup, $E_{\text{pos}} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times d}$ is the positional embedding lookup (row $i$ of $E_{\text{pos}}$ is added to token $i$), and $E_{\text{segment}} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times d}$ distinguishes sentence A from sentence B in NSP-style pre-training.

### 3.1. Why BioBERT Inherits BERT's Positional Embeddings

BioBERT is a fine-tuned BERT — it inherits the architecture, including the learned positional embeddings, unchanged. The PubMed fine-tuning adjusts the token and segment embeddings and the attention weights, but the positional embedding matrix is also fine-tuned, just like every other parameter. This is fine because PubMed abstracts are roughly the same length distribution as Wikipedia text, so the positional embeddings do not need to be reinitialised.

### 3.2. The 512-Token Limit

The learned positional embedding matrix has only 512 rows, which is why BERT cannot accept sequences longer than 512 tokens. This is a hard architectural constraint:

- A long patient note (e.g., a 2000-word discharge summary) must be **chunked** into 512-token windows, with each window processed independently and the results pooled.
- Alternatively, **Longformer** or **BigBERT** variants use sparse attention + sinusoidal or rotary positional encodings to extend to 4096+ tokens.

For this project, all patient notes are pre-truncated to under 512 tokens by the LLM Clinical Cleaner (Chapter 5.1), which extracts only the clinically relevant sentences before passing the cleaned text to BioBERT. This is the standard production pattern.

## 4. Relative Positional Encodings (Shaw et al., 2018; T5)

An alternative to absolute positional embeddings is to inject **relative** position information directly into the attention computation. Instead of adding a position vector to the input, the relative position $i - j$ between query $i$ and key $j$ is encoded and added to the attention score:

$$\text{score}_{ij} = \frac{(X_i W^Q)(X_j W^K + a^K_{i-j})^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}$$

where $a^K_{i-j}$ is a learned embedding for the relative position $i - j$.

### 4.1. Why Relative Encodings Generalise Better

Absolute encodings tie the model to specific position indices. If a relevant clinical pattern (e.g., a "symptom → duration → severity" triple) always appears in the same syntactic structure but at different absolute positions in different notes, absolute encodings force the model to learn the pattern separately for each position. Relative encodings let the model learn it once, anchored to the structure of the pattern itself.

The T5 model (Raffel et al., 2020) uses a refined version of relative positional biases and demonstrates strong length-generalisation behaviour. Modern encoder models increasingly favour this family.

## 5. Rotary Positional Encodings (RoPE, Su et al., 2021)

Rotary Positional Embeddings (RoPE) are the current state-of-the-art for decoder LLMs (LLaMA, Mistral, Gemini-class models). RoPE encodes position by **rotating** the query and key vectors in a 2D subspace before computing their dot product.

### 5.1. The Mathematical Construction

For a $d$-dimensional vector, RoPE pairs up dimensions $(2i, 2i+1)$ and applies a 2D rotation by an angle $\theta_i \cdot pos$ to each pair:

$$R_{pos} = \text{blockdiag}\!\left(\begin{pmatrix} \cos(pos\,\theta_0) & -\sin(pos\,\theta_0) \\ \sin(pos\,\theta_0) & \cos(pos\,\theta_0) \end{pmatrix}, \dots, \begin{pmatrix} \cos(pos\,\theta_{d/2-1}) & -\sin(pos\,\theta_{d/2-1}) \\ \sin(pos\,\theta_{d/2-1}) & \cos(pos\,\theta_{d/2-1}) \end{pmatrix}\right)$$

with $\theta_i = 10000^{-2i/d}$. The attention score then becomes:

$$\text{score}_{ij} = (R_i X_i W^Q)^\top (R_j X_j W^K) = (X_i W^Q)^\top R_i^\top R_j (X_j W^K)$$

Since $R_i^\top R_j = R_{j - i}$, the score depends only on the **relative** position $j - i$, which is the key property. RoPE combines the best of both worlds: it is applied at attention time (not added to the input), it depends on relative position, and it is multiplicative rather than additive.

### 5.2. Why RoPE Matters for This Project

The LLM Clinical Cleaner (Chapter 5.1) and the LLM used in Stage 4 of the pipeline (if any) are decoder models. Their internal positional encoding is almost certainly RoPE. Understanding RoPE is therefore necessary to reason about:

- **Length extrapolation**: RoPE supports context-extension techniques like NTK-aware scaling and YaRN, which let a model trained at 4k context run at 32k+ context.
- **Attention pattern analysis**: When debugging why the LLM Cleaner failed to extract a phenotype from a long note, the position of the phenotype in the note matters. RoPE's rotation structure tells us how attention decays with relative distance.

BERT-family models (BioBERT, PubMedBERT, ClinicalBERT) still use learned absolute positional embeddings. RoPE is not used in this project's BioBERT stage.

## 7. Why This Matters for Clinical NLP

The choice of positional encoding has three concrete clinical consequences:

1. **Sentence-order sensitivity**. Without positional information, *"Patient denies fever"* and *"Patient has fever, denies cough"* would produce identical `[CLS]` vectors after attention. The negation scope (Chapter 1.1) cannot be recovered without position-aware attention.
2. **Long-note handling**. Patient notes are routinely 500+ words. A 512-token-capped BERT cannot ingest them whole; the choice of positional encoding determines whether chunking loses information.
3. **Cross-position pattern learning**. Clinical patterns are often syntactic ("gene → mutation → clinical consequence"). Positional encodings let attention heads specialise for these patterns regardless of absolute position.
