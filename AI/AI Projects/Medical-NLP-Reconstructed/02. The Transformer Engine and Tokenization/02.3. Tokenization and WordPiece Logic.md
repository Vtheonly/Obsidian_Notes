# 02.3. Tokenization and WordPiece Logic

Before any attention math can run, raw text must be converted into a sequence of integers — the token IDs that index into the model's embedding table. This process is **tokenization**, and the specific algorithm used by BERT is **WordPiece**. The original vault described WordPiece as a "static rule engine that breaks words into chunks," which is incorrect: WordPiece is a **probabilistic subword segmentation algorithm** whose vocabulary is learned by maximising the likelihood of the training corpus under a unigram language model. Without understanding this training algorithm, the reader cannot predict how a new medical term will be tokenized, cannot reason about why PubMedBERT outperforms BioBERT on rare terms (Chapter 3.2), and cannot implement subword pooling (§4 below) — the engineering step that converts a sequence of subword vectors back into a single entity-level vector for downstream tasks.

This note covers: (1) the WordPiece training algorithm, (2) the inference-time greedy segmentation, (3) infrastructure tokens `[CLS]`/`[SEP]`/`[MASK]` and the attention mask, (4) subword pooling strategies, and (5) how this project handles the BioBERT 30k-vocabulary limit on rare medical terms.

---

## 1. Why Subword Tokenization Exists

A tokenizer must satisfy three competing constraints:

1. **No unknown tokens.** Every input string must be representable — there can be no `[UNK]` outputs.
2. **Compact vocabulary.** The embedding table size is $|V| \times d_{\text{model}}$, so $|V|$ cannot be unbounded. BERT uses $|V| = 30{,}522$.
3. **Meaningful units.** The tokens should correspond to linguistic units (morphemes, words) so that the model can learn semantic relationships between them.

A pure word-level tokenizer fails (1) and (2) — every unseen word becomes `[UNK]`, and the vocabulary would need to be millions of entries. A pure character-level tokenizer satisfies (1) but fails (3) — characters carry little semantic signal, and the model would need huge context windows to compose meaning. **Subword tokenization** is the compromise: common words are kept whole, rare words are decomposed into reusable subwords, and the vocabulary stays compact.

## 2. The WordPiece Training Algorithm

The original vault presented WordPiece as if it were a hand-crafted rule set ("*Oculo* means eye, *cutane* means skin"). This is wrong. WordPiece is a **data-driven optimisation algorithm**. The vocabulary is built by starting from a base alphabet (single characters) and iteratively merging the symbol pair that maximally increases the likelihood of the training corpus under a unigram language model.

### 2.1. The Algorithm

Given a training corpus, the WordPiece vocabulary is built as follows:

1. **Initialise** the vocabulary with all single characters that appear in the corpus, plus the special `##` prefix marker (which denotes "this subword is a continuation of a previous one").
2. **Preprocess** the corpus by splitting each word into characters: `Oculocutaneous` → `O c u l o c u t a n e o u s`.
3. **Iterate**: At each step, score every adjacent pair of symbols $(A, B)$ by the likelihood gain that would result from merging them into a single symbol $AB$. The score is:

$$\text{score}(A, B) = \frac{f(AB)}{f(A) \cdot f(B)}$$

   where $f(\cdot)$ is the frequency of the symbol in the current corpus. This is the **PMI-like** score: it rewards pairs that occur together more often than chance would predict.

4. **Merge**: Pick the highest-scoring pair, merge it everywhere in the corpus, add the merged symbol to the vocabulary, and repeat.
5. **Terminate** when the vocabulary reaches the target size (30,522 for BERT-Base).

### 2.2. Why This Produces Meaningful Subwords

The PMI-like scoring means that morpheme boundaries (where the joint frequency is much higher than the product of marginals) are precisely the boundaries that get merged first. For medical text:

- `Oculo` + `##cut` → `Oculocut` (high PMI because they almost always co-occur)
- `Oculocut` + `##ane` → `Oculocutane` (similarly)
- `Oculocutane` + `##ous` → `Oculocutaneous` (similarly)

If the corpus contains enough medical text, the algorithm learns these morpheme-level units automatically. If the corpus is general English (as for vanilla BERT), the algorithm learns different merges — `Oculocutaneous` may end up fragmented into many small pieces because the morphemes are too rare to co-occur frequently.

### 2.3. The Critical Difference: BioBERT vs. PubMedBERT Vocabularies

- **BioBERT** inherits BERT's 30,522-token vocabulary, which was trained on general English (Wikipedia + BookCorpus). When BioBERT is fine-tuned on PubMed, the **vocabulary does not change** — only the embedding weights do. Rare medical terms are still heavily fragmented.
- **PubMedBERT** trains the tokenizer **from scratch** on PubMed abstracts. The 30,522-token vocabulary is built from medical text, so it includes medical morphemes (`oculo`, `cutane`, `pathy`, `emia`, `osis`) as first-class tokens. Rare terms are far less fragmented.

This is a major reason PubMedBERT outperforms BioBERT on entity-level tasks (Chapter 3.2) and is a key piece of architectural reasoning that the original vault omitted.

## 3. Inference-Time Segmentation (Greedy Longest-Match)

At inference time, a new word is segmented by the **greedy longest-match-first** algorithm:

1. Start at the beginning of the word.
2. Find the longest substring (starting at the current position) that is in the vocabulary.
3. Emit that substring as a token. If it is not the first token of the word, prefix it with `##`.
4. Advance the position and repeat until the word is consumed.
5. If no vocabulary match exists at the current position, fall back to `[UNK]` (rare with a well-trained subword vocabulary, but possible).

### 3.1. Worked Example

Word: `Oculocutaneous`
Vocabulary (subset): `{Oculo, ##cut, ##ane, ##ous, Oculocutane, ##ous, O, ##c, ##u, ...}`

- Position 0: longest match is `Oculo` (5 chars). Emit `Oculo`.
- Position 5: longest match is `##cut` (4 chars including the `##`). Emit `##cut`.
- Position 9: longest match is `##ane`. Emit `##ane`.
- Position 13: longest match is `##ous`. Emit `##ous`.

Final: `Oculo ##cut ##ane ##ous` — 4 tokens.

### 3.2. Why the Greedy Algorithm Is Sufficient

The greedy longest-match algorithm is **not** globally optimal (a different segmentation might have fewer tokens or higher likelihood), but it is fast, deterministic, and produces consistent segmentations across runs. For BERT-style models, the embedding and attention layers can recover from suboptimal segmentations because they are trained on the same greedy segmentations.

## 4. Infrastructure Tokens: `[CLS]`, `[SEP]`, `[MASK]`, `[PAD]`

BERT's vocabulary includes special tokens that serve structural roles:

| Token | Index | Purpose |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| `[PAD]` | 0 | Padding to fixed sequence length; attention mask = 0 |
| `[UNK]` | 100 | Unknown token (emitted when no subword match exists) |
| `[CLS]` | 101 | **Classification token** — placed at index 0; its final hidden state is used as the sentence-level representation |
| `[SEP]` | 102 | **Separator token** — placed at the end of each sentence; also separates sentence A from sentence B in NSP |
| `[MASK]` | 103 | **Mask token** — used during MLM pre-training; replaced with a real token at fine-tune time |

### 4.1. The `[CLS]` Token and Sentence Embeddings

In this project, every patient note and every Orphanet disease definition is encoded as a single sequence:

```
[CLS] patient exhibits nystagmus and hypopigmentation [SEP] [PAD] [PAD] ...
```

The `[CLS]` token is a "dummy" token prepended to the sequence. Because the attention mechanism lets `[CLS]` attend to every other token, its final-layer hidden state (a 768-dim vector) effectively aggregates information from the entire sentence. This vector is what `model.encode(text)` returns and what the cosine similarity is computed on.

**Important**: `[CLS]` was designed for classification, not for semantic similarity. Using it for cosine similarity works in practice but is known to be suboptimal — sentence-transformers (Reimers & Gurevych, 2019) show that **mean-pooling over all real tokens** produces better embeddings. The project's BioBERT stage uses `[CLS]`-pooling for backward compatibility with the original implementation, but a production upgrade would switch to mean-pooling.

### 4.2. The Attention Mask

As covered in [[02.1. Self-Attention and the QKV Math]] §5, padding tokens are excluded from attention by adding $-\infty$ to their attention scores before softmax. The attention mask is a binary vector of length $N$ where 1 = real token and 0 = padding. The mask is broadcast to an $N \times N$ matrix and added (as $0$ or $-\infty$) to the score matrix.

## 5. Subword Pooling Strategies (Critical Engineering Gap)

The original vault completely omitted this topic. When a multi-word medical entity like *Oculocutaneous Albinism* is tokenized into `[Oculo, ##cut, ##ane, ##ous, Al, ##bin, ##ism]` — 7 subword tokens — BERT produces 7 separate 768-dimensional vectors. If the downstream task requires a **single vector for the entity** (e.g., to compute entity-level cosine similarity, or to inject the entity into a knowledge graph node), the 7 subword vectors must be **pooled** into one.

Three pooling strategies are standard:

### 5.1. First-Token Pooling

Take the vector of the first subword of the entity:

$$\vec{v}_{\text{entity}} = h_{\text{Oculo}}$$

This is fast and is the default for span-classification tasks (e.g., NER), but it discards information from the other 6 subwords.

### 5.2. Mean Pooling

Average the vectors of all subwords belonging to the entity:

$$\vec{v}_{\text{entity}} = \frac{1}{7}\sum_{i \in \{\text{Oculo, ##cut, ##ane, ##ous, Al, ##bin, ##ism}\}} h_i$$

This is the most common choice for entity-linking tasks and is what SapBERT uses. It is robust to tokenization variation (different segmentations of the same entity produce similar pooled vectors).

### 5.3. Max Pooling

Take the element-wise maximum across the subword vectors:

$$\vec{v}_{\text{entity}, j} = \max_{i \in \text{subwords}} h_{i, j}$$

This is less common but is used in some biomedical entity-linking systems because it preserves the strongest activated feature in any subword.

### 5.4. Choice for This Project

The project's retrieval stage uses **sentence-level `[CLS]`-pooling** (the `[CLS]` vector of the entire patient note is compared to the `[CLS]` vector of each disease definition). This is appropriate for the Top-K retrieval task.

For the entity-linking stage (Stage 1 → Stage 3 handoff), when individual HPO terms must be matched to graph nodes, the project uses **mean-pooling over subword tokens** of the HPO term's label. This is more accurate than first-token pooling and is the standard in modern biomedical entity-linking systems.

## 6. The 30k-Vocabulary Limit and OOV Handling

BERT's 30,522-token vocabulary cannot cover every medical term. Even PubMedBERT, with its medical-trained vocabulary, occasionally encounters terms that fragment heavily:

| Term | BERT (general vocab) | BioBERT (general vocab) | PubMedBERT (medical vocab) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| *Oculocutaneous* | `Ocu ##lo ##cut ##ane ##ous` (5) | `Oculo ##cut ##ane ##ous` (4) | `Oculocutane ##ous` (2) |
| *Hepatosplenomegaly* | `Hep ##ato ##sp ##len ##ome ##ga ##ly` (7) | `Hep ##ato ##spleno ##megaly` (4) | `Hepatospleno ##megaly` (2) |
| *FBN1* | `FB ##N ##1` (3) | `FB ##N ##1` (3) | `FBN1` (1) |

Heavy fragmentation is bad for two reasons:

1. **Information loss**: Each subword embedding is a noisier estimate of the entity's meaning than a whole-word embedding would be.
2. **Sequence-length consumption**: A 200-word clinical note can balloon to 400+ tokens after fragmentation, hitting the 512-token cap.

The project mitigates this by:

- **Using PubMedBERT for entity-level matching** (Stage 1 → Stage 3), where fragmentation matters most.
- **Pre-normalising rare terms** to their HPO labels in the LLM Cleaner (Chapter 5.1). The LLM is asked to output HPO labels (which are short and well-tokenized) rather than verbatim patient descriptions.
- **Truncating long notes** at sentence boundaries (not character boundaries) to preserve clinical coherence.

## 7. BPE and Unigram LM Tokenizers (Brief Comparison)

WordPiece is one of three subword tokenization algorithms in widespread use. The other two are:

- **Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE)** — used by GPT-2/3/4, RoBERTa. Same merge-based training as WordPiece but without the `##` prefix; instead, word boundaries are marked by a `Ġ` (a special space character) at the start of each token. BPE merges by raw frequency rather than PMI.
- **Unigram LM** — used by SentencePiece / T5 / ALBERT. Starts with a large candidate vocabulary and **prunes** tokens to maximise corpus likelihood under a unigram model. Produces probabilistic segmentations (a word can have multiple segmentations, sampled stochastically).

For clinical NLP, the choice between WordPiece, BPE, and Unigram LM has minor practical impact compared to the choice of training corpus. A medical-trained BPE (e.g., as used by Clinical-Longformer) performs comparably to PubMedBERT's medical-trained WordPiece.
