# 15 — GPT-2 (Radford et al., 2019)

> [!info] TL;DR
> GPT-2 scaled the GPT-1 recipe to 1.5B parameters and 40GB of web text (WebText), and demonstrated **zero-shot task transfer**: the model could perform summarization, translation, and QA without any fine-tuning, just by conditioning on the prompt. The paper introduced the hypothesis that language modeling implicitly learns to perform any task for which the training data contains sufficient examples. The full model was initially withheld for "safety" reasons — a notable moment in the responsible-AI discourse.

## Citation

Radford, A., Wu, J., Child, R., Luan, D., Amodei, D., & Sutskever, I. (2019). *Language Models are Unsupervised Multitask Learners*. OpenAI technical report.

## The Problem Being Solved

[[14 - GPT-1 2018|GPT-1]] showed that pretrain-then-finetune worked. But fine-tuning still required labeled data for each task, which limited scalability. The GPT-2 paper asked a more ambitious question: **what if pretraining alone, at sufficient scale, is enough?**

The hypothesis: language modeling on a sufficiently diverse corpus implicitly teaches the model to perform every task that appears in the data. Translation appears in web pages (bilingual content), summarization appears in headlines and TL;DRs, QA appears in FAQ pages. If the model learns to predict the next token well, it should learn these tasks as side effects — and be able to perform them at inference time by conditioning on the right prompt.

This was a bet on scale and data diversity, not on architectural innovation. GPT-2 is architecturally identical to GPT-1; the difference is 10× parameters (117M → 1.5B) and 50× data (BooksCorpus → WebText).

## The Architecture

GPT-2 is a decoder-only Transformer, larger than GPT-1:

| Property            | GPT-1   | GPT-2 (small) | GPT-2 (full) |
|---------------------|---------|---------------|--------------|
| Parameters          | 117M    | 117M          | 1.5B         |
| Layers              | 12      | 12            | 48           |
| Hidden dimension    | 768     | 768           | 1600         |
| Attention heads     | 12      | 12            | 16           |
| Context window      | 512     | 1024          | 1024         |
| Vocabulary          | ~40K    | ~50K          | ~50K         |

Architectural changes from GPT-1:
- **Pre-norm** instead of post-norm (improves training stability at scale).
- **Modified initialization** (scaled residual layers).
- **Larger context** (1024 vs 512).
- **Byte-level BPE** tokenization (avoids out-of-vocabulary issues by operating on bytes).

## The WebText Dataset

GPT-2's training data was WebText: ~40GB of text from Reddit outbound links with karma ≥ 3 (a proxy for "humans found this useful"). This filtered out low-quality content and gave the model exposure to a wide range of styles and topics — articles, discussions, code, fiction, how-tos.

The choice of WebText was important. BooksCorpus (GPT-1) taught long-form discourse but was narrow in topic. WebText was broad and diverse, which was necessary for the zero-shot transfer hypothesis: the model needed to see examples of every task to learn them implicitly.

## The Zero-Shot Hypothesis

The paper's central claim: language modeling at sufficient scale implicitly learns tasks. The model is trained only on next-token prediction, but at inference time it can perform tasks by being given a prompt in the right format.

Examples from the paper:

### Translation (zero-shot)
```
Prompt: "The dog ran. In French, this means: Le chien courut."
```
The model continues with translations of subsequent sentences — without ever being trained on translation pairs explicitly. It learned translation from bilingual web content where English text is followed by French translations (or vice versa).

### Summarization (zero-shot)
```
Prompt: "<article text> TL;DR:"
```
The model generates a summary after `TL;DR:` — without being fine-tuned on summarization datasets. It learned summarization from web content where articles are followed by TL;DR summaries.

### QA (zero-shot)
```
Prompt: "Q: What is the capital of France? A:"
```
The model answers the question — without being fine-tuned on QA datasets. It learned QA from FAQ pages and Q&A forums.

This was the first widely-publicized demonstration that language models could perform tasks *they were never explicitly trained on*. The implications were profound: if scale alone unlocked task transfer, then the path to better AI was primarily about scale, not about specialized architectures or training schemes.

## Key Results

GPT-2 achieved state-of-the-art or near-SOTA on 7 of 8 zero-shot tasks:

| Task                              | Previous SOTA | GPT-2 (zero-shot) |
|-----------------------------------|---------------|--------------------|
| LAMBADA (last word prediction)    | 59.2          | 63.7               |
| WikiText-2 (perplexity)           | 65.9 (GPT-1)  | 17.5               |
| CoQA (F1)                         | 65.1          | 55.0               |
| Daily Mail/CNN (ROUGE-L)          | 30.5          | 23.3               |

On LAMBADA and WikiText, GPT-2 set new SOTA — remarkable for a zero-shot model competing with fine-tuned baselines. On summarization (Daily Mail/CNN) and QA (CoQA), zero-shot GPT-2 underperformed fine-tuned baselines but produced coherent, often-correct outputs.

The qualitative results were as important as the metrics. GPT-2's generated text was often indistinguishable from human writing at the paragraph level — a first for a language model. This capability raised concerns about misuse (fake news, spam, impersonation) that would shape the AI safety discourse for years.

## The "Staged Release"

OpenAI initially released only the small (117M) and medium (345M) versions, withholding the full 1.5B model citing concerns about misuse. The full model was released in November 2019, 9 months after the paper.

This staged release was controversial:
- **Supporters** argued it set a precedent for responsible disclosure of capable AI systems.
- **Critics** argued it was unnecessary (the model wasn't actually that dangerous), anti-competitive (OpenAI had access to the full model while researchers didn't), and set a bad precedent for closed AI development.

The staged release shaped how subsequent frontier models would be released. OpenAI's later models (GPT-3, GPT-4) were released via API only, never as open weights — a more extreme version of the same caution. Other labs (Meta with Llama, Mistral) took the opposite approach, releasing weights openly.

## Why It Worked

### Scale Unlocks Emergent Behavior
GPT-2 was the first clear demonstration that scaling up (parameters + data + compute) unlocks capabilities not present at smaller scales. Zero-shot task transfer does not work with GPT-1's 117M parameters; it starts to work at 1.5B. This was the empirical foundation for the "scaling hypothesis" that would dominate 2019–2024 AI research.

### Diverse Pretraining Data
WebText's diversity was essential. The model needed to see examples of every task to learn them implicitly. A narrow corpus (e.g., only news) would not have taught translation or coding.

### Causal Modeling as Implicit Multitask Learning
The paper's theoretical contribution was the framing of language modeling as implicit multitask learning: `p(output | input) = ∏ p(output_t | input, output_<t)`. Every text sequence is a (input, output) pair for some implicit task. Training on enough text teaches enough tasks.

### Byte-Level Tokenization
Byte-level BPE eliminated out-of-vocabulary tokens. This was important for zero-shot performance — the model could process any text, including unfamiliar words, code, or multilingual content, without `<unk>` tokens.

## Limitations

- **Coherence over long passages**: while paragraph-level quality was high, long-form coherence (tracking facts, characters, arguments over pages) was weak. The model would drift and contradict itself.
- **Factual errors**: GPT-2 hallucinated facts confidently — the model had no notion of "knowing" vs "guessing".
- **No tool use**: GPT-2 could not call calculators, search the web, or execute code. All reasoning had to happen in the forward pass.
- **Limited zero-shot performance on hard tasks**: while impressive for a zero-shot model, GPT-2 was far behind fine-tuned baselines on most tasks. The "zero-shot matches fine-tuned" claim would only hold at GPT-3 scale.
- **Safety concerns**: the model could generate convincing misinformation, impersonate writing styles, and produce inappropriate content. These issues would only intensify with larger models.

## Impact and Legacy

GPT-2 was a turning point in AI research:

1. **Validated the scaling hypothesis**. The empirical demonstration that scale unlocks emergent behavior motivated the wave of large-model training that defined 2019–2024 (GPT-3, Chinchilla, PaLM, Llama).

2. **Introduced zero-shot / few-shot evaluation**. The idea of evaluating models without fine-tuning became standard. By GPT-3, few-shot (with a handful of examples in the prompt) would become the dominant evaluation paradigm.

3. **Raised the AI safety stakes**. GPT-2's coherence made misuse a real concern. The staged release initiated a debate about responsible AI development that continues today.

4. **Established the GPT-2 architecture as the LLM default**. Pre-norm, decoder-only, byte-level BPE, learned positional embeddings — this became the template for Llama, Mistral, and most open-source LLMs.

5. **Catalyzed the generative AI industry**. GPT-2's writing quality showed that language models could produce useful text, not just classify it. This insight motivated the founding of numerous AI writing startups and, eventually, ChatGPT.

For AI engineers, GPT-2 is the inflection point where language models went from research curiosities to practical tools. Understanding GPT-2 clarifies why scale became the dominant research strategy and why zero-shot/few-shot evaluation replaced fine-tuning as the default.
