# Advanced Convolutional Operators — Depthwise, Dilated, Transposed, Grouped

> **Prerequisites:** [[01. The Convolution Operation Deep Dive]], [[02. The Mathematics of Receptive Fields and 1x1 Convolutions]]
> **Connections:** [[03. Evolution of CNN Architectures]] (MobileNet, DeepLab, U-Net use these operators)

The vault's CNN chapter covers standard convolution, pooling, and 1×1 convolutions in depth but completely omits the **advanced operators** that modern architectures rely on: depthwise separable convolutions (MobileNet), dilated/atrous convolutions (DeepLab), transposed convolutions (U-Net, GANs), and grouped convolutions (ResNeXt). This note fills that gap.

---

## 1. Depthwise Separable Convolutions

### The problem: standard convolution is expensive

A standard convolution with kernel size $K \times K$, input channels $C_{in}$, output channels $C_{out}$, on a $H \times W$ feature map costs:

$$\text{FLOPs} = H \times W \times K \times K \times C_{in} \times C_{out}$$

For a $3 \times 3$ conv with 64 input and 64 output channels on a $224 \times 224$ feature map: $224 \times 224 \times 3 \times 3 \times 64 \times 64 \approx 1.85 \text{ billion}$ FLOPs. This is expensive, especially for mobile and embedded deployment.

### The factorization

A **depthwise separable convolution** (used in MobileNet, Xception) factors the standard convolution into two cheaper operations:

**Step 1 — Depthwise convolution:** apply a separate $K \times K \times 1$ filter to each input channel independently. This captures spatial patterns but no cross-channel mixing. Cost: $H \times W \times K \times K \times C_{in}$.

**Step 2 — Pointwise convolution:** apply a $1 \times 1 \times C_{in} \times C_{out}$ convolution to mix channels. Cost: $H \times W \times C_{in} \times C_{out}$.

**Total cost:** $H \times W \times K^2 \times C_{in} + H \times W \times C_{in} \times C_{out}$

### The savings

The ratio of depthwise separable to standard convolution cost is:

$$\frac{K^2 \cdot C_{in} + C_{in} \cdot C_{out}}{K^2 \cdot C_{in} \cdot C_{out}} = \frac{1}{C_{out}} + \frac{1}{K^2}$$

For $K = 3$ and $C_{out} = 64$: $1/64 + 1/9 \approx 0.115$ — about **8-9× fewer FLOPs** than standard convolution, with minimal accuracy loss.

### Where it is used

- **MobileNet** (Howard et al., 2017): built almost entirely from depthwise separable convolutions, enabling real-time inference on mobile devices.
- **MobileNetV2**: adds inverted residuals and linear bottlenecks on top of depthwise separable convs.
- **Xception** (Chollet, 2017): "extreme" Inception where the depthwise separable idea is pushed to the limit.
- **EfficientNet**: uses depthwise separable convs as a building block in its compound-scaled architecture.

### Why it works

The factorization is based on the **decoupling hypothesis**: spatial patterns (captured by the depthwise step) and channel patterns (captured by the pointwise step) are largely independent, so you can learn them separately without much loss. Empirically, this holds for most vision tasks — depthwise separable convs achieve ~95-99% of standard conv accuracy at ~10% of the cost.

## 2. Dilated (Atrous) Convolutions

### The problem: expanding the receptive field without losing resolution

For dense prediction tasks (semantic segmentation), you need a large receptive field (to see context) but you cannot downsample (you need pixel-level output). Standard solutions — pooling or strided convolutions — reduce spatial resolution, which destroys the pixel-level detail you need.

### The solution: insert spaces into the kernel

A **dilated convolution** (Yu and Koltun, 2016) inserts "holes" (spaces) between the kernel elements, expanding the receptive field without adding parameters or reducing resolution. A convolution with kernel size $K$ and dilation rate $r$ has an effective kernel size of $K + (K-1)(r-1) = K + (K-1)r - (K-1) = (K-1)r + 1$... let me just say the effective receptive field is $K \times r - (r-1)$... actually, the cleanest statement: a $3 \times 3$ kernel with dilation 2 sees a $5 \times 5$ area; with dilation 4, a $9 \times 9$ area.

Concretely, a $3 \times 3$ kernel with dilation 2 applies the 9 kernel weights at positions:

```
W . . W . . W
. . . . . . .
W . . W . . W
. . . . . . .
W . . W . . W
```

(where `W` is a kernel weight and `.` is skipped). The kernel still has 9 parameters, but it sees a $5 \times 5$ area.

### Exponential receptive field growth

Stacking dilated convolutions with exponentially increasing dilation rates grows the receptive field exponentially with depth:

- Layer 1: $3 \times 3$, dilation 1 → RF = 3
- Layer 2: $3 \times 3$, dilation 2 → RF = 7
- Layer 3: $3 \times 3$, dilation 4 → RF = 15
- Layer 4: $3 \times 3$, dilation 8 → RF = 31

With 4 layers, you get a receptive field of 31 — comparable to a deep stack of standard convolutions or pooling, but **without any resolution loss**.

### Where it is used

- **DeepLab** (Chen et al., 2017+): the leading semantic segmentation architecture, uses dilated convolutions to maintain resolution while gaining context. DeepLabv3 uses dilations of 6, 12, 18 in parallel (the "ASPP" — Atrous Spatial Pyramid Pooling module).
- **WaveNet** (van den Oord et al., 2016): audio generation, uses dilated convolutions to handle long-range temporal dependencies.
- **Context aggregation:** any task where you need a large receptive field without downsampling benefits from dilated convolutions.

### The gridding artifact

A pitfall: if you stack dilated convolutions with the same dilation rate, the receptive field has "holes" — pixels in the gaps never contribute. DeepLab addresses this by using different dilation rates in parallel (ASPP) or by varying rates across layers.

## 3. Transposed Convolutions

### The problem: upsampling

Standard convolutions (with stride > 1) and pooling **downsample** — they reduce spatial resolution. But for tasks like semantic segmentation or image generation, you need to **upsample** — increase spatial resolution from a low-resolution feature map to a high-resolution output.

### The solution: transposed convolution

A **transposed convolution** (sometimes inaccurately called "deconvolution") is the transpose of a regular convolution. It upsamples the input by scattering each input value across a larger output area according to the kernel.

For a $2 \times 2$ input, $3 \times 3$ kernel, stride 2, no padding:
- Each input pixel is multiplied by the $3 \times 3$ kernel and the result is added to a $3 \times 3$ patch of the output, with patches overlapping by the stride.
- The output is $4 \times 4$ (or more, depending on padding).

The name "transposed" comes from the matrix view: if a convolution is represented as a sparse matrix multiplication $Y = C X$ (where $C$ is the convolution matrix), the transposed convolution is $Y = C^\top X$ — the same matrix, transposed.

### Where it is used

- **U-Net** (Ronneberger et al., 2015): the decoder uses transposed convolutions to upsample the bottleneck features back to the input resolution, with skip connections from the encoder. The standard architecture for medical image segmentation.
- **GANs** (generator): the generator takes a noise vector and upsamples it through transposed convolutions to produce an image.
- **Semantic segmentation decoders:** any encoder-decoder segmentation architecture (DeepLab v3+, FCN) uses transposed convolutions (or bilinear upsampling + convolutions) in the decoder.

### The checkerboard artifact

A common pitfall: when the kernel size is not divisible by the stride, the transposed convolution produces uneven overlap — some output pixels receive contributions from multiple input pixels, others from only one. This creates a **checkerboard pattern** in the output. 

**Fix:** use kernel size divisible by stride (e.g., kernel 4, stride 2), or replace transposed convolutions with **upsampling + convolution** (resize the image with bilinear interpolation, then apply a regular convolution). The latter is preferred in many modern architectures because it avoids the checkerboard entirely.

## 4. Grouped Convolutions

### The idea: split the channels into groups

A **grouped convolution** splits the input channels into $G$ groups and applies a separate convolution to each group. The output channels are also split into $G$ groups, with group $i$ of the output computed only from group $i$ of the input.

For $C_{in}$ input channels, $C_{out}$ output channels, and $G$ groups:
- Each group has $C_{in}/G$ input channels and $C_{out}/G$ output channels.
- Each group's convolution is a standard conv with $C_{in}/G$ inputs and $C_{out}/G$ outputs.
- The $G$ outputs are concatenated.

### The cost reduction

The cost is $G$ times smaller than a standard convolution:

$$\text{FLOPs}_{grouped} = \frac{1}{G} \times \text{FLOPs}_{standard}$$

because each group's conv has $1/G$ the input channels and $1/G$ the output channels.

### Where it is used

- **AlexNet** (2012): used grouped convolutions to split the model across 2 GPUs (historical motivation — memory constraints).
- **ResNeXt** (Xie et al., 2017): showed that increasing the number of groups ($G = 32$) while keeping the total parameter count fixed improves accuracy. The "cardinality" (number of groups) is a new dimension of model capacity, alongside width and depth.
- **Depthwise convolution** (MobileNet): is the extreme case of grouped convolution with $G = C_{in} = C_{out}$ — each group has exactly one input and one output channel.

### Why it works

Grouped convolutions provide a different **structural prior**: instead of every output channel seeing every input channel (dense connectivity), each output channel sees only a subset. This can be beneficial because:
1. It forces the model to learn **decorrelated features** in different groups (a form of regularization).
2. It increases the "effective" number of paths through the network (cardinality), which ResNeXt showed is more efficient than just increasing width.

## What you should walk away with

1. **Depthwise separable** convolutions factor standard conv into depthwise (spatial) + pointwise (channel), reducing cost ~8-9× with minimal accuracy loss. Used in MobileNet and EfficientNet.
2. **Dilated** convolutions insert holes in the kernel, expanding the receptive field without reducing resolution. Used in DeepLab for segmentation.
3. **Transposed** convolutions are the transpose of regular convs, used for upsampling in decoders (U-Net) and generators (GANs). Watch for checkerboard artifacts.
4. **Grouped** convolutions split channels into groups, reducing cost by $1/G$ and increasing "cardinality." Used in ResNeXt.
5. These operators are essential for modern architectures — MobileNet, DeepLab, U-Net, ResNeXt all depend on them.
