# 1.4 Color Spaces and Models

> **Prerequisites:** [[1.1 What is a Digital Image]]
> **Connections:** [[1.7 The Color Image Processing Pipeline]] (next — how cameras produce RGB), [[2.2 Point Processing Operations]] (gamma correction)

A color model is a mathematical way to describe colors as tuples of numbers (e.g., triplets or quadruplets). Different models exist because they solve different problems (displaying light, printing ink, or human perception). This note covers the major color models and the conversion logic between them.

---

## 1. RGB (Red, Green, Blue)

**Type:** Additive Color Model.
**Used In:** Monitors, Cameras, Screens.

- **Concept:** It models the mixing of **light**. All colors start from black (no light) and are built by adding wavelengths.
    - **Black:** No light $(0,0,0)$.
    - **White:** All light at max intensity $(255,255,255)$.
    - **Colors:** Mixed by adding wavelengths. Red + Green = Yellow.
- **Geometry:** It is represented as a **Cartesian Unit Cube**.
    - Axes: R, G, B.
    - The "Main Diagonal" from $(0,0,0)$ to $(255,255,255)$ represents the **Grayscale** line. Points on this line have equal amounts of R, G, and B ($r=g=b$).

**Limitations of RGB for processing:** RGB is device-dependent (different cameras/monitors produce different RGB values for the same color), non-intuitive (hard to reason about "make this more pink" in RGB), and **correlated** (changing brightness changes all three channels). For image processing, we often convert to a perceptual space (HSV, LAB) or a luminance-chrominance space (YCbCr, YUV).

---

## 2. CMYK (Cyan, Magenta, Yellow, Key/Black)

**Type:** Subtractive Color Model.
**Used In:** Printing.

- **Concept:** It models the mixing of **pigments/ink**. Pigments absorb (subtract) light. White paper reflects all light; adding ink subtracts wavelengths.
    - **Cyan** absorbs Red (reflects Green and Blue).
    - **Magenta** absorbs Green.
    - **Yellow** absorbs Blue.
- **Why K (Key/Black)?** Theoretically, C+M+Y = Black. In practice, mixing all three creates a muddy brown and uses expensive ink. Pure Black ink (Key) is added for sharp text, deep blacks, and cost savings (black ink is cheaper than three colors).

---

## 3. HSL / HSV (Hue, Saturation, Lightness/Value)

**Type:** Perceptual Model.
**Used In:** Color Pickers, Image Editing, Human-Computer Interaction.

RGB is not intuitive. If I ask you to "make this pixel a bit more pink," calculating the change in R, G, and B is hard. HSL/HSV solves this by decomposing color into three perceptual axes:

- **Hue (H):** The "type" of color (Red, Orange, Blue). Measured in degrees ($0^\circ$ to $360^\circ$) around a color wheel. Red is 0°, Green is 120°, Blue is 240°.
- **Saturation (S):** The "purity" of the color. 100% = vivid color; 0% = gray.
- **Lightness (L) / Value (V):** The brightness. 0% = Black; 100% = White (HSL) or the full color (HSV).

**HSL vs HSV:** the difference is in how Lightness vs Value treats full saturation. In HSL, L=50% is the pure color and L=100% is white. In HSV, V=100% is the pure color. HSV is more common in computer vision; HSL is more common in graphics tools.

**Use case in processing:** If you want to detect a "Red Car" in an image with shadows, RGB is terrible because the shadow changes all three RGB values. In HSV, the shadow only changes the Value (V), but the Hue (H) remains "Red." This makes object detection much more robust to illumination changes.

---

## 4. YCbCr / YUV (Luminance-Chrominance)

**Type:** Luminance-Chrominance separation.
**Used In:** JPEG compression, video (MPEG, TV broadcasting).

These spaces separate **luminance** (brightness, Y) from **chrominance** (color information, Cb/Cr or U/V). The rationale: the human eye is more sensitive to brightness than to color, so we can compress the chrominance more aggressively without visible quality loss. JPEG and video codecs subsample the chrominance (e.g., 4:2:0 — one Cb/Cr value per 4 pixels) while keeping full luminance resolution.

**Conversion from RGB:**
$$Y = 0.299R + 0.587G + 0.114B$$
$$Cb = 0.564(B - Y)$$
$$Cr = 0.713(R - Y)$$

The Y formula is the **luminance** — the same weighted average used for grayscale conversion (reflecting the eye's greater sensitivity to green).

---

## 5. CIELAB (LAB)

**Type:** Perceptually uniform color space.
**Used In:** Color difference measurement, color-based segmentation.

LAB was designed to be **perceptually uniform** — a fixed Euclidean distance in LAB corresponds to a fixed perceptual color difference, regardless of where in the color space you are. (RGB and HSV are not perceptually uniform — a distance of 10 in the blue region looks different from a distance of 10 in the yellow region.)

- **L:** Lightness (0 = black, 100 = white).
- **a:** Green-red axis (negative = green, positive = red).
- **b:** Blue-yellow axis (negative = blue, positive = yellow).

**Use case:** if you want to measure "how different are these two colors" or segment by color, LAB is the right space — Euclidean distance in LAB is meaningful. RGB distance is not.

---

## 6. Grayscale Conversion (Luminance)

Converting a color image to grayscale is not just `(R+G+B)/3`. The human eye is more sensitive to Green light than Red or Blue (due to the relative density of cone cells). Therefore, we use a weighted average to calculate Luminance ($Y$):

$$ Y = 0.299R + 0.587G + 0.114B $$

- The weight for Green (0.587) is much higher than Blue (0.114), reflecting the eye's sensitivity.
- This is the same Y used in YCbCr.
- A naive average `(R+G+B)/3` produces a grayscale image that looks "off" — blues appear too bright, greens too dark.

---

## Choosing the right color space

| Task | Recommended space | Why |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Display / storage | RGB | Native to displays and most image formats |
| Color-based detection | HSV | Shadow/lighting changes only affect V, not H |
| Color difference measurement | LAB | Perceptually uniform — Euclidean distance is meaningful |
| Compression | YCbCr | Subsample chrominance (human eye is less sensitive to color detail) |
| Printing | CMYK | Subtractive model matches ink-on-paper physics |
| Grayscale conversion | RGB → Y | Weighted luminance reflects human perception |

---

## What you should walk away with

1. **RGB** is additive (light), device-dependent, and non-intuitive for processing.
2. **CMYK** is subtractive (ink), used for printing.
3. **HSL/HSV** is perceptual — separates hue, saturation, brightness. Best for illumination-robust color detection.
4. **YCbCr/YUV** separates luminance from chrominance — the basis of JPEG and video compression (chroma subsampling).
5. **LAB** is perceptually uniform — Euclidean distance is meaningful. Best for color difference and color-based segmentation.
6. Grayscale conversion uses weighted luminance $Y = 0.299R + 0.587G + 0.114B$, reflecting the eye's green sensitivity — not a naive average.
