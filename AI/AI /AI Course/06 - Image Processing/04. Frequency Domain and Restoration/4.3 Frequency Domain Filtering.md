# 4.3 Frequency Domain Filtering

> **Prerequisites:** [[4.1 Introduction to the Frequency Domain]], [[4.2 The Fourier Transform]]
> **Connections:** [[3.2 Linear Smoothing Filters]] (spatial low-pass), [[4.4 Image Degradation and Noise Models]]

The convolution theorem ($\mathcal{F}\{f * g\} = F \cdot G$) means that filtering in the spatial domain is **multiplication** in the frequency domain. This note covers how to design filters directly in the frequency domain — low-pass, high-pass, band-reject, and notch — and why this is sometimes easier and faster than spatial filtering.

---

## The frequency-domain filtering pipeline

To filter an image in the frequency domain:

1. **Pad** the image (to avoid wraparound artifacts from the DFT's periodicity assumption — usually pad to 2× the image size).
2. **FFT:** compute $F = \text{FFT}(f)$.
3. **Shift:** move zero-frequency to the center: $F_{shifted} = \text{fftshift}(F)$.
4. **Multiply** by a filter mask $H$: $G_{shifted} = F_{shifted} \cdot H$.
5. **Unshift:** $G = \text{ifftshift}(G_{shifted})$.
6. **Inverse FFT:** $g = \text{IFFT}(G)$.
7. **Crop** back to the original size (remove the padding).

The filter $H$ is a 2D array of the same size as the (padded) image, with values that specify how much of each frequency to keep (1 = keep, 0 = remove, values in between = partial).

---

## Low-pass filters (blurring)

A low-pass filter keeps low frequencies (near the center) and removes high frequencies (near the edges). The result is a blurred image — the high-frequency detail is gone.

### Ideal low-pass filter

$$H(u, v) = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } D(u, v) \leq D_0 \\ 0 & \text{if } D(u, v) > D_0 \end{cases}$$

where $D(u, v) = \sqrt{(u - u_c)^2 + (v - v_c)^2}$ is the distance from the center and $D_0$ is the cutoff frequency.

**Problem:** the sharp cutoff causes **ringing artifacts** (Gibbs phenomenon) — ripples around edges in the filtered image. This is the frequency-domain equivalent of the spatial sinc-function kernel (which has infinite support). Ideal filters are mathematically clean but visually poor.

### Butterworth low-pass filter

$$H(u, v) = \frac{1}{1 + [D(u, v) / D_0]^{2n}}$$

where $n$ is the filter **order**. $n=1$ is maximally smooth (no ringing); higher $n$ approaches the ideal filter (more ringing but sharper cutoff). $n=2$ is a common compromise.

The Butterworth filter transitions smoothly from passband to stopband, avoiding the ideal filter's ringing. It is the standard choice when you want a controlled tradeoff between sharpness and ringing.

### Gaussian low-pass filter

$$H(u, v) = \exp\left(-\frac{D(u, v)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)$$

The Gaussian has no ringing (it is smooth everywhere) and its inverse FFT is also a Gaussian (so the equivalent spatial filter is also a Gaussian — this is a special property of Gaussians). The Gaussian is the "friendliest" filter — no artifacts — but has a gradual cutoff that may not remove enough high-frequency noise.

**Key fact:** the Gaussian low-pass filter in the frequency domain corresponds exactly to the Gaussian blur in the spatial domain (see [[3.2 Linear Smoothing Filters]]). They are the same operation, just in different domains. This is why the convolution theorem is useful — you can choose whichever domain is more convenient.

---

## High-pass filters (sharpening / edge extraction)

A high-pass filter keeps high frequencies and removes low frequencies. This extracts edges and fine detail.

$$H_{HP}(u, v) = 1 - H_{LP}(u, v)$$

Any low-pass filter can be turned into a high-pass filter by subtracting it from 1. So there are ideal, Butterworth, and Gaussian high-pass filters, with the same tradeoffs.

**Note:** a pure high-pass filter removes the DC component (the image's average brightness), so the result has negative values and zero mean. For display, you usually add back the DC or offset the result. A **high-boost filter** adds back a fraction of the original: $H_{HB} = (A - 1) + H_{HP}$, which sharpens while preserving brightness (see [[3.5 Sharpening and Edge Detection]]).

---

## Band-reject and band-pass filters

A **band-reject** filter removes a specific band of frequencies (between $D_0$ and $D_1$), keeping both lower and higher frequencies. A **band-pass** does the reverse — keeps only the band.

These are useful when the noise or signal of interest occupies a known frequency range.

---

## Notch filters — the killer use case

A **notch filter** removes specific frequencies (or small regions around them) rather than a whole band. This is the frequency domain's superpower: **surgical removal of periodic noise**.

### The problem: periodic noise

If an image has periodic interference (e.g., from electrical noise at 50/60 Hz, or from a scanning device's mechanical rhythm), this appears as **distinct spikes** in the frequency spectrum — bright points at the interference frequency. The noise is concentrated at specific, known frequencies, not spread across the spectrum.

### The solution: notch filter

Identify the spikes in the magnitude spectrum (they are visually obvious — bright dots away from the center), and set $H = 0$ at those locations (small neighborhoods around each spike). Inverse-FFT to get the cleaned image.

This is **nearly impossible in the spatial domain** — periodic noise's spatial kernel is a sinusoid with infinite support, so spatial convolution would require an infinitely large kernel. In the frequency domain, it is pointwise multiplication — trivial.

**Procedure:**
1. FFT + fftshift the image.
2. Visually inspect the magnitude spectrum for spikes.
3. Manually or automatically create a mask $H$ that is 0 at the spike locations and 1 elsewhere.
4. Multiply, inverse-FFT.

This is the standard method for removing moiré patterns, electrical interference, and scanning artifacts.

---

## When to filter in frequency vs spatial domain

| Situation | Better domain | Why |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Small kernel (3×3, 5×5) | Spatial | FFT overhead dominates |
| Large kernel (15×15+) | Frequency | $O(N \log N)$ beats $O(N^2 k^2)$ |
| Periodic noise removal | Frequency | Spatial equivalent has infinite support |
| Gaussian blur | Either | Same operation; frequency is faster for large $\sigma$ |
| Non-linear filter (median, etc.) | Spatial | No frequency equivalent (convolution theorem only applies to linear filters) |
| Custom frequency response | Frequency | Design $H$ directly |

**Rule of thumb:** for linear filters with kernels larger than ~13×13, the frequency domain is faster. For non-linear filters or small kernels, the spatial domain is better.

---

## What you should walk away with

1. Frequency-domain filtering = FFT the image, multiply by a filter mask, inverse-FFT.
2. **Low-pass filters:** ideal (ringing), Butterworth (controlled tradeoff), Gaussian (no ringing, gradual cutoff). Gaussian in frequency = Gaussian in space.
3. **High-pass:** $1 - H_{LP}$, extracts edges but removes brightness (use high-boost to preserve it).
4. **Notch filters** are the killer use case: surgically remove periodic noise by zeroing specific frequencies. Nearly impossible in the spatial domain.
5. Frequency domain is faster for large kernels ($O(N \log N)$ vs $O(N^2 k^2)$); spatial domain is better for small kernels and non-linear filters.
