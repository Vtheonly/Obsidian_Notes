The Fourier Transform is the mathematical operation that converts a signal from the spatial (or time) domain to the frequency domain. This note covers the 1D and 2D Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT), the magnitude and phase spectra, and the critical insight that **phase matters more than magnitude** for image structure.

---

## The 1D Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT)

For a discrete signal $f[n]$ of length $N$ (e.g., one row of an image), the DFT is:

$$F[k] = \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} f[n] \cdot e^{-j 2\pi kn / N}, \quad k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$$

Each $F[k]$ is a **complex number** representing the amplitude and phase of the sinusoidal component at frequency $k$. The inverse DFT reconstructs the original signal:

$$f[n] = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{k=0}^{N-1} F[k] \cdot e^{j 2\pi kn / N}$$

The DFT decomposes the signal into $N$ frequency components; the inverse DFT sums them back. No information is lost — the DFT is a **change of representation**, not an approximation.

### Euler's formula and the two parts of $F[k]$

Using Euler's formula $e^{-j\theta} = \cos\theta - j\sin\theta$, each $F[k]$ can be written as:

$$F[k] = \sum_n f[n] \cos(2\pi kn/N) - j \sum_n f[n] \sin(2\pi kn/N)$$

So $F[k]$ has a **real part** (the cosine component) and an **imaginary part** (the sine component). The **magnitude** $|F[k]| = \sqrt{\text{Re}^2 + \text{Im}^2}$ is the amplitude of that frequency; the **phase** $\angle F[k] = \arctan(\text{Im}/\text{Re})$ is the phase offset.

## The 2D DFT (for images)

For a 2D image $f[x, y]$ of size $M \times N$, the 2D DFT is:

$$F[u, v] = \sum_{x=0}^{M-1} \sum_{y=0}^{N-1} f[x, y] \cdot e^{-j 2\pi (ux/M + vy/N)}$$

Each $F[u, v]$ represents a 2D sinusoidal grating at spatial frequency $(u, v)$ — $u$ is the horizontal frequency, $v$ is the vertical frequency. The magnitude $|F[u, v]|$ is the strength of that grating in the image; the phase $\angle F[u, v]$ is its position.

### The FFT (Fast Fourier Transform)

Computing the DFT directly is $O(N^2)$ for a 1D signal of length $N$ — too slow for images. The **Fast Fourier Transform** (Cooley and Tukey, 1965) computes the same result in $O(N \log N)$ by exploiting the symmetry of the complex exponentials. For a 512×512 image, this is the difference between minutes and milliseconds. **Always use the FFT**, not the direct DFT.

```python
import numpy as np
F = np.fft.fft2(image)           # 2D FFT
F_shifted = np.fft.fftshift(F)   # shift zero-frequency to center (for visualization)
magnitude = np.log(np.abs(F_shifted) + 1)  # log-scaled magnitude for display
```

## The magnitude and phase spectra

The DFT produces a complex-valued array. We usually visualize two things:

### The magnitude spectrum

$|F[u, v]|$ — the strength of each frequency. Because magnitudes span a huge range (the DC component $F[0,0]$ is the sum of all pixels, which is enormous), we display the **log magnitude** $\log(|F[u,v]| + 1)$ for visualization.

The magnitude spectrum tells you **how much of each frequency is present**. For a typical natural image:
- The DC component (center, after fftshift) is the brightest — the image's average brightness.
- Low frequencies (near the center) are bright — smooth structure dominates.
- High frequencies (near the edges) are dim — fine detail and noise are less energetic.
- The spectrum is roughly radially symmetric for isotropic images (no preferred direction).

### The phase spectrum

$\angle F[u, v]$ — the phase of each frequency. This looks like noise when visualized, but it is **critically important** — it encodes the spatial structure (where edges and features are located).

---

## The key insight: phase matters more than magnitude

This is the most important and surprising fact about the Fourier transform of images.

**Experiment:** take two images A and B. Compute their DFTs. Now reconstruct:
- Image 1: magnitude of A + phase of B.
- Image 2: magnitude of B + phase of A.

**Result:** Image 1 looks like image B (the phase donor); Image 2 looks like image A. The phase, not the magnitude, determines what the image looks like.

**Why?** The magnitude tells you *which frequencies are present*; the phase tells you *how they are aligned*. Edges, lines, and object boundaries are defined by the alignment of frequencies (constructive interference at the edge, destructive elsewhere). If you keep the phases but change the magnitudes, the edges are still there — just at different contrast. If you keep the magnitudes but scramble the phases, the frequencies are all present but misaligned — the image becomes unrecognizable noise.

**Practical implication:** for most image processing, we manipulate the magnitude (filtering, compression) and leave the phase alone. The phase is too important to change.

---

## The fftshift convention

The DFT output has the zero-frequency (DC) component at position $[0, 0]$ (top-left corner), with frequencies increasing outward and wrapping around (high frequencies at the corners). For visualization, we **shift** the zero-frequency to the center using `fftshift`, so the spectrum is symmetric around the center — low frequencies in the middle, high frequencies at the edges. This is the convention for all frequency-domain visualization and filtering.

---

## Properties of the Fourier Transform

These properties are essential for understanding frequency-domain filtering:

1. **Linearity:** $\mathcal{F}\{a \cdot f + b \cdot g\} = a \cdot F + b \cdot G$.
2. **Convolution theorem:** $\mathcal{F}\{f * g\} = F \cdot G$. Spatial convolution = frequency multiplication. This is the basis for efficient large-kernel filtering.
3. **Correlation theorem:** $\mathcal{F}\{f \star g\} = F^* \cdot G$ (where $*$ is complex conjugate). Used for template matching.
4. **Shift invariance:** shifting $f$ in space only changes the phase of $F$, not the magnitude. (This is why the magnitude spectrum is shift-invariant — useful for recognition.)
5. **Rotation:** rotating $f$ by $\theta$ rotates $F$ by $\theta$. The spectrum rotates with the image.
6. **Scaling:** scaling $f$ by $a$ scales $F$ by $1/a$ (and vice versa). Enlarging the image in space compresses the spectrum in frequency.

---

## What you should walk away with

1. The DFT converts a signal to its frequency components; each $F[k]$ is a complex number (magnitude = amplitude, phase = offset).
2. The 2D DFT decomposes an image into 2D sinusoidal gratings; the magnitude spectrum shows the strength of each frequency, the phase spectrum shows the alignment.
3. The **FFT** computes the DFT in $O(N \log N)$ instead of $O(N^2)$ — always use it.
4. **Phase matters more than magnitude** — the phase encodes the image's structure (edges, features). The magnitude only encodes contrast.
5. The **convolution theorem** ($\mathcal{F}\{f * g\} = F \cdot G$) is the basis for efficient filtering: spatial convolution = frequency multiplication.
6. `fftshift` moves the zero-frequency to the center for visualization and filtering.
