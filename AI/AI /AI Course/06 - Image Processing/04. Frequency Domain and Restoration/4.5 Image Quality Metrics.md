# 4.5 Image Quality Metrics — MSE, PSNR, SSIM, and Beyond

> **Prerequisites:** [[4.2 The Fourier Transform]], [[4.4 Image Degradation and Noise Models]]
> **Connections:** [[03. Regression Metrics - MAE, MSE, RMSE, R2, MAPE]] (regression metrics), [[05. Computer Vision Metrics - IoU, mAP, Dice]] (CV metrics)

After compressing, denoising, or restoring an image, you need to measure **how good the result is**. This note covers the standard image quality metrics: MSE and PSNR (simple, widely used but limited), SSIM (perceptually motivated), and modern perceptual metrics (LPIPS, MS-SSIM). Each metric captures a different notion of "quality," and choosing the right one matters.

---

## The problem: what is "quality"?

If you have an original image $f$ and a processed version $\hat{f}$ (compressed, denoised, restored), how do you measure the difference? The naive answer — compare pixel by pixel — is unsatisfying because it does not match **human perception**. A slightly shifted edge (invisible to humans) produces large pixel-wise error; a smooth blur (very visible to humans) produces small pixel-wise error. Good quality metrics must capture **perceptual** quality, not just numerical difference.

---

## 1. Mean Squared Error (MSE)

$$\text{MSE} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i} (f_i - \hat{f}_i)^2$$

The average squared difference between original and processed pixels.

- **Pros:** simple, differentiable (usable as a loss function), fast.
- **Cons:** does not match perception. Two images with the same MSE can look very different (one may have visible artifacts, the other a uniform slight blur).
- **Use:** as a training loss, or as a baseline metric. Never as the sole quality measure.

See [[03. Regression Metrics - MAE, MSE, RMSE, R2, MAPE]] for MSE's properties as a regression metric.

---

## 2. Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio (PSNR)

$$\text{PSNR} = 10 \log_{10}\left(\frac{\text{MAX}^2}{\text{MSE}}\right) \quad \text{(dB)}$$

where MAX is the maximum possible pixel value (255 for 8-bit images).

- **Pros:** logarithmic scale (intuitive — each 10 dB is a 10× improvement in MSE), widely used in compression and denoising literature.
- **Cons:** still based on MSE, so it inherits MSE's perceptual mismatch. A PSNR of 30 dB is "good" but does not guarantee visual quality.
- **Typical values:**
    - 20-30 dB: acceptable quality (visible artifacts).
    - 30-40 dB: good quality (minor artifacts).
    - 40+ dB: excellent quality (near-transparent).
    - > 50 dB: visually indistinguishable from the original.

**PSNR is the standard metric for compression benchmarks** (JPEG, JPEG2000, video codecs), despite its flaws, because it is simple and reproducible. Always report it alongside a perceptual metric (SSIM).

---

## 3. Structural Similarity Index (SSIM)

SSIM (Wang et al., 2004) is the first major **perceptually-motivated** metric. Instead of comparing pixels, it compares **structural information** — the patterns of pixel values that convey structural content (edges, textures, object shapes).

### The SSIM formula

SSIM compares local windows (typically 11×11 or 8×8) of the two images, measuring three aspects:

$$\text{SSIM}(x, y) = \frac{(2\mu_x \mu_y + C_1)(2\sigma_{xy} + C_2)}{(\mu_x^2 + \mu_y^2 + C_1)(\sigma_x^2 + \sigma_y^2 + C_2)}$$

- **Luminance:** $(2\mu_x \mu_y + C_1) / (\mu_x^2 + \mu_y^2 + C_1)$ — compares the means (brightness).
- **Contrast:** $(2\sigma_x \sigma_y + C_2) / (\sigma_x^2 + \sigma_y^2 + C_2)$ — compares the standard deviations (contrast).
- **Structure:** $(\sigma_{xy} + C_3) / (\sigma_x \sigma_y + C_3)$ — compares the correlation (structural similarity, normalized by contrast).

where $\mu$ is the mean, $\sigma$ is the standard deviation, $\sigma_{xy}$ is the covariance, and $C_1, C_2, C_3$ are small constants to avoid division by zero.

The overall SSIM is the mean of the local SSIM values over all windows. It ranges from $-1$ to $1$, with $1$ = perfect match.

### Why SSIM is better than MSE

SSIM captures **structural** similarity, which is what humans perceive. Two images with the same MSE but different structure (one has a shifted edge, the other a uniform blur) will have different SSIM — the shifted edge preserves structure (high SSIM), the blur destroys it (low SSIM). This matches perception.

### Limitations of SSIM

- Still a **local** metric (window-based) — does not capture global artifacts.
- Assumes the degradation is local and moderate — fails on severe distortions (e.g., color shifts, geometric warps).
- The fixed window size may not match the scale of the artifacts.

---

## 4. MS-SSIM (Multi-Scale SSIM)

MS-SSIM (Wang et al., 2003) computes SSIM at **multiple scales** (by downsampling the image repeatedly) and combines the results. This captures quality at different frequencies — fine detail at the original scale, coarse structure at downsampled scales.

MS-SSIM outperforms single-scale SSIM, especially for images with artifacts at specific scales (e.g., JPEG block artifacts at the 8×8 scale). It is the standard perceptual metric for image compression research.

---

## 5. LPIPS (Learned Perceptual Image Patch Similarity)

LPIPS (Zhang et al., 2018) is a **deep-learning-based** perceptual metric. It extracts features from a pretrained CNN (VGG, AlexNet, or SqueezeNet) at multiple layers for both images, and computes the weighted L2 distance between the feature representations.

- **Why it works:** the CNN has been trained on ImageNet to recognize objects, so its features capture **semantic** structure (edges, textures, object parts). Two images that look similar to a human produce similar CNN features, even if they differ in pixels.
- **Advantage over SSIM:** LPIPS captures high-level perceptual quality that SSIM misses (e.g., a generated face that looks "uncanny" may have high SSIM but high LPIPS distance).
- **Use:** the standard metric for generative models (GANs, diffusion) and image restoration (super-resolution, denoising). If you are training a GAN, you report LPIPS, not just MSE/SSIM.

---

## Choosing the right metric

| Task | Primary metric | Also report |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Compression (JPEG, video) | PSNR | MS-SSIM |
| Denoising / restoration | PSNR, SSIM | LPIPS |
| Super-resolution | SSIM, LPIPS | PSNR |
| Generative models (GAN, diffusion) | LPIPS, FID | SSIM |
| Benchmarking / comparison | All of the above | — |

**The golden rule:** never report only MSE/PSNR. Always include a perceptual metric (SSIM at minimum, LPIPS for generative models). MSE/PSNR are reproducible but perceptually blind; the perceptual metrics catch quality issues that MSE misses.

---

## The ultimate metric: human evaluation

No computational metric perfectly matches human perception. For final quality assessment (e.g., comparing two compression standards), the gold standard is **human subjective evaluation** — show the images to human raters and collect Mean Opinion Scores (MOS). This is expensive and slow, which is why computational metrics are used for development, but final benchmarks use human studies.

---

## What you should walk away with

1. **MSE:** simple pixel-wise error, does not match perception. Use as a loss or baseline, never as the sole quality measure.
2. **PSNR:** log-scale MSE, standard for compression benchmarks (20-40 dB typical). Still perceptually limited.
3. **SSIM:** perceptually motivated — compares luminance, contrast, and structure in local windows. Captures structural similarity that MSE misses.
4. **MS-SSIM:** multi-scale SSIM, captures quality at different scales. Standard for compression research.
5. **LPIPS:** deep-learning-based, compares CNN features. Standard for generative models and restoration.
6. **Always report a perceptual metric alongside PSNR.** The golden rule: never report only MSE/PSNR.
7. The ultimate metric is human evaluation (MOS) — computational metrics approximate it.
