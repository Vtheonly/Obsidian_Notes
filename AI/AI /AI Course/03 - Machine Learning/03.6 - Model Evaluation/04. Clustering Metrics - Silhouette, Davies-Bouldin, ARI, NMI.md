# 04. Clustering Metrics — Silhouette, Davies-Bouldin, ARI, NMI

> **Prerequisites:** [[01. K-Means Clustering]], [[01. Model Evaluation and Metrics]]
> **Connections:** [[03. Regression Metrics]] (previous), [[05. Computer Vision Metrics - IoU, mAP, Dice]] (next)

The vault's K-Means note covers the algorithm and WCSS (within-cluster sum of squares, also called inertia) but does not cover how to **evaluate** clustering quality. This is a critical gap: WCSS always decreases as you add more clusters (trivially, $K = N$ clusters gives WCSS = 0), so it cannot be used to choose $K$ or compare algorithms. This note fills the gap.

---

## The clustering evaluation problem

Clustering is **unsupervised** — there are no ground-truth labels. So how do you evaluate it? There are two cases:

1. **No ground truth available** (the typical case): evaluate based on the **intrinsic structure** of the clusters — are they compact and well-separated? Metrics: Silhouette, Davies-Bouldin, Calinski-Harabasz.

2. **Ground truth available** (e.g., for benchmarking): compare the predicted clusters to the true labels. Metrics: Adjusted Rand Index (ARI), Normalized Mutual Information (NMI), V-measure.

---

## Intrinsic metrics (no ground truth)

### Silhouette Coefficient

For each sample $i$:

$$s(i) = \frac{b(i) - a(i)}{\max(a(i), b(i))}$$

where:
- $a(i)$ = mean intra-cluster distance (average distance from $i$ to other points in its cluster).
- $b(i)$ = mean nearest-cluster distance (the smallest average distance from $i$ to points in any other cluster).

The Silhouette Coefficient is the average of $s(i)$ over all samples. It ranges from $-1$ to $+1$:
- $+1$: sample is far from neighboring clusters, close to its own — well-clustered.
- $0$: sample is on the boundary between two clusters — ambiguous.
- $-1$: sample is closer to a different cluster than its own — misclassified.

**When to use it:**
- To choose $K$ in K-Means: compute Silhouette for $K = 2, 3, \ldots, 10$ and pick the $K$ with the highest score.
- To compare clustering algorithms on the same data.
- To identify problematic samples (those with negative Silhouette are likely misclustered).

**Pitfalls:**
- Computationally expensive: $O(n^2)$ for all pairwise distances. Use sampling for large datasets.
- Biased toward convex (round) clusters — does not work well for non-convex clusters (e.g., moons, circles) even if the clustering is correct.

### Davies-Bouldin Index

$$DB = \frac{1}{K} \sum_{i=1}^K \max_{j \neq i} \frac{\sigma_i + \sigma_j}{d(c_i, c_j)}$$

where:
- $K$ is the number of clusters.
- $\sigma_i$ is the average distance from points in cluster $i$ to its centroid (cluster "spread").
- $d(c_i, c_j)$ is the distance between centroids of clusters $i$ and $j$.

**Lower is better.** The index measures the worst-case ratio of (within-cluster spread) to (between-cluster distance) — you want compact clusters far apart, which gives a low DB.

**When to use it:**
- Simpler and faster than Silhouette ($O(nK)$ instead of $O(n^2)$).
- Good for quick cluster-quality checks.

**Pitfalls:**
- Same convexity bias as Silhouette.
- The "max" makes it sensitive to the worst cluster pair.

### Calinski-Harabasz Index (Variance Ratio Criterion)

$$CH = \frac{\text{between-cluster dispersion}}{\text{within-cluster dispersion}} \times \frac{n - K}{K - 1}$$

**Higher is better.** Measures the ratio of between-cluster variance to within-cluster variance, normalized by degrees of freedom.

**When to use it:**
- Very fast to compute ($O(n)$).
- Good for high-dimensional data.

**Pitfalls:** Same convexity bias; biased toward larger $K$ (the normalization helps but does not fully correct).

## Extrinsic metrics (with ground truth)

These compare predicted clusters to true labels. They do not require the label and cluster IDs to match — they measure whether samples in the same true class are in the same predicted cluster (and vice versa).

### Adjusted Rand Index (ARI)

The Rand Index measures the fraction of pairs of samples that are "correctly" grouped (both in the same cluster, or both in different clusters). The **Adjusted** Rand Index corrects for chance:

$$ARI = \frac{RI - \mathbb{E}[RI]}{\max(RI) - \mathbb{E}[RI]}$$

- $ARI = 1$: perfect match.
- $ARI = 0$: random clustering.
- $ARI < 0$: worse than random (rare).

**When to use it:**
- When you have ground-truth labels and want to compare clusterings.
- When the number of clusters may differ from the number of true classes.
- Range is bounded and interpretable.

**Pitfalls:**
- Requires ground truth (not available in most real clustering applications).
- Sensitive to the number of clusters — tends to be higher when $K$ matches the true number of classes.

### Normalized Mutual Information (NMI)

$$NMI = \frac{I(C, Y)}{\sqrt{H(C) \cdot H(Y)}}$$

where $I(C, Y)$ is the mutual information between clusters $C$ and labels $Y$, and $H$ is entropy.

- $NMI = 1$: perfect match.
- $NMI = 0$: clusters and labels are independent.

**When to use it:**
- When you want an information-theoretic measure (how much does knowing the cluster tell you about the label?).
- Handles different numbers of clusters and classes naturally.

**Pitfalls:** Not adjusted for chance (NMI is not zero for random clustering when $K$ is large). Use **Adjusted Mutual Information (AMI)** for a chance-corrected version.

### V-measure

$$V = \frac{2 \cdot \text{homogeneity} \cdot \text{completeness}}{\text{homogeneity} + \text{completeness}}$$

where:
- **Homogeneity:** each cluster contains only members of a single class (precision-like).
- **Completeness:** all members of a class are in the same cluster (recall-like).

V-measure is the harmonic mean of homogeneity and completeness — analogous to F1 score for clustering.

**When to use it:**
- When you want to separately diagnose whether the problem is "impure clusters" (low homogeneity) or "split classes" (low completeness).

---

## The decision framework

| Situation | Recommended metric |
| :--- | :--- |
| No ground truth, choosing K | Silhouette (compute for each K, pick the max) |
| No ground truth, fast check | Davies-Bouldin or Calinski-Harabasz |
| Ground truth available | ARI (chance-corrected, bounded) or AMI |
| Information-theoretic preference | NMI (or AMI for chance correction) |
| Diagnosing purity vs completeness | V-measure (with homogeneity and completeness reported separately) |

---

## The convexity caveat

All the intrinsic metrics (Silhouette, Davies-Bouldin, Calinski-Harabasz) implicitly favor **convex** clusters — round, compact blobs. They will give poor scores to correct clusterings of non-convex data (e.g., two concentric circles, a moons dataset) even when the clustering is semantically correct.

For non-convex data:
- Use **DBSCAN** or **spectral clustering** (which handle non-convex shapes) instead of K-Means.
- Evaluate with extrinsic metrics (if ground truth is available) or with **density-based metrics** (DBCV — Density-Based Cluster Validity).

---

## What you should walk away with

1. WCSS/inertia always decreases with $K$ — it cannot be used to choose $K$ or compare algorithms.
2. **Silhouette** (intrinsic, no ground truth): measures per-sample compactness vs. separation. Use to choose $K$.
3. **Davies-Bouldin** (intrinsic): faster than Silhouette, lower is better.
4. **ARI** (extrinsic, with ground truth): chance-corrected agreement between clusters and labels.
5. **NMI** (extrinsic): information-theoretic, how much clusters tell you about labels.
6. **V-measure** (extrinsic): harmonic mean of homogeneity and completeness.
7. All intrinsic metrics favor convex clusters — use DBSCAN + DBCV for non-convex data.
8. The choice depends on whether you have ground truth and what aspect of clustering quality you care about.
