# 03. From RNNs to Attention — The Historical Bridge

> **Connections:** [[08 - Attention and Transformers]] (the chapter this note hands off to)

---

## The story so far, in four limitations

By 2014, the Encoder-Decoder RNN was the state of the art for sequence-to-sequence tasks like translation. But it had four well-understood limitations:

1. **The bottleneck** ([[01. The Bottleneck Problem - Why One Vector Is Not Enough]]): the entire input must be compressed into a single fixed-size vector $c$, which is information-theoretically impossible for long inputs.
2. **No lookback** (same note): the decoder cannot revisit specific input tokens; it is stuck with whatever made it into $c$.
3. **Long-range decay** ([[02. Long-Range Dependencies and Why RNNs Struggle]]): even with LSTMs, information from far back in the input is degraded by the time it reaches the decoder.
4. **Sequential processing**: the recurrence is inherently sequential — step $t$ cannot be computed until step $t-1$ is done. This makes training slow on long sequences and forbids parallelism across time.

Each of these is a consequence of the same design choice: **the only connection between encoder and decoder is a single vector, passed through a sequential chain of transformations.**

---

## The 2015 fix: attention (Bahdanau et al.)

Bahdanau, Cho, and Bengio (2015) addressed limitations 1, 2, and 3 with a single idea: **let the decoder compute a different context vector $c_t$ at each output step, as a weighted average of all encoder hidden states.**

$$c_t = \sum_{i=1}^{T} \alpha_{t,i} \cdot h_i^{enc}$$

where $\alpha_{t,i}$ is an attention weight — how much output step $t$ should attend to input step $i$.

This shatters the bottleneck (limitation 1): the decoder is no longer limited to a fixed-size summary. It grants lookback (limitation 2): the decoder can attend to any input position at any output step. And it addresses long-range decay (limitation 3): the path from input $i$ to output $t$ is now a single weighted connection, not a chain of $|t - i|$ recurrent steps.

Limitation 4 (sequential processing) remained — the encoder and decoder were still RNNs, still sequential. But the first three were solved, and translation quality improved dramatically, especially on long sentences.

---

## The 2017 generalization: self-attention (Vaswani et al.)

The Bahdanau attention was **cross-attention**: the decoder attended to the encoder. But the idea of "compute a context as a weighted average of vectors, with weights computed by a small network" is more general than the encoder-decoder setting.

Vaswani et al. (2017) asked: **what if a sequence attends to itself?** Instead of a decoder attending to an encoder, let every position in a sequence attend to every other position in the *same* sequence. This is **self-attention**, and it has a remarkable property: it captures intra-sequence dependencies directly, with path length 1, regardless of distance.

With self-attention, the RNN becomes redundant. The RNN's job was to propagate information across time so that position $t$ could "know about" position $i < t$. Self-attention does this directly: position $t$ attends to position $i$ in a single step. There is no need to carry information through a sequential chain.

So Vaswani et al. dropped the RNN entirely and built a model out of stacked self-attention layers plus feedforward layers. This model is the **Transformer**, and it addressed all four limitations:

1. **No bottleneck** — every position has direct access to every other position.
2. **Full lookback** — attention weights let each position select what it needs.
3. **No long-range decay** — the path length is 1, not $O(T)$.
4. **Parallel processing** — all positions can be computed simultaneously (no sequential recurrence).

---

## The design decisions that fall out of this story

Once you understand the trajectory "RNN → bottleneck → attention → self-attention → Transformer," several otherwise-mysterious design decisions become natural:

**Why positional encodings?** Self-attention has no notion of order — it treats the sequence as a set. But order matters for sequences (see [[01. Why Sequence Models - The Limits of Feedforward Networks]]). Positional encodings inject order information back in. The RNN did not need this because its sequential processing *was* the order; the Transformer needs it because its parallel processing *erases* order.

**Why multi-head attention?** A single attention head can learn one pattern of relationships ("which input word should I translate now?"). But language has many simultaneous relationship types — syntactic, semantic, coreference, discourse. Multi-head attention lets the model learn multiple relationship patterns in parallel, like multiple feature maps in a CNN.

**Why the $\frac{1}{\sqrt{d_k}}$ scaling?** Without scaling, the dot products grow with dimension $d_k$, pushing the softmax into saturated regions where gradients vanish. The scaling keeps the softmax in a well-behaved range. This is a numerical-stability detail, not a deep idea — but it is necessary.

**Why LayerNorm instead of BatchNorm?** BatchNorm depends on batch statistics, which are ill-defined for variable-length sequences and small batches. LayerNorm normalizes across the feature dimension per-token, so it is batch-independent and sequence-length-independent. This is the same reason LayerNorm is used in RNNs (see [[05. Batch Normalization]]'s alternatives section).

Each of these is a response to a specific problem that arose when you replace recurrence with attention. None of them is arbitrary; each falls out of the design.

---

## What the Transformer does *not* solve

It is worth being honest about what the Transformer does not fix:

1. **Quadratic cost in sequence length.** Self-attention compares every pair of positions, so it is $O(T^2)$ in memory and compute. RNNs are $O(T)$. For sequences of 1000 tokens this is fine; for sequences of 100,000 tokens (long documents, high-resolution images) it becomes prohibitive. This has driven a large research literature on efficient attention (sparse attention, linear attention, flash attention, state-space models like Mamba).

2. **Exposure bias.** Transformers still use teacher forcing for training and still suffer from exposure bias at inference (see [[02. Teacher Forcing and Exposure Bias]]).

3. **The ordering problem for generation.** A Transformer decoder generates left-to-right, just like an RNN decoder. It cannot generate tokens in parallel or in arbitrary order. This is a limitation of the *decoder* design, not of attention itself, and has driven work on non-autoregressive generation.

These are the frontiers of current research, and they are the natural next limitations to attack after the Transformer's wins have been consolidated.
